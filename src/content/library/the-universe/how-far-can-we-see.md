## Light is fast, but not instant

Light travels about 300,000 km every second. That is fast enough to circle Earth seven times in one second, but space is so large that even light takes time:

- From the Moon: about 1.3 seconds
- From the Sun: about 8 minutes
- From the Andromeda Galaxy: about 2.5 million years

A **light-year** is the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 trillion km. It measures distance, not time.

## Looking far is looking back

When you see the Sun, you see it as it was 8 minutes ago. When a telescope sees a galaxy 2.5 million light-years away, it sees light that left before humans existed.

So telescopes are time machines in one direction only: the farther they look, the further back in time they see. The James Webb Space Telescope has spotted galaxies as they were roughly 300 million years after the Big Bang.

## Why the edge is farther than 13.8 billion light-years

The Universe is about 13.8 billion years old, so you might expect we can see 13.8 billion light-years. We can see farther, because **space kept stretching while the light travelled**. The places that sent the oldest light are now about 46 billion light-years away. That makes the observable Universe about 93 billion light-years across.

## The observable part is not the whole

This edge is a limit on what we can see, not a wall. The whole Universe is probably much bigger, and it may have no edge at all. We simply cannot receive light from beyond our horizon yet.

## Remember this

Our view has a horizon set by the speed of light and the age of the Universe. Every telescope picture is a message from the past.
