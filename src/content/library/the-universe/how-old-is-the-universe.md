## Run the film backwards

Galaxies are moving apart because space is expanding (Leaf 11 explains how we know). If you run that film backwards, everything gets closer, hotter and denser. Measure how fast the expansion is happening, account for how it has changed over time, and you can estimate when everything was packed together.

## Read the oldest light

The Universe is filled with faint light from when it was 380,000 years old, called the **cosmic microwave background**. Tiny patterns of hotter and cooler spots in that light depend on the Universe's ingredients and age.

The Planck satellite mapped this light across the whole sky. Its data gives an age of **13.8 billion years**, with an uncertainty of only about 20 million years either way.

## Check against the oldest stars

A good scientist does not trust one method. Stars age in a predictable way, so astronomers can date old star clusters. The oldest stars we know of are around 12 to 13 billion years old, a little younger than the Universe.

That is exactly what we should find. If any star had turned out older than the Universe, the whole picture would be wrong.

## Is it settled?

The age itself is well supported. But there is a live puzzle about the expansion rate today, called the **Hubble tension**: two good methods give slightly different values. If it is resolved in a surprising way, the age could shift a little. Science updates when the evidence does.

## Remember this

13.8 billion years is not a guess. It is where three independent clues meet.
