## A glowing fog

After its first minutes the Universe had nuclei, but it was still too hot for atoms. Electrons flew around freely, and free electrons bounce light around.

So light could not travel in a straight line. It was scattered again and again, like car headlights in thick fog. The Universe glowed, but you could not have seen through it.

## The fog clears

At about **380,000 years**, the temperature fell to about 3,000 kelvin (roughly 2,700 °C). Now electrons could settle around nuclei and stay there, forming the first neutral **atoms** of hydrogen and helium.

Atoms let light pass. Almost at once, the Universe became transparent, and light streamed off in every direction.

## That light is still here

The light released then has been travelling ever since. As space stretched, its waves were stretched too, from glowing orange-hot light into faint **microwaves** at just 2.7 degrees above absolute zero.

This is the **cosmic microwave background**, the oldest light we can see. In 1965 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson found it by accident as a hiss in their radio antenna that would not go away. They won a Nobel Prize for it.

A small part of the static on old analogue televisions came from this light. People were watching the Big Bang's afterglow without knowing it.

## A baby picture of the Universe

The microwave background is almost perfectly even, but not quite. Tiny differences, about one part in 100,000, mark slightly denser regions. Those were the seeds that gravity later grew into galaxies.

## Remember this

The Universe went from fog to clear at 380,000 years, and the light from that moment is the baby picture we use to read its age and recipe.
