## A millionth of a second: building blocks

At first the Universe was too hot for anything to hold together. Matter existed as a soup of tiny particles called **quarks**. By about a millionth of a second, it had cooled enough for quarks to group in threes, making **protons** and **neutrons**.

A single proton is the nucleus of hydrogen, the simplest element. So the raw material for most of the Universe was ready in less than the blink of an eye.

## Minutes 3 to 20: the first nuclei

For a few minutes the whole Universe behaved like the core of a star, at around a billion degrees. Protons and neutrons collided and stuck together, making:

- **Helium**, the next simplest element
- Small amounts of **deuterium** (heavy hydrogen)
- A tiny pinch of **lithium**

Then the Universe cooled too much for fusion to continue, and the recipe was fixed. Scientists call this **Big Bang nucleosynthesis**.

## The prediction that came true

The theory predicts the Universe should be about **75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass**. When astronomers measure the oldest gas clouds and stars, that is close to what they find. That match is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the Big Bang.

One honest gap: the theory predicts about three times more lithium than we see in old stars. This **lithium problem** is still unsolved.

## Where the name comes from

"The First Three Minutes" is the title of a famous 1977 book by physicist Steven Weinberg. The phrase stuck because so much was decided so fast.

## Remember this

Nearly all the hydrogen in the Universe, including the hydrogen in the water you drink, was made in those first minutes.
