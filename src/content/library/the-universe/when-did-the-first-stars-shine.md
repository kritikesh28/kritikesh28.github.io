## The cosmic dark ages

After the fog cleared, the Universe was full of hydrogen and helium gas, slowly cooling. Nothing was shining yet. Astronomers call this stretch the **cosmic dark ages**, and it lasted for a hundred million years or more.

Notice the order: **atoms came long before stars**. For millions of years the Universe was simply dark gas.

## Gravity does the work

Some regions were slightly denser than others; we saw those seeds in Leaf 7. Gravity pulled more gas into these regions. As the gas squeezed together it heated up. When the centre of a clump reached about 10 million degrees, hydrogen began fusing into helium, and a star switched on.

## The first stars were different

The first stars were made only of hydrogen and helium, because nothing heavier existed yet. Theory suggests they were huge, often tens to hundreds of times the mass of our Sun, very hot and short-lived.

No one has yet seen one of these first stars directly. It is one of the big targets of modern astronomy.

## Light returns

As stars and young galaxies formed, their ultraviolet light gradually broke up the surrounding hydrogen gas again. By about one billion years after the Big Bang, this process, called **reionisation**, was largely complete.

## What Webb is showing us

The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, has found bright galaxies from only about 300 million years after the Big Bang. Some are brighter and more developed than expected, and scientists are now working out what that means for how fast early galaxies grew.

## Remember this

First came dark gas, then gravity, then light. Every star you see tonight belongs to a family line that began at cosmic dawn.
