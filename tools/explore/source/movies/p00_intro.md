## MOV-00.01 What did people do for stories before the movies?

**Short answer:** They gathered around storytellers, theatres, puppet shows and "magic lanterns". Cinema did not invent the human hunger for stories; it gave that hunger a new machine.

**Keywords:** entertainment before cinema, shadow puppets history, magic lantern, kathputli, history of storytelling

### Stories around the fire

For most of human history, the movie screen was a face lit by fire. Storytellers carried epics in their memory. In India, kathakars, bhopas with their painted phad scrolls in Rajasthan, and singers of the Alha and Pabuji epics travelled from village to village. Their audiences knew the story already and came anyway, for the telling.

### Theatre: the first "big screen"

Theatre gave stories a stage, costumes and an audience in rows. Bharata's **Natyashastra** (compiled roughly 200 BCE to 200 CE) is one of the oldest books on drama in the world, and Greek tragedy in Athens is of similar antiquity. Both understood something movies would later use: a crowd feels more together than alone.

### Light and shadow

Long before electricity, people were already telling stories with light. Shadow puppets danced in India (tholu bommalata in Andhra Pradesh, togalu gombeyaata in Karnataka), in Indonesia (wayang kulit), in China and in Turkey (Karagöz). Rajasthan's kathputli used strings instead of shadows.

From the 1600s, the **magic lantern** projected painted glass slides onto a wall; it is usually credited to the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens around 1659. Showmen used moving slides, smoke and sound effects to make audiences scream at ghosts.

### So what was missing?

Real motion. Every one of these arts could show a picture, or a performer moving live. None could capture a real moment and play it back. That was the dream of the 1800s.

**Remember this:** Movies are the newest member of a very old family: the storyteller, the stage and the shadow on the wall.

**Next:** So who first caught a real moment on film, and did audiences really run from a train?

## MOV-00.02 Who made the first movie, and did audiences really run from a train?

**Short answer:** The oldest surviving motion picture is Louis Le Prince's *Roundhay Garden Scene* (1888). Cinema as a public show began with the Lumière brothers' paid screening in Paris on 28 December 1895. The famous story of people running from a train is most likely a legend.

**Keywords:** first movie ever made, Lumière brothers, Roundhay Garden Scene, Edison kinetoscope, train panic myth

### Many inventors, one race

In the 1880s and 1890s, inventors in France, Britain, the USA and Germany all raced to capture motion. **Eadweard Muybridge** photographed a galloping horse with a row of cameras in 1878. **Louis Le Prince** filmed a few seconds of people walking in a garden in Leeds in 1888. **Thomas Edison's** team, led by William Kennedy Laurie Dickson, built the **Kinetoscope**, a peep-show box for one viewer at a time (public from 1894).

### The night cinema was born

Auguste and Louis Lumière built the **Cinématographe**, a camera that was also a projector. On **28 December 1895**, in the Salon Indien of the Grand Café in Paris, they showed about ten short films to a paying audience. One showed workers leaving their factory. Life itself had become a show.

### The train that did not attack

*The Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat* is famous for making audiences flee. Film historians have found no reliable report of such a panic, and the film was not part of the first programme. The legend survived because it says something true: people had never seen anything like it.

### From records to dreams

A magician named **Georges Méliès** saw the show and understood it could do more than record. His *A Trip to the Moon* (1902) used tricks, sets and stop-motion to make the impossible visible. Cinema now had two paths: the Lumières' real world and Méliès' dream world. Every film since walks one of them, or both.

**Remember this:** Cinema has no single inventor. It was born from many hands, and became an art the moment someone used it to tell a story.

**Next:** But a film strip is just a row of still photos. Why does it look like movement?

## MOV-00.03 Why does a row of still pictures look like it is moving?

**Short answer:** Your brain fills the gaps. When still images that differ slightly flash by fast enough, the visual system reads them as smooth motion. The old explanation, "persistence of vision", is now considered incomplete.

**Keywords:** how do movies work, frames per second, persistence of vision myth, phi phenomenon, 24 fps

### A trick played by your brain

A film is a series of still frames. Show them fast and your brain stops seeing separate pictures and starts seeing a moving world. Psychologists call related effects **apparent motion** (the phi phenomenon and beta movement, studied by Max Wertheimer in 1912). The brain has motion detectors that respond to change between images, and they happily accept a well-timed series of stills.

### Why "persistence of vision" is not the full story

Many textbooks still say an image "stays on the retina" and blends with the next. Lingering images do help hide the dark gap between frames, but they would produce a blur, not motion. The motion itself is built by the brain.

### How fast is fast enough?

Silent films ran at around 16 to 20 frames per second, often varied by hand. With sound, speed had to be fixed so voices would not change pitch, and **24 frames per second** became the standard in the late 1920s. Projectors flash each frame two or three times, so the light flickers 48 or 72 times a second, too fast for most eyes to notice. Peter Jackson's *The Hobbit* (2012) tried 48 frames per second; many viewers felt it looked "too real", which shows how much our taste is trained by habit.

### The pictures before the movies

Toys like the **zoetrope** and the **phenakistiscope** (1830s) proved the effect decades before film. Spin a drum of drawings, look through the slits, and a horse runs.

**Remember this:** Motion in the movies lives in your head. The screen gives the brain clues; the brain makes the magic.

**Next:** Within months of Paris, those flickering pictures reached Bombay. How did India get its first film?

## MOV-00.04 How did India get its first film?

**Short answer:** The Lumière films were shown in Bombay on 7 July 1896. Dadasaheb Phalke's *Raja Harishchandra*, released on 3 May 1913, is generally regarded as India's first full-length feature film.

**Keywords:** first Indian film, Raja Harishchandra, Dadasaheb Phalke, Alam Ara first talkie, history of Indian cinema

### Cinema arrives by ship

Barely six months after Paris, the Lumière agents showed their films at **Watson's Hotel in Bombay on 7 July 1896**. Indians soon picked up cameras: Harishchandra Sakharam Bhatavdekar ("Save Dada") filmed a wrestling match in Bombay in 1899, one of the first films shot by an Indian.

### A printer who saw Christ on screen

**Dhundiraj Govind Phalke** was a printer and photographer. In 1910 he watched *The Life of Christ* and asked: why not Indian gods and heroes on screen? He went to London to learn, bought equipment, and filmed the story of the truthful king Harishchandra. No woman from a "respectable" family would act then, so a young man, **Anna Salunke**, played the queen Taramati. *Raja Harishchandra* opened at the Coronation Cinema, Bombay, on **3 May 1913**. (Some historians point to *Shree Pundalik*, 1912, as an earlier contender; it is usually treated as a filmed play.)

### Mythology first, then everything

Early Indian films drew on the Ramayana, the Mahabharata and the Puranas, because audiences already loved those stories from theatre and katha. The South followed with *Keechaka Vadham* (1918), Bengal with Madan Theatres' productions, and soon studios rose in Bombay, Calcutta, Kolhapur, Pune and Madras.

### The day India spoke

On **14 March 1931**, Ardeshir Irani's *Alam Ara* became India's first talkie. Within a year, films spoke Tamil, Telugu, Bengali and Marathi. Sound turned one Indian cinema into many: the birth of the language industries you will meet in Part 1.

**Remember this:** Indian cinema began with a printer's dream and a myth everyone knew. India now certifies more feature films each year than any other country.

**Next:** Before 1927, films everywhere had no recorded sound. So why do historians say silent films were never silent?

## MOV-00.05 Why were silent films never silent?

**Short answer:** Because almost every screening had live sound: a pianist, an organ, a full orchestra or, in Japan, a narrator called a benshi. The films had no recorded dialogue, but the cinema hall was full of music.

**Keywords:** silent film music, what is a benshi, Charlie Chaplin silent films, intertitles, silent era

### The music in the room

In big city cinemas of the 1910s and 1920s, orchestras played scores written or arranged for the film. Smaller halls had a single pianist who improvised to the mood: chase music, love music, danger music. Cinema organs were built with drums, bells and bird whistles. In India too, silent shows were accompanied by live musicians, and sometimes by a narrator explaining the story.

### The storytellers of Japan

Japan's **benshi** stood beside the screen and voiced characters, explained scenes and added commentary. Popular benshi were stars, and audiences chose cinemas by who was narrating. Their popularity is one reason Japan kept making silent films well into the 1930s.

### Telling a story with the face

Without speech, actors and directors invented a universal language: faces, bodies and editing. **Charlie Chaplin**, **Buster Keaton** and **Harold Lloyd** made comedy that needs no translation even today. Short written cards called **intertitles** gave dialogue or explanations, and could be swapped for any language, which made silent films easy to sell around the world.

### An art that peaked and vanished

By the late 1920s, films such as F. W. Murnau's *Sunrise* (1927) and Carl Theodor Dreyer's *The Passion of Joan of Arc* (1928) showed how far the silent art had grown. Then, within about three years, it was nearly gone. Sadly, most silent films are lost: the Library of Congress estimates that around 75 percent of American silent features no longer survive, and only a few fragments of India's roughly 1,300 silent films remain.

**Remember this:** Silent cinema was a complete art of music, gesture and image, and it taught the world a shared language of the screen.

**Next:** Then the movies learned to talk. What did they gain, and what did they lose?

## MOV-00.06 What changed when movies learned to talk?

**Short answer:** Sound arrived with *The Jazz Singer* (1927) and changed everything within a few years: acting, cameras, music, careers and, above all, language. In India, sound gave birth to film songs and to separate regional industries.

**Keywords:** first sound film, The Jazz Singer, talkies history, Alam Ara songs, why Indian films have songs

### "You ain't heard nothin' yet"

Warner Bros. released *The Jazz Singer* in October 1927 using the **Vitaphone** system, which played sound from a disc in time with the film. Only parts of it had synchronised speech and song, but audiences were thrilled. By 1930, Hollywood had mostly stopped making silent films.

### What was lost for a while

Early microphones were weak, so cameras were locked inside soundproof booths and actors had to stand near hidden microphones. Films became stiff for a few years. Some stars with voices or accents audiences disliked lost their careers. (The musical *Singin' in the Rain*, 1952, jokes about exactly this.) Soon cameras learned to move again, and sound became a new tool: the creak of a door, a silence before a gunshot.

### Why India sings

*Alam Ara* (1931) had seven songs. Indian audiences came from a theatre tradition, from Parsi theatre to Sangeet Natak to folk forms, in which music and drama were one. So songs became the heart of Indian cinema, and playback singing (singers recording songs that actors lip-sync) became standard from the mid-1930s. Even today, a film's songs often reach people before the film does.

### One cinema becomes many

Silent films could swap their intertitles for any language. Talkies could not. So sound split cinema along language lines everywhere. In India, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Marathi and later Kannada, Malayalam and other industries grew their own studios, stars and styles.

**Remember this:** Sound gave films a voice, and gave every language its own cinema.

**Next:** Talking pictures made us laugh and weep. But why do we cry for people who never existed?

## MOV-00.07 Why do we cry for people who do not exist?

**Short answer:** Because the brain responds to stories much as it responds to real people, and because a film gives us emotion in a safe frame. Indian aesthetics calls this tasting of emotion "rasa"; Aristotle called its cleansing effect "catharsis".

**Keywords:** why do we cry at movies, rasa theory explained, catharsis, psychology of film, paradox of fiction

### The puzzle

You know the hero is an actor. You know the music was added later. You cry anyway. Philosophers call this the **paradox of fiction** (the philosopher Colin Radford asked it sharply in 1975): how can we feel real emotions for what we know is unreal?

### What the brain does

When we watch a character, we read faces, follow goals and predict danger, using the same social skills we use with real people. Brain-imaging studies show that watching emotions activates regions also involved in feeling them. Research by Paul Zak's team has linked emotionally gripping stories to a rise in oxytocin, a hormone tied to empathy, though such results are still debated. The short version: the brain does not fully switch off its caring system just because the person is fictional.

### Rasa: India's answer, 2,000 years old

The **Natyashastra** says a performance creates **rasa**, a "taste" of emotion that the audience savours. We do not suffer the hero's grief; we taste *karuna* (compassion) in a refined, shared form. Classical writers listed eight rasas, and later thinkers such as **Abhinavagupta** added *shanta* (peace), making the famous **navarasa**. A good Indian "masala" film often tries to serve many rasas in one sitting, like a thali.

### Catharsis and safety

**Aristotle** wrote that tragedy arouses pity and fear and brings their **catharsis**, a release or cleansing. Psychologists today add that films give us a safe rehearsal: we feel loss, fear or love without the real cost, and practise feeling for others.

### The editing trick

In the 1920s, Soviet filmmaker Lev Kuleshov reportedly showed the same neutral face after shots of soup, a coffin and a child, and viewers "saw" hunger, grief and joy. This **Kuleshov effect** shows that much of the emotion is built by us.

**Remember this:** Movies borrow the brain's machinery for caring about people, and give it a safe space to feel.

**Next:** Emotion on that scale became a business. How did movies become the world's biggest dream factory?

## MOV-00.08 How did movies become the world's biggest dream factory?

**Short answer:** Through studios and stars. From the 1910s, studios in Hollywood, Bombay, Madras, Tokyo and elsewhere made films like a factory makes cars, on a schedule, with salaried stars. That system made cinema the dominant popular art of the 20th century.

**Keywords:** Hollywood studio system, star system, Bombay Talkies, Prabhat studio, why is it called Hollywood

### Why Hollywood?

American filmmakers moved west to Los Angeles in the 1910s for sunshine, varied landscapes and distance from Edison's patent company in the East. By the 1920s, Hollywood was the capital of world cinema.

### The studio system

From about 1920 to 1950, five big studios (MGM, Paramount, Warner Bros., 20th Century-Fox and RKO) owned everything: writers, stars on long contracts, sound stages and even the cinemas. In 1948, the US Supreme Court's **Paramount decision** forced them to sell their cinemas, and the old system began to break.

### India's studios

India built its own dream factories: **Prabhat** (Kolhapur, later Pune), **New Theatres** (Calcutta), **Bombay Talkies**, **Gemini** and **AVM** (Madras), **Vauhini** and **Vijaya** in the Telugu world. Actors, musicians and writers were on monthly salaries. After independence, independent producers and the star system took over.

### The star system

Studios learned that people come to see *someone*. Stars became brands, from Charlie Chaplin and Greta Garbo to Raj Kapoor, Dilip Kumar, M. G. Ramachandran, N. T. Rama Rao, Rajkumar, Uttam Kumar, Amitabh Bachchan and Rajinikanth. In South India, film stars even became chief ministers: M. G. Ramachandran and J. Jayalalithaa in Tamil Nadu, N. T. Rama Rao in Andhra Pradesh.

### How big is it now?

India certifies more feature films in a year than any other country, usually well over 1,500 (CBFC annual reports), and Nigeria's Nollywood is second or third by volume (UNESCO data). The USA still earns the most money worldwide.

**Remember this:** Studios turned dreams into a product and stars into family members of millions.

**Next:** Yet Hollywood, Bollywood, Seoul and Tehran make films that feel totally different. Why?

## MOV-00.09 Why does every country make its films differently?

**Short answer:** Because films grow out of a country's language, history, money, censorship and taste in storytelling. Indian films serve many emotions with songs; Hollywood loves tight plots; Iranian and Italian masters found poetry in everyday life.

**Keywords:** world cinema explained, difference between Bollywood and Hollywood, Italian neorealism, Iranian cinema, Nollywood

### Taste in stories

A typical Indian popular film runs longer, mixes action, comedy, romance and tears, and pauses for songs, because it grew from theatre and the **navarasa** idea (MOV-00.07). Hollywood screenwriting manuals teach a tight three-act structure around one goal. Neither is "right"; they are different meals.

### History leaves fingerprints

After the Second World War, Italian directors with no money and ruined studios filmed on real streets with non-actors: **Italian neorealism** (*Bicycle Thieves*, 1948). It inspired Satyajit Ray to make *Pather Panchali* (1955). In the 1950s and 1960s, French critics who became directors broke the rules of editing: the **French New Wave**.

### Limits that sparked creativity

Iranian filmmakers worked under strict censorship and often turned to children, landscapes and small moral stories; Abbas Kiarostami's *Taste of Cherry* won the Palme d'Or in 1997. Danish directors wrote themselves strict rules in the **Dogme 95** manifesto to escape big-budget habits.

### Money shapes form

Nigeria's **Nollywood** grew from home-video tapes after *Living in Bondage* (1992): fast, cheap and close to its audience. South Korea built the "Korean wave" with screen quotas, investment and fearless genre films, leading to *Parasite* winning the Best Picture Oscar in 2020, the first non-English-language film to do so.

### Language and market

A Malayalam film speaks to about 35 million native speakers; a Hindi or English film to hundreds of millions. That affects budgets, themes and risk-taking, and helps explain why smaller industries often surprise us with bold, realistic stories.

**Remember this:** Every cinema is a mirror of its people, shaped by what they love, what they have lived through and what they are allowed to say.

**Next:** Cinema survived wars and censors. Then came television, video and streaming. Did they try to kill it?

## MOV-00.10 Did television, video and streaming try to kill the cinema?

**Short answer:** Each new screen was expected to kill cinema, and each time cinema changed instead: widescreen and colour against television, multiplexes against video, event films against streaming. Theatres shrank, but the habit of watching together survived.

**Keywords:** did TV kill cinema, CinemaScope history, VHS and movies, multiplex in India, streaming vs theatres

### Round one: television (1950s)

As American families bought televisions, cinema attendance fell sharply. Hollywood fought back with what a small black-and-white box could not give: **colour**, **widescreen** (CinemaScope, launched with *The Robe*, 1953), 3D glasses and huge epics such as *Ben-Hur* (1959). In India, television spread later, in the 1980s, with Doordarshan's *Ramayan* (1987) emptying streets on Sunday mornings.

### Round two: video (1970s to 1990s)

Videocassettes let people watch films at home and also made piracy easy. In 1984, the US Supreme Court ruled in the "Betamax case" that home recording for personal use was legal. Studios then found that selling tapes was very profitable. In India, the video boom of the 1980s hurt cinemas and audiences moved back with better halls; the first multiplex, PVR Anupam in Delhi (1997), began a new era.

### Round three: streaming (2007 onwards)

Netflix began streaming in 2007; then came Amazon Prime Video, Disney+, Hotstar and many more. During the COVID-19 pandemic (2020 to 2021), cinemas closed and many films went straight to streaming. Some people predicted the end of theatres. Instead, big "event" films returned audiences to halls, and films such as *RRR* and *Oppenheimer* became shared experiences again.

### What survives

Each round killed something (the newsreel, the single-screen village theatre, the DVD shop) and kept the core: a story, a dark room and people feeling it together.

**Remember this:** New screens do not kill old ones; they force them to discover what only they can do.

**Next:** Theatres, TV, streaming: someone is paying for all of this. How much money does a film really make?

## MOV-00.11 How much money does a movie really make?

**Short answer:** Less than the headline number. "Box office" usually means gross ticket sales, of which cinemas keep a large share and taxes take another. Studios also earn from streaming, TV, music and other rights. As checked on 5 October 2026, the highest worldwide gross is *Avengers: Endgame* (about US$2.93 billion after a 2026 re-release), just ahead of *Avatar*.

**Keywords:** highest grossing movie of all time, box office explained, gross vs net collection, Indian box office, film budget

### Gross, net and share

**Gross** is the total value of tickets sold. In India, trade reports also quote **net** (gross minus GST and local taxes). From the net, the cinema keeps its share, and the rest (the **distributor share**) goes back toward the producer. A film with a "₹300 crore" headline might return far less to the people who made it.

### Budget is only half the cost

A film's production budget does not include marketing, which for a big Hollywood release can equal a large part of the budget. A common rule of thumb is that a theatrical film must earn roughly two to three times its budget at the box office to break even, though every deal is different.

### The other money

Indian producers often sell **satellite, digital (streaming) and music rights** before release, sometimes covering a large share of the cost. Hollywood earns from streaming licences, TV, merchandise and theme parks.

### The record holders (as checked on 5 October 2026)

Worldwide, not adjusted for inflation: *Avengers: Endgame* (2019) about US$2.925 billion, *Avatar* (2009) about US$2.923 billion, *Avatar: The Way of Water* (2022), *Titanic* (1997) and China's *Ne Zha 2* (2025), the highest-grossing animated film and non-English-language film (figures: Euronews from Box Office Mojo data). Adjusted for inflation, *Gone with the Wind* (1939) is usually ranked first (Guinness World Records). In India, *Dangal* (2016) is the highest-grossing Indian film worldwide, at around ₹2,000 crore, much of it earned in China.

### Why numbers mislead

Figures are rarely audited publicly, are reported by interested parties and change with exchange rates and re-releases. That is why this site always gives the source and date.

**Remember this:** Box office is a scoreboard, not a bank statement. Read it with the source and date.

**Next:** With streaming, AI and virtual sets, the movies are changing again. What is their future?

## MOV-00.12 What is the future of the movies?

**Short answer:** Movies are moving toward virtual production, AI-assisted tools, global audiences and streaming-first releases, while the big-screen event survives. The open questions are about creativity, jobs and trust, not about whether people will still want stories.

**Keywords:** future of cinema, AI in filmmaking, virtual production LED volume, pan-Indian films, will cinemas survive

### New tools on set

**Virtual production** uses giant LED screens showing computer-generated backgrounds that move with the camera, popularised by the series *The Mandalorian* (2019). Directors can shoot a desert or a planet inside a studio, with real light falling on the actors.

### AI: helper or threat?

AI can now help with editing, dubbing, de-ageing, visual effects and even generating short clips. In 2023, Hollywood's writers (WGA) and actors (SAG-AFTRA) went on long strikes, in part to win protections against unconsented use of AI and digital replicas. The debate continues worldwide, and India's industry is having it too.

### One world, many languages

Subtitles and dubbing on streaming platforms have made language less of a wall. *Parasite*, *RRR*, *Squid Game* (a series) and *KPop Demon Hunters* (Best Animated Feature at the 2026 Oscars) found global audiences. In India, "pan-Indian" films like *Baahubali*, *K.G.F* and *Pushpa* are released in five or more languages at once. *RRR*'s "Naatu Naatu" won the Oscar for Best Original Song in 2023.

### New forms

Interactive films (*Black Mirror: Bandersnatch*, 2018), virtual reality, vertical "micro-dramas" on phones and games that feel like films are all testing the edges of what a movie is.

### The question that remains

Every new technology has changed *how* we watch. None has changed *why*. We still want to sit with others in the dark and feel a story together. That is the thread you will follow through the rest of this site.

**Remember this:** The future of film will be decided less by machines than by whether stories keep earning our trust.
