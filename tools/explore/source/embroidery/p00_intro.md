## EMB-00 The Gateway: How did women write stories in thread long before they were allowed to write books?

For most of history, most women were not taught to read or write. But they were taught to stitch. On quilts made from old saris in Bengal, on wedding shawls in Punjab, on dresses in Palestine and story cloths of the Hmong, women recorded births, weddings, gods, villages, jokes and griefs in thread. Embroidery is decoration, and it is also memory.

## EMB-00.01 What is embroidery?

**Short answer:** Embroidery is decorating a fabric (or leather, or paper) that already exists, using a needle and thread, and sometimes beads, mirrors, metal or cut cloth. It differs from weaving and knitting, which make the fabric itself, and from printing, which uses dye.

**Keywords:** what is embroidery, types of embroidery, embroidery vs weaving, surface embroidery, counted thread

### The main families

- **Surface (free) embroidery**: stitches follow a drawn design (chikankari, crewel, kantha).
- **Counted-thread work**: stitches are counted over the fabric's threads (cross stitch, kasuti, blackwork).
- **Whitework**: white thread on white cloth (chikankari, Hardanger, broderie anglaise).
- **Goldwork and metal thread**: zardozi, gota.
- **Appliqué**: cloth shapes sewn onto cloth (Pipli, khatwa).
- **Beads, sequins and mirrors**: shisha work, aari with beads.
- **Quilting stitches**: running stitches through layers (kantha, sujani, sashiko).
- **Machine and digital embroidery**.

### Why people embroider

To beautify, to show status or faith, to mark identity (your village, your community), to protect (mirrors against the evil eye), to mend and strengthen, to earn, and to remember.

**Remember this:** Embroidery adds meaning to cloth, one stitch at a time.

## EMB-00.02 Why is the Bayeux Tapestry not a tapestry?

**Short answer:** A tapestry is woven: the picture is made on a loom as the cloth is made. The Bayeux Tapestry is wool thread stitched onto plain linen, so it is an embroidery. Nearly 70 metres long, it tells the story of the Norman conquest of England in 1066, and was probably made in England in the 1070s.

**Keywords:** Bayeux Tapestry embroidery, Bayeux stitch, Norman conquest 1066, medieval embroidery, laid and couched work

### What it shows

About 58 scenes: Harold's oath, Halley's Comet (1066), the Channel crossing, the Battle of Hastings and Harold's death. A Latin caption runs above; animals and everyday scenes fill the borders.

### How it was made

Wool yarn in about ten natural-dye colours, worked in **stem stitch** for outlines and **laid-and-couched work** (the "Bayeux stitch") for filling. It was probably commissioned by Bishop Odo, William's half-brother, and stitched by skilled English needleworkers, very likely women, whose names are lost.

### News

The embroidery is kept at Bayeux in Normandy. A loan to the British Museum was announced in 2025 for 2026 to 2027 while its museum is rebuilt; check the museums' websites for current dates.

**Remember this:** The Bayeux "Tapestry" is a 70-metre embroidered history book.

## EMB-00.03 How is chikankari made?

**Short answer:** Chikankari, the white-on-white embroidery of Lucknow, passes through many hands: the cloth is cut, the design is **block-printed** in washable blue ink, embroiderers (mostly women working at home) work the stitches, and the piece is **washed** to remove the ink, then bleached, starched and ironed.

**Keywords:** chikankari process, Lucknow chikan, chikankari stitches, shadow work, chikan GI tag

### The steps

1. **Cutting and stitching** the garment pieces.
2. **Chhapai**: printing the pattern with carved wooden blocks dipped in *neel* (indigo-based or washable ink).
3. **Embroidery**: over 30 named stitches, including *bakhiya* (shadow work, done from the back), *phanda* and *murri* (knots shaped like grains), *jaali* (a net made by pulling threads apart, no thread cut), *tepchi* (running stitch), *keel kangan* and *hool*.
4. **Dhulai**: washing in the Gomti-side washing ghats, bleaching, starching.
5. **Finishing**: ironing and checking.

### Where it came from

Tradition credits Empress Nur Jahan (17th century) with popularising it; the Nawabs of Awadh patronised it in the 18th and 19th centuries. Lucknow Chikan Craft received a GI tag in 2008.

### Who earns

Embroiderers are often paid by the piece through agents; groups such as SEWA Lucknow (1984) work for fairer wages.

**Remember this:** Chikankari is teamwork: printer, embroiderer and washer make one garment.

## EMB-00.04 Why does every phulkari tell a family story?

**Short answer:** A phulkari ("flower work") was a shawl embroidered by a Punjabi girl's grandmother, mother and aunts, and by the girl herself, for her wedding. Begun at her birth in many families, it held their hopes, songs and motifs from daily life. It was made for love, not for sale.

**Keywords:** phulkari history, bagh phulkari, Punjab embroidery, phulkari meaning, phulkari GI

### How it is made

Untwisted silk floss (*pat*) is worked in **darning stitch from the back** of coarse hand-spun cotton (*khaddar*), dyed red or rust. The embroiderer counts threads; no pattern is drawn.

### Kinds

- **Bagh** ("garden"): fully covered, so the cloth disappears.
- **Chope**: given by the maternal grandmother; worked on both sides.
- **Sainchi**: scenes of village life: trains, wrestlers, animals, jewellery.
- **Thirma**: on white cloth, worn by older women and widows.
- **Til patra** and others for gifts.

### Loss and revival

Partition in 1947 scattered families and their phulkaris. Many are now in museums (the Philadelphia Museum of Art, the National Museum in Delhi). Phulkari has a GI tag (2011) for Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan, and today is made for sale, often on finer fabrics.

**Remember this:** A phulkari is a family's blessing in silk.

## EMB-00.05 Can a machine embroider like a hand?

**Short answer:** A machine can embroider faster, cheaper and more evenly than a hand, and computerised machines can copy almost any design. What it cannot give is the slight irregularity, the choice made at each stitch and the story of the maker. Both have their place; the honest thing is to label which is which.

**Keywords:** hand vs machine embroidery, Schiffli machine, computerised embroidery, how to identify hand embroidery, aari work

### A short history of machines

- **1828**: Josué Heilmann's hand-embroidery machine (France).
- **1863**: Isaak Gröbli's **Schiffli** machine (Switzerland), using a shuttle like a sewing machine; made St Gallen a lace capital.
- **20th century**: multi-head machines.
- **1980s on**: computerised machines read digital design files.

### How to tell

- **Back of the work**: machine work is very regular, often with a white bobbin thread and stabiliser paper; hand work shows knots and varied stitch lengths.
- **Repeats**: identical motifs suggest a machine.
- **Price and time**: a hand-embroidered sari can take weeks or months.

### In between

India's *aari* (a hooked awl, like tambour) and *zardozi* workers use hand tools at great speed; "computer embroidery" units now work beside them in Surat, Lucknow and Delhi.

**Remember this:** Machines copy the look; hands carry the story. Ask, and label honestly.
