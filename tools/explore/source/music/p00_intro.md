## MUS-00 The Gateway: Why does a pattern of vibrating air make you cry, dance or pray?

**Web address:** /music/why-music-moves-us/ · **Keywords:** what is music, why music moves us, music for beginners, history of music

Music is only air pushed into waves. Yet a lullaby calms a baby who knows no words, a drumbeat gets a stadium on its feet, and a raga at dawn can make a grown person weep. Every human culture ever found has music.

This short course follows one question in eight steps: **what is music, and why does it reach so deep inside us?** It goes from bone flutes of the Ice Age to the Samaveda, from the time-of-day ragas to the recording studio, and from the earworm to AI music. Each page takes about two minutes.

## MUS-00.01 What was the first music?

**Short answer:** The first music was almost certainly the human voice, clapping and body percussion, which leave no trace. The oldest surviving instruments are bone and ivory flutes from Germany, about 40,000 years old.

**Keywords:** first music ever, oldest musical instrument, prehistoric flute, Divje Babe flute, origin of music

### Music before instruments

Singing, humming, clapping and stamping need no tools, so they were surely first. Hunter-gatherer peoples today, such as the BaAka of Central Africa, sing complex layered songs with voices alone.

### The oldest instruments we have

Flutes made from vulture bone and mammoth ivory, found in caves such as **Hohle Fels** and **Geissenklösterle** in Germany, date to about 40,000–43,000 years ago. A bone from **Divje Babe** in Slovenia (about 50,000–60,000 years old) has holes that some call a Neanderthal flute; many scientists think a cave bear chewed it.

### Early India

Rock shelters at **Bhimbetka** (Madhya Pradesh) contain paintings of dancing figures and possibly drums. Some boulders in India and elsewhere ring like bells when struck ("ringing rocks"), which may have been used as lithophones. The Indus civilisation left a famous bronze "dancing girl" and figurines that suggest music and dance.

### Why it matters

Music is not a late luxury. It appears as early as art itself, which suggests it did something vital for early humans: bonding groups, soothing babies, marking rituals.

**Remember this:** The first instrument was the human body; the oldest surviving one is a 40,000-year-old flute.

**Next:** If music is that old, could it be older than language itself?

## MUS-00.02 Is music older than language?

**Short answer:** Nobody knows for sure. Charles Darwin suggested that a "musical protolanguage" came before speech, and some researchers agree. Others think music grew out of language. Both use pitch, rhythm and emotion, and they share brain networks.

**Keywords:** music and language evolution, musical protolanguage, Darwin music, why humans make music

### Darwin's guess

In *The Descent of Man* (1871), Darwin proposed that our ancestors sang before they spoke, using song in courtship. The archaeologist **Steven Mithen** (*The Singing Neanderthals*, 2005) argued for a shared "Hmmmmm" stage, holistic, musical and emotional.

### The other view

The psychologist **Steven Pinker** once called music "auditory cheesecake": a pleasant by-product of abilities evolved for other purposes. Many researchers disagree, pointing to music's role in bonding (singing together releases endorphins and synchronises groups).

### What babies tell us

Parents all over the world speak to babies in a sing-song voice ("motherese"), and lullabies across cultures sound alike. A 2018 Harvard study (Mehr and colleagues) found that people could guess whether a song from an unfamiliar culture was a lullaby, a dance song or a healing song.

### India's view

Indian tradition treats sound itself as sacred: **nada brahma**, "sound is the absolute". The Samaveda set Rigvedic verses to melody.

**Remember this:** Music and language are cousins; which came first is still an open question.

**Next:** India built the most detailed system linking music to time. Why does a raga change with the time of day?

## MUS-00.03 Why does a raga change with the time of day?

**Short answer:** In Hindustani tradition, each raga is linked to a time (samay) and sometimes a season, because its mood is felt to match that hour: Bhairav at dawn, Yaman at evening, Malhar in the monsoon. The rule is a tradition of feeling, systematised by musicians and theorists such as V. N. Bhatkhande, not a law of physics.

**Keywords:** raga time theory, morning ragas, evening ragas, samay chakra, raga and seasons

### What a raga is

A raga is more than a scale. It is a set of notes with rules of movement, important notes (vadi and samvadi), typical phrases (pakad) and a mood (rasa). The same seven notes can make hundreds of ragas.

### The clock of ragas

Musicians sort ragas into eight three-hour **prahars** of the day and night. Ragas with komal (flat) re and dha, like **Bhairav** and **Todi**, are morning ragas; **Yaman** and **Puriya** belong to the evening; **Darbari Kanada** to late night. Bhatkhande noticed patterns in which notes are used at which hour.

### Seasons too

**Malhar** ragas are sung in the rains (Tansen's Miyan ki Malhar is the most famous), **Basant** and **Bahar** in spring. Legends say Tansen's Deepak raga lit lamps; these are stories, not history.

### Carnatic and other views

Carnatic music has no strict time theory, though some ragas such as Bhoopalam are linked to morning. Many modern musicians perform any raga at any time on stage or recordings, and listeners still feel the moods.

**Remember this:** A raga's time is about mood and tradition; it is a map for feelings, not a timetable.

**Next:** Why does a group of notes feel happy or sad at all?

## MUS-00.04 Why do some notes sound happy and others sad?

**Short answer:** Partly nature and partly culture. Faster tempo, higher pitch and major scales often sound happy to Western-trained ears; slow tempo, low pitch and minor scales sound sad. But studies of listeners with little exposure to Western music show the major/minor link is learned, while tempo and loudness cues are more universal.

**Keywords:** why minor keys sound sad, music and emotion, major vs minor, rasa in music, emotional music

### What the body hears

Sad speech is slow, low and soft; excited speech is fast, high and loud. Music copies these vocal cues, so even an unfamiliar tune can sound joyful or mournful.

### Learned meanings

A 2016 study (McDermott and colleagues, *Nature*) with the Tsimane' people of Bolivia, who have little exposure to Western music, found they did not prefer consonant over dissonant chords as Western listeners do. This suggests preferences for "sweet" chords are partly learned.

### India's answer: rasa and bhava

Indian theory links ragas to **rasas**: Darbari Kanada to gravity and karuna (compassion), Bhairavi to devotion, Desh to romance. A singer's **bhava** (feeling) and the lyric deepen the mood.

### Mixed feelings

Sad music is often enjoyed. Researchers suggest it lets us feel emotion safely, gives comfort and connects us to others, an idea close to the Indian view that rasa is joyful even when the emotion is sorrow.

**Remember this:** Some musical emotions are universal (speed, loudness), others are learned (major and minor).

**Next:** For most of history, music vanished the moment it ended. How did it get trapped in a box?

## MUS-00.05 How did music get trapped in a box?

**Short answer:** Through recording. Édouard-Léon Scott's phonautograph (1857) drew sound waves but could not play them back; **Thomas Edison's phonograph (1877)** recorded and replayed sound. Then came discs, radio, tape, vinyl, cassettes, CDs, MP3s and streaming.

**Keywords:** history of recorded music, first sound recording, phonograph, gramophone India, first Indian recording

### From air to wax

Edison recorded "Mary Had a Little Lamb" on tinfoil in 1877. **Emile Berliner's gramophone** (1887) used flat discs that could be mass-produced, which won the format war.

### India on record

The **Gramophone Company** made India's first commercial recordings in Calcutta in **1902**. **Gauhar Jaan** became India's first recording star, announcing "My name is Gauhar Jaan" at the end of her records. Later, **All India Radio** (named in 1936) brought music to millions, and film songs dominated sales.

### Each format changed music

The three-minute 78 rpm disc shaped the length of popular songs. The LP (1948) allowed whole albums and long ragas. The cassette (1960s, booming in India in the 1980s with T-Series) made music cheap and local. The CD (1982), the MP3 (1990s) and streaming (Spotify 2008; JioSaavn and Gaana in India) made almost all music instantly available.

### What we gained and lost

Recording preserved voices that would have vanished and let a village singer reach the world. It also made live, local music less central to daily life.

**Remember this:** Recording turned music from an event into an object, and then into a stream.

**Next:** Recording lets songs repeat endlessly. Why does a song get stuck in your head?

## MUS-00.06 Why does a song get stuck in your head?

**Short answer:** It is called an **earworm** (involuntary musical imagery). Songs with simple, repetitive melodies, a slightly unexpected interval and a fast tempo stick best. They pop up when the mind is idle or stressed, and usually fade by themselves.

**Keywords:** earworm, why songs get stuck in your head, catchy songs science, how to stop an earworm

### What happens

Your brain replays a short loop, often just the chorus. Research led by **Kelly Jakubowski** (2016) found that earworm songs tend to have common melodic shapes with a few surprising leaps, like many hits.

### Why the brain does it

Repetition is how memory works; music is built on repetition. Unfinished loops may nag the mind (the "Zeigarnik effect" is often suggested).

### How to get rid of one

Listening to the whole song, chewing gum, or doing a moderately hard puzzle can help. Or simply wait.

### Catchy by design

Advertising jingles, film songs and pop choruses are designed to be catchy. In India, a "hook line" in a film song can become a national catchphrase within days.

**Remember this:** Earworms are a side effect of a brain that loves patterns and repetition.

**Next:** Catchy songs now travel the whole planet at once. How does the world share one pop chart?

## MUS-00.07 How does the world share one pop chart now?

**Short answer:** Streaming platforms and social media let a song spread across countries in days. Charts such as Billboard's Global 200 (2020) and Spotify's global charts measure this. Songs in Korean, Spanish, Punjabi and other languages now top global lists, not just English.

**Keywords:** global music charts, K-pop global success, Despacito, Punjabi music global, streaming and music

### From national to global

Charts began with sheet music and record sales (*Billboard*, USA, from 1940; UK charts from 1952). Each country had its own. Streaming made listening data global and instant.

### The new world hits

Luis Fonsi and Daddy Yankee's "Despacito" (2017) became one of the most viewed videos on YouTube. **BTS** and **BLACKPINK** made K-pop a global force. **Bad Bunny** became one of the world's most streamed artists singing in Spanish, and his album won the Grammy for Album of the Year in 2026. Punjabi artists such as **Diljit Dosanjh** and **AP Dhillon** filled arenas abroad.

### India's place

India is one of the world's largest music-streaming markets by users. Film songs still lead, but independent artists and regional music are growing.

### The cost

Streaming pays artists fractions of a cent per play, and algorithms can favour the already famous. These are live debates (MUS-21).

**Remember this:** The global chart is new; the human love of a shared song is ancient.

**Next:** If algorithms pick songs, can AI also make music that moves us?

## MUS-00.08 Can AI make music that moves us?

**Short answer:** AI can now generate songs that many listeners cannot tell from human ones. Whether they move us depends partly on the listener, and partly on knowing a human meant something. AI music also raises big questions about training on artists' work without permission and fair pay.

**Keywords:** AI music, can AI write songs, AI generated music copyright, future of music

### Machines and music: an old story

Mozart's "musical dice game" generated minuets by chance. In 1957 the **Illiac Suite** was composed by a computer at the University of Illinois. Today, AI tools can produce full songs from a text prompt.

### The worries

Record labels and artists have sued AI companies over training on copyrighted recordings (from 2024). Voice-cloning can imitate a singer without consent. Platforms now debate labelling AI tracks.

### The promise

AI can help a beginner compose, restore old recordings, separate instruments in a mix (used to rescue the Beatles' "Now and Then", released 2023) and translate songs into other languages.

### What music still needs

Music is also a relationship: a performer and a listener, a community at a concert, a singer whose life we know. Curious Sparoows labels any AI-assisted work openly.

**Remember this:** AI can make sounds; meaning still comes from the people who make and share them.

## MUS-00.09 Recap: the story of music in one page

**Short answer:** Music began with voices and bodies, became sacred and systematic in ancient India, China and Greece, grew into great classical traditions, was captured by recording, spread by radio and streaming, and is now being reshaped by AI. Its power comes from rhythm, melody and the human need to feel together.

**Keywords:** history of music summary, music explained, story of music

1. **Body and voice** (forever): song, clapping, dance.
2. **First instruments** (about 40,000 years ago): bone flutes.
3. **Ancient systems**: Samaveda chant (India), Chinese pitch pipes, Greek modes, the *Natyashastra*'s music chapters.
4. **Classical traditions**: Hindustani and Carnatic, maqam, Western classical, gamelan, gagaku.
5. **Recording** (1877 onward): phonograph, gramophone (India 1902), radio (AIR 1936).
6. **Popular music explosion** (20th century): jazz, film song, rock, pop, hip-hop.
7. **Digital** (1980s onward): CD, MP3, streaming.
8. **AI** (2020s): new tools and new questions.

**Remember this:** Every song you love joins a chain of human voices going back 40,000 years.
