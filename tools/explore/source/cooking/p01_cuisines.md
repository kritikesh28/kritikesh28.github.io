## COK-01 The hierarchy: from Forest to Leaf

> **Before you start — COK-01.** The Forest is "Cooking". It splits into six Trees: cuisines (where food comes from), taste (how we experience it), ingredients (what it is made of), techniques (how it is cooked), dishes (what it becomes) and the human side (history, people, ideas, health and practice). A dish like biryani appears under its cuisine, its technique (dum) and its family (rice dishes); the website links all three. **See also:** COK-00 (the Gateway).

- ☐ **COK-01.1 Cuisines**: continents, countries, regions; India state by state and community by community (Part 2).
- ☐ **COK-01.2 Taste and flavour**: the six rasas, the five tastes, aroma, texture and heat (Part 3).
- ☐ **COK-01.3 Ingredients**: grains to fermented foods, with their journeys (Part 4).
- ☐ **COK-01.4 Techniques**: knife skills, heat methods, Indian techniques, baking, fermentation and preservation (Part 5).

# PART 2 — WORLD CUISINES

> **About this Part.** Continent, then country, then region. Each line names the cuisine's core ingredients and signature dishes. "Cuisine" here means a shared style of cooking; within every country there are many home kitchens.

## COK-02 Asia (outside India)

> **Before you start — COK-02.** Asia holds over half of humanity and most of the world's rice eaters. Rice, soy, fish sauce, chilli and fresh herbs connect many of its kitchens. **See also:** COK-03 (India).

- ☐ **COK-02.1 China**: eight great regional cuisines, including Sichuan (mala: numbing Sichuan pepper and chilli), Cantonese (dim sum, roast meats, steaming), Shandong, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Hunan and Anhui; the wok, soy and rice or wheat.
- ☐ **COK-02.2 Japan**: *washoku* (UNESCO intangible heritage, 2013): rice, miso soup, dashi, sushi, tempura (a Portuguese gift), ramen, seasonal *kaiseki*.
- ☐ **COK-02.3 Korea**: kimchi and *kimjang* (UNESCO, 2013), bibimbap, gochujang, Korean barbecue, banchan (side dishes).
- ☐ **COK-02.4 Thailand**: balance of sweet, sour, salty and hot; tom yum, green curry, pad thai, som tam.
- ☐ **COK-02.5 Vietnam**: pho, banh mi (French baguette meets Vietnamese fillings), fresh herbs, fish sauce.
- ☐ **COK-02.6 Indonesia and Malaysia**: rendang, nasi goreng, satay, sambal; Malaysia's Malay, Chinese, Indian and Peranakan (Nyonya) blend.
- ☐ **COK-02.7 Philippines**: adobo, sinigang, lechon; Spanish and Chinese layers.
- ☐ **COK-02.8 South Asia beyond India**: Pakistan (nihari, karahi, Peshawari kebabs), Bangladesh (hilsa, bhorta), Sri Lanka (hoppers, kottu, coconut sambols), Nepal (momo, dal bhat, gundruk), Bhutan (ema datshi: chilli and cheese), Maldives (tuna and coconut), Afghanistan (Kabuli pulao, mantu).
- ☐ **COK-02.9 West Asia**: Lebanon and the Levant (hummus, falafel, mezze, tabbouleh), Iran (polo rice with tahdig, khoresh stews, saffron), Turkey (kebabs, meze, baklava, Turkish coffee: UNESCO 2013), the Gulf (machboos), Israel and Palestine (shakshuka, maqluba).
- ☐ **COK-02.10 Central Asia**: plov (UNESCO, Uzbekistan 2016), manti, laghman, nomadic dairy.

## COK-03 Europe, Africa, the Americas and Oceania

- ☐ **COK-03.1 France**: the "gastronomic meal of the French" (UNESCO, 2010); mother sauces, bread and pastry, regional cooking from Provence to Normandy; the codifier Auguste Escoffier (COK-21).
- ☐ **COK-03.2 Italy**: regional pasta, pizza (Neapolitan *pizzaiuolo* art: UNESCO 2017), risotto, olive oil, cheese; Italian cooking as a whole was inscribed by UNESCO in December 2025.
- ☐ **COK-03.3 Spain and Portugal**: tapas, paella, jamón; Portugal's bacalhau and pastel de nata; Portuguese sailors spread chilli, bread and vinegar cooking to Goa, Macau and Japan.
- ☐ **COK-03.4 The Mediterranean diet**: UNESCO (2013) for seven countries; much studied for heart health.
- ☐ **COK-03.5 Britain and Ireland**: roasts, pies, fish and chips, afternoon tea; chicken tikka masala as a British-Indian dish.
- ☐ **COK-03.6 Central and Eastern Europe**: German breads and sausages, Austrian pastry, Hungarian paprika (goulash), Polish pierogi, Russian borscht (Ukrainian borscht culture: UNESCO 2022).
- ☐ **COK-03.7 The Nordic countries**: rye bread, smoked and pickled fish, the "New Nordic" movement (Noma, 2003).
- ☐ **COK-03.8 North Africa**: couscous (UNESCO 2020, shared by Algeria, Mauritania, Morocco and Tunisia), tagines, harissa, Egyptian koshari and ful medames.
- ☐ **COK-03.9 West Africa**: jollof rice (friendly rivalry between Nigeria and Ghana), fufu, egusi soup, suya.
- ☐ **COK-03.10 East and Southern Africa**: Ethiopian injera and wat with berbere; Kenyan ugali and nyama choma; South African braai, bobotie and the Durban bunny chow (Indian roots).
- ☐ **COK-03.11 North America**: US regional food (barbecue, Cajun and Creole, Tex-Mex, soul food), Native American foods (maize, beans, squash), Canada's poutine.
- ☐ **COK-03.12 Mexico**: traditional Mexican cuisine (UNESCO, 2010): maize and nixtamal, tortillas, mole, tacos, chilli varieties.
- ☐ **COK-03.13 The Caribbean**: jerk, rice and peas; Trinidad's roti and doubles from Indian indentured workers.
- ☐ **COK-03.14 South America**: Peru (ceviche, potatoes, Nikkei and Chifa fusions; Lima's restaurants top world lists), Brazil (feijoada, açaí), Argentina (asado), Colombia (arepas).
- ☐ **COK-03.15 Oceania**: Australian bush foods and modern Australian cooking; Māori hāngī (earth oven); Pacific island taro, coconut and fish.

## COK-04 India: the many cuisines of one country

> **Before you start — COK-04.** "Indian food" is really dozens of cuisines shaped by climate, crops, religion, trade and history. A simple map: wheat and dairy in the north-west, rice and fish in the east and coast, millets and pulses in the dry interior, coconut in the south. Mustard oil, groundnut oil, sesame oil, coconut oil and ghee divide the country almost as much as language. K. T. Achaya's *Indian Food: A Historical Companion* (1994) is the standard history.

### ☐ COK-04.1 North and North-West India

- ☐ **COK-04.1a Rajasthani and Marwari**: food for a desert: dal-baati-churma, gatte ki sabzi, ker sangri, bajre ki roti, laal maas (Mewar), mirchi vada, ghevar; long-keeping foods with little water and much ghee. Marwari trading families carried their vegetarian cooking across India.
- ☐ **COK-04.1b Punjabi**: tandoor breads, sarson da saag with makki di roti, rajma, chole, dal makhani and butter chicken (popularised by Delhi's Moti Mahal after 1947; who invented it is now disputed in court), lassi.
- ☐ **COK-04.1c Kashmiri**: *Wazwan*, a multi-course Muslim feast cooked by *wazas* (rista, gushtaba, rogan josh); Kashmiri Pandit cooking with yoghurt, fennel, dry ginger and asafoetida, no onion or garlic; kahwa tea.
- ☐ **COK-04.1d Himachali and Pahari**: *dham* (festive feast), siddu, Garhwali and Kumaoni food (kafuli, bhatt ki churkani, mandua roti).
- ☐ **COK-04.1e Awadhi**: the Lucknow court cuisine: dum pukht (slow, sealed cooking), galouti kebab, nihari, sheermal, biryani in the *pakki* style.
- ☐ **COK-04.1f Mughlai**: Mughal court food: kormas, pulao, kebabs, rich gravies with nuts and cream, Persian and Central Asian roots.
- ☐ **COK-04.1g Delhi and Old Delhi street food**: chaat, parathe wali gali, kebabs of Jama Masjid.
- ☐ **COK-04.1h Uttar Pradesh and Bihar**: litti chokha, sattu, Banarasi chaat and paan, thekua, Mathura peda.

### ☐ COK-04.2 East and North-East India

- ☐ **COK-04.2a Bengali**: rice and fish (*maachh-bhaat*), mustard oil, panch phoron; shukto, machher jhol, chingri malai curry, mishti doi, rosogolla, sandesh; a formal order of courses from bitter to sweet.
- ☐ **COK-04.2b Odia**: dalma, pakhala (fermented rice water), chhena poda, temple food of Puri (*mahaprasad*); rasagola (GI, 2019).
- ☐ **COK-04.2c Assamese**: khar (alkaline dishes), masor tenga (sour fish), pitha, bamboo shoot, joha rice.
- ☐ **COK-04.2d The north-eastern states**: Naga smoked pork and bhut jolokia; Manipuri eromba and black rice (chak-hao); Meghalaya's jadoh; Mizo bai; Sikkimese momos and gundruk; Arunachal's apong (rice beer); Tripura's berma (fermented fish).
- ☐ **COK-04.2e Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and tribal cuisines**: mahua, red-ant chutney (*chapda*, Odisha and Chhattisgarh; GI for Similipal kai chutney, 2024), millets, forest greens, rice beer (*handia*).

### ☐ COK-04.3 West India

- ☐ **COK-04.3a Gujarati**: a sweet, sour and salty balance; the Gujarati thali, dhokla, thepla, undhiyu, khandvi, kadhi; Kathiawadi food is spicier.
- ☐ **COK-04.3b Maharashtrian**: varan bhaat, puran poli, misal pav, vada pav (Mumbai, 1960s), Kolhapuri mutton, Malvani seafood, modak.
- ☐ **COK-04.3c Goan**: Hindu Saraswat and Catholic traditions; fish curry rice, xacuti, vindaloo (from Portuguese *carne de vinha d'alhos*), sorpotel, bebinca, feni.
- ☐ **COK-04.3d Konkani and Mangalorean**: coconut, kokum and seafood; neer dosa, kori rotti, Mangalorean Catholic pork bafat.
- ☐ **COK-04.3e Parsi**: from Iranian Zoroastrian migrants: dhansak, patra ni machhi, salli boti, lagan nu custard, akuri; Irani cafés of Mumbai and Pune.
- ☐ **COK-04.3f Sindhi**: sai bhaji, Sindhi kadhi, koki, dal pakwan; carried to India after 1947.

### ☐ COK-04.4 South India

- ☐ **COK-04.4a Tamil and Chettinad**: sambar, rasam, idli and dosa, pongal; Chettinad's fiery pepper, star anise and kalpasi (stone flower) dishes; banana-leaf meals.
- ☐ **COK-04.4b Udupi**: temple-linked vegetarian cooking of coastal Karnataka; masala dosa and the Udupi restaurant chains that spread South Indian food across India.
- ☐ **COK-04.4c Karnataka beyond Udupi**: bisi bele bath, ragi mudde, Mysore pak, Kodava pandi curry, Mangalore buns.
- ☐ **COK-04.4d Kerala**: coconut and coconut oil, appam and stew, puttu and kadala, karimeen pollichathu, Malabar (Moplah) biryani, Syrian Christian cooking, *sadya* on Onam.
- ☐ **COK-04.4e Andhra and Telangana**: famous for heat: gongura pickle, pesarattu, pulihora; Hyderabadi biryani (*kacchi* style), haleem (GI, 2010), double ka meetha.
- ☐ **COK-04.4f Hyderabadi**: the Nizams' Deccani court cuisine, mixing Mughlai, Persian, Turkish and Telugu flavours.

### ☐ COK-04.5 Community, faith and history kitchens

- ☐ **COK-04.5a Jain food**: no root vegetables (to spare tiny life in the soil), no onion or garlic, eating before sunset; ingenious use of raw banana, dried vegetables and spices.
- ☐ **COK-04.5b Satvik food**: fresh, light, vegetarian food linked with yoga and Ayurveda; no onion, garlic or stale food.
- ☐ **COK-04.5c Temple food**: *prasad* and temple kitchens: Puri Jagannath's *mahaprasad*, Tirupati laddu (GI, 2009), Golden Temple's *langar* (feeding tens of thousands every day), Udupi Krishna Matha.
- ☐ **COK-04.5d Anglo-Indian**: railway mutton curry, mulligatawny, jalfrezi, pepper water, "country captain"; a blend of British and Indian kitchens.
- ☐ **COK-04.5e Indian-Chinese**: Kolkata's Tangra Hakka community gave India chilli chicken and hakka noodles; chicken manchurian is credited to Nelson Wang (Mumbai, 1975), and gobi manchurian is its vegetarian child.
- ☐ **COK-04.5f Muslim community cuisines**: Bohri thaal, Memon, Moplah, Kutchi Memon, Bhopali and Rampuri kitchens.
- ☐ **COK-04.5g Jewish communities**: Bene Israel, Cochin and Baghdadi Jewish food (chitarnee, aloo makala).
- ☐ **COK-04.5h Tribal and forest food**: millets, wild greens, mushrooms, insects, smoked meats and rice beers; the knowledge of Adivasi communities, often unrecorded.
- ☐ **COK-04.5i Indian diaspora food**: Durban, Trinidad, Fiji, Mauritius, Malaysia and the British curry house.
