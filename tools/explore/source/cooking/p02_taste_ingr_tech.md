# PART 3 — TASTE, FLAVOUR AND THE SCIENCE OF EATING

> **About this Part.** How we experience food, in two languages: Ayurveda's six rasas and modern sensory science. They answer different questions and are presented side by side, not as rivals.

## COK-05 The six rasas and the five tastes

> **Before you start — COK-05.** The *Charaka Samhita* lists six rasas and links each with the body's doshas; a balanced Indian thali tries to include all six. Modern science counts five basic tastes detected by receptors on the tongue, and treats pungency (chilli) and astringency (tannins) as touch and pain sensations rather than tastes. **Caution:** Ayurvedic food advice is traditional knowledge; for illness, consult a doctor.

### ☐ COK-05.1 The six rasas of Ayurveda

- ☐ **COK-05.1a Madhura (sweet)**: rice, milk, ghee, jaggery, dates; said to build and calm.
- ☐ **COK-05.1b Amla (sour)**: lemon, tamarind, curd, kokum, amla; said to stimulate appetite.
- ☐ **COK-05.1c Lavana (salty)**: rock salt, sea salt, black salt (*kala namak*).
- ☐ **COK-05.1d Katu (pungent)**: chilli, black pepper, ginger, mustard, garlic.
- ☐ **COK-05.1e Tikta (bitter)**: karela, methi, neem flowers (eaten at Ugadi), turmeric.
- ☐ **COK-05.1f Kashaya (astringent)**: raw banana, pomegranate, lentils, tea; the dry, puckering feeling.

### ☐ COK-05.2 The five basic tastes in science

- ☐ **COK-05.2a Sweet**: sugars, sensed by T1R2 and T1R3 receptors; a signal of energy.
- ☐ **COK-05.2b Salty**: sodium ions; a signal of minerals.
- ☐ **COK-05.2c Sour**: acids (hydrogen ions); a signal of ripeness or spoilage.
- ☐ **COK-05.2d Bitter**: about 25 receptor types (T2Rs); a warning of possible poison, which children dislike and adults learn to enjoy in coffee and karela.
- ☐ **COK-05.2e Umami**: glutamate and nucleotides (COK-00.05).
- ☐ **COK-05.2f Candidates**: kokumi (richness, studied by Ajinomoto researchers), fat taste (oleogustus), starchy taste.
- ☐ **COK-05.2g The "tongue map" myth**: the idea that each taste lives on one area of the tongue came from a mistranslation of a 1901 German paper; all areas sense all tastes.

## COK-06 Aroma, texture, heat and pairing

- ☐ **COK-06.1 Aroma**: most of flavour is smell (COK-00.06); spices are bundles of aromatic compounds, which is why tempering in hot fat releases them.
- ☐ **COK-06.2 The Maillard reaction**: browning of proteins and sugars above about 140 °C (Louis-Camille Maillard, 1912); the flavour of roti, toast, seared meat and roasted coffee.
- ☐ **COK-06.3 Caramelisation**: sugar browning on its own (onions slowly fried for biryani are mostly Maillard plus caramelisation).
- ☐ **COK-06.4 Texture**: crisp, crunchy, chewy, creamy, spongy; Japanese has hundreds of words for it; texture contrasts make chaat delightful.
- ☐ **COK-06.5 Heat and spice**: the Scoville scale (COK-00.02); black pepper's piperine, mustard's and wasabi's isothiocyanates (which rise to the nose), ginger's gingerol.
- ☐ **COK-06.6 Temperature and colour**: food served hot smells stronger; colour changes how sweet or fresh we expect food to taste (the "red wine" experiments of Gil Morrot and colleagues, 2001).
- ☐ **COK-06.7 Flavour pairing**: the idea that foods sharing aroma compounds go well together (Heston Blumenthal's chocolate and caviar); a 2011 study by Ahn and colleagues found Western recipes tend to share compounds while East Asian recipes tend to avoid them; a 2015 analysis by Jain, Rakhi and Bagler (IIT Jodhpur) found Indian recipes also pair ingredients with few shared compounds.
- ☐ **COK-06.8 Balance**: salt reduces bitterness, acid brightens, fat carries aroma, sugar tames chilli; the core of seasoning "to taste".

# PART 4 — INGREDIENTS AND THEIR JOURNEYS

> **About this Part.** What food is made of, with where each ingredient began. Origins come from crop-history research (FAO, Achaya, the *Oxford Companion to Food*); some are debated.

## COK-07 Grains, pulses, vegetables and fruits

- ☐ **COK-07.1 Rice**: Asian rice domesticated in the Yangtze region, with a separate early rice cultivation in the Ganga plains; basmati (GI, Indo-Gangetic plains), sona masoori, gobindobhog, joha, black rice; African rice is a separate species.
- ☐ **COK-07.2 Wheat**: from the Fertile Crescent; India's durum and bread wheats; atta, maida, suji (semolina), dalia.
- ☐ **COK-07.3 Millets (shree anna)**: jowar (sorghum), bajra (pearl millet), ragi (finger millet), and small millets (kodo, kutki, foxtail, barnyard, proso, little millet); 2023 was the UN International Year of Millets at India's proposal.
- ☐ **COK-07.4 Maize**: from Mexico; makki ki roti, bhutta, popcorn.
- ☐ **COK-07.5 Pulses (dal)**: toor (arhar), moong, masoor, urad, chana, rajma (from the Americas), lobia, moth, kulthi (horse gram); India is the world's largest producer and consumer of pulses.
- ☐ **COK-07.6 Vegetables**: native (brinjal, cucumber, gourds, taro), Old World (onion, garlic, spinach, cabbage) and New World (potato, tomato, chilli, capsicum, pumpkin, sweet potato).
- ☐ **COK-07.7 Fruits**: mango (India's national fruit, native to South and South-East Asia), banana, jackfruit (Kerala's state fruit), citrus (from the eastern Himalaya and South-East Asia), and New World guava, papaya, pineapple, custard apple.
- ☐ **COK-07.8 Nuts and seeds**: almonds, walnuts (Kashmir), cashew (from Brazil via the Portuguese), groundnut (from South America), sesame (domesticated in India, *til*), coconut.

## COK-08 Spices and masalas

> **Before you start — COK-08.** Spices drove world history: the search for pepper sent Vasco da Gama to Calicut (1498) and Columbus west. India grows more spices than any other country; the Spices Board India lists 52 (check indianspices.com). **See also:** COK-17 (history).

- ☐ **COK-08.1 Native Indian spices**: black pepper (the "king of spices", Western Ghats), cardamom ("queen of spices"), turmeric, ginger, long pepper, cinnamon (true cinnamon is from Sri Lanka; Indian cooks often use cassia).
- ☐ **COK-08.2 Spices from elsewhere**: cumin and coriander (West Asia and the Mediterranean), cloves and nutmeg (the Maluku islands of Indonesia), saffron (Persia; grown in Kashmir's Pampore), chilli (the Americas), asafoetida (*hing*, from Iran and Afghanistan; India began growing it in Himachal in 2020).
- ☐ **COK-08.3 Whole-spice tempering sets**: panch phoron (Bengal: fenugreek, nigella, cumin, black mustard, fennel), the South Indian tadka (mustard, urad dal, curry leaves, dried chilli), the North Indian jeera-hing tadka.
- ☐ **COK-08.4 Masala families**: garam masala (warm, North), sambar podi and rasam podi (South), goda masala (Maharashtra), Chettinad masala, Kashmiri masala tikki (ver), chaat masala, Kolhapuri masala.
- ☐ **COK-08.5 World spice blends**: ras el hanout (Morocco), za'atar (Levant), berbere (Ethiopia), five-spice (China), shichimi (Japan), curry powder (a British invention for Indian flavours).
- ☐ **COK-08.6 Herbs**: coriander leaves, mint, curry leaves, methi, dill (*sowa*), basil and tulsi; world herbs such as parsley, oregano, thyme, rosemary, lemongrass, kaffir lime and Thai basil.

## COK-09 Fats, dairy, proteins, sweeteners and ferments

- ☐ **COK-09.1 Oils and fats**: ghee, mustard oil (East and North), groundnut oil (West), sesame oil (*gingelly*, South), coconut oil (Kerala), olive oil (Mediterranean), butter and lard (Europe); smoke points matter for frying.
- ☐ **COK-09.2 Dairy**: milk, curd (dahi), buttermilk (chaas), paneer and chhena (acid-set), khoa and rabri (reduced milk), cheeses of the world; India is the world's largest milk producer, thanks to Operation Flood (from 1970) under Verghese Kurien.
- ☐ **COK-09.3 Meat**: goat and mutton, chicken, pork (North-East, Goa, Kerala, Coorg), beef (some states and communities; laws vary by state), game; cuts and cooking times.
- ☐ **COK-09.4 Fish and seafood**: hilsa (Bengal), rohu and catla, pomfret, surmai (kingfish), Bombay duck (*bombil*), prawns, crab; dried fish across the coasts.
- ☐ **COK-09.5 Eggs**: anda bhurji, egg curry, Parsi akuri, the world's omelettes.
- ☐ **COK-09.6 Plant proteins**: pulses, soy, tofu, tempeh, seitan, millets.
- ☐ **COK-09.7 Sweeteners**: jaggery (*gur*), khandsari, sugar (India refined sugar crystals early; "sugar" comes from Sanskrit *sharkara*), honey, palm jaggery (Bengal's *nolen gur*), date syrup.
- ☐ **COK-09.8 Fermented foods**: idli and dosa batter, dhokla, curd, kanji, jalebi batter, appam; Himalayan and North-Eastern ferments (gundruk, axone, tungrymbai, bamboo shoot); world ferments (kimchi, miso, sauerkraut, yoghurt, sourdough, kombucha, cheese, wine and beer).

# PART 5 — TECHNIQUES

> **About this Part.** How food is cooked. Each technique has a one-line "what" and its home cuisines. Recipes are described, not copied.

## COK-10 Knife work and heat methods

### ☐ COK-10.1 Cutting

- ☐ **COK-10.1a Knife basics**: a sharp knife is safer than a blunt one; the claw grip; the Indian *boti* or *bonti* (a floor blade used in Bengal and Odisha) and the *hasiya*.
- ☐ **COK-10.1b Classic cuts**: dice, julienne, brunoise, chiffonade, mince, chop; Indian *bareek* (fine) and *lachha* (rings).
- ☐ **COK-10.1c Grinding**: sil-batta (grinding stone), mortar and pestle, the wet grinder for idli batter, mixer-grinder.

### ☐ COK-10.2 Dry heat

- ☐ **COK-10.2a Roasting and baking**: hot air in an oven or tandoor.
- ☐ **COK-10.2b Grilling and barbecuing**: direct heat: seekh kebab, satay, asado, braai.
- ☐ **COK-10.2c Dry roasting**: spices and dals on a tawa (*bhunna* without oil).
- ☐ **COK-10.2d Sand and ash roasting**: bhutta in sand, baati in embers, litti in cow-dung cake fire.

### ☐ COK-10.3 Moist heat

- ☐ **COK-10.3a Boiling and simmering**: dal, rice, stocks.
- ☐ **COK-10.3b Steaming**: idli, dhokla, momo, puttu, dim sum.
- ☐ **COK-10.3c Pressure cooking**: higher boiling point (about 120 °C) cooks dal fast; Denis Papin's "digester" (1679) is the ancestor; India's Hawkins (1959) and Prestige brands made it universal.
- ☐ **COK-10.3d Braising and stewing**: slow cooking in liquid: nihari, rogan josh, beef stew.
- ☐ **COK-10.3e Poaching**: gentle simmering (eggs, fish, *rasgulla* in syrup).

### ☐ COK-10.4 Fat-based heat

- ☐ **COK-10.4a Sautéing and stir-frying**: high heat, little oil, moving food: the Chinese wok and Indian kadhai.
- ☐ **COK-10.4b Shallow frying**: tikki, cutlet, paratha.
- ☐ **COK-10.4c Deep frying**: pakora, samosa, puri, jalebi, tempura; keep oil at about 170 to 190 °C.

## COK-11 Indian techniques

> **Before you start — COK-11.** Many Indian techniques have no exact English word. These are the ones every Indian cook knows by hand.

- ☐ **COK-11.1 Tadka, chhaunk, baghaar, oggarane (tempering)**: whole spices fried in hot ghee or oil and poured over dal or added at the start; releases aroma into the fat.
- ☐ **COK-11.2 Bhuna (roasting a masala)**: frying onion, ginger-garlic, tomato and ground spices until the oil separates; the heart of North Indian gravies.
- ☐ **COK-11.3 Dum (steam-sealed slow cooking)**: a pot sealed with dough and heated gently from above and below; dum biryani, dum aloo, dum pukht of Awadh.
- ☐ **COK-11.4 Talna (frying)**: the Hindi word for frying, from talna fish to puris.
- ☐ **COK-11.5 Tandoor**: a clay oven, often in the ground, reaching over 400 °C; naan, tandoori roti and chicken; its form is ancient across West and Central Asia, and tandoori chicken in its modern restaurant form was popularised by Moti Mahal in Delhi.
- ☐ **COK-11.6 Dhungar (smoking)**: placing a hot coal in a small bowl inside the pot, adding ghee and covering, so the dish absorbs smoke; used for dal, raita and kebabs.
- ☐ **COK-11.7 Dhuan, sigri and angithi cooking**: charcoal braziers for slow heat.
- ☐ **COK-11.8 Kadhai and tawa cooking**: the wok-like kadhai and flat tawa; the Mughal and Punjabi *tawa* kebabs.
- ☐ **COK-11.9 Fermenting batters**: idli and dosa batter left overnight; the warmth of the kitchen decides the sourness.
- ☐ **COK-11.10 Reducing milk**: making khoa, rabri and basundi by slow stirring.
- ☐ **COK-11.11 Banana-leaf and clay-pot cooking**: patrode, patra ni machhi, handi meat, matka kulfi.

## COK-12 Baking, fermentation, preservation and modern methods

- ☐ **COK-12.1 Baking**: breads (yeast, sourdough), cakes, biscuits, pastry (shortcrust, puff, choux); Indian bakeries of Goa and the Irani cafés; *nankhatai* and Kerala's plum cake.
- ☐ **COK-12.2 Fermentation**: yeast (bread, beer), lactic acid bacteria (curd, kimchi, idli, kanji), moulds (cheese, soy sauce, miso); the scientist Louis Pasteur showed in the 1850s and 1860s that microbes cause fermentation.
- ☐ **COK-12.3 Pickling**: in oil and spices (Indian *achaar*), in vinegar (Goan and Anglo-Indian), in brine (sauerkraut); the sun-cured mango achaar of North India and the avakaya of Andhra.
- ☐ **COK-12.4 Drying**: sun-dried vegetables (*sukha*, ker sangri, vadis, papads, sandige), dried fish, jerky.
- ☐ **COK-12.5 Smoking and curing**: Naga and Khasi smoked meats, European ham and salmon.
- ☐ **COK-12.6 Sugar preserving**: murabba, jams, candied peel, gulkand.
- ☐ **COK-12.7 Freezing and canning**: Nicolas Appert's bottled foods (1810); Clarence Birdseye's quick-freezing (1920s).
- ☐ **COK-12.8 Molecular gastronomy and modernist cooking**: the science of cooking, named by Nicholas Kurti and Hervé This (1988); spherification, foams and sous-vide (cooking sealed food in a precise water bath); chefs Ferran Adrià (elBulli) and Heston Blumenthal (The Fat Duck); Gaggan Anand's "progressive Indian" food.
