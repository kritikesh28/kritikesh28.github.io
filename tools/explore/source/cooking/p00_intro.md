## COK-00 The Gateway: Did cooking make us human?

**Web address:** /cooking/did-cooking-make-us-human/ · **Keywords:** history of cooking, world cuisines, Indian cuisine, science of taste, cooking for beginners

No other animal cooks. Chimpanzees chew raw fruit and leaves for about six hours a day; humans eat in an hour or two and spend the rest of the day talking, building and dreaming. The primatologist Richard Wrangham argues that fire and cooking gave us that time and the energy for our large brains. Whether or not he is fully right, every culture on Earth cooks, and every culture believes its food is the best.

This short course asks one question in six steps: **what is cooking, and why does it matter so much to us?** It goes from the first campfires to the chilli's journey from Mexico to Rajasthan, from the science of umami to the memory in grandmother's dal. Each page takes about two minutes.

## COK-00.01 What did humans eat before fire?

**Short answer:** Our ancestors ate raw plants (fruits, leaves, roots, nuts, seeds), insects, eggs and some raw or scavenged meat, softened by pounding and cutting with stone tools. Controlled fire appears in the record at least 1 million years ago (Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa), and regular hearths by about 400,000 years ago.

**Keywords:** what did early humans eat, raw food diet history, discovery of fire, cooking hypothesis, Wrangham Catching Fire

### Food before fire

Stone tools are at least 3.3 million years old. Cut marks on animal bones show that early humans sliced meat and broke bones for marrow. Plants were the daily staple: tubers dug from the ground, seeds and fruit.

### The cooking hypothesis

In *Catching Fire: How Cooking Made Us Human* (2009), Richard Wrangham argues that cooking began with *Homo erectus*, perhaps 1.8 million years ago. Cooking makes starch and protein easier to digest, kills germs and softens food, so our guts and teeth could shrink while our brains grew.

### The debate

Clear evidence of fire that old is rare; many archaeologists think regular cooking came later. Everyone agrees, though, that by the time of modern humans, cooking was universal. No known human society eats only raw food.

**Remember this:** Cooking may be older than our species, and it changed our bodies.

**Next:** If cooking is about pleasure, why do we love chilli, which hurts?

## COK-00.02 Why do we love spicy food that hurts?

**Short answer:** Chilli's capsaicin triggers the same pain receptor (TRPV1) that senses real heat, so the brain thinks the mouth is burning. The body releases endorphins, and the psychologist Paul Rozin calls the pleasure "benign masochism": enjoying a thrill we know is safe, like a roller coaster.

**Keywords:** why is chilli hot, capsaicin, Scoville scale, why we like spicy food, benign masochism

### A fake fire

Capsaicin binds to TRPV1, a receptor that normally warns us of temperatures above about 43 °C. David Julius discovered this receptor using capsaicin, work that shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2021.

### Measuring heat

The **Scoville scale** (Wilbur Scoville, 1912) once used tasters diluting a chilli extract until the heat vanished; today labs measure capsaicin directly. Bell peppers score 0; India's *bhut jolokia* (ghost pepper, from the north-east) passed 1 million Scoville units and was the world's hottest in 2007.

### Why spice?

- **Pleasure and thrill** (Rozin).
- **Culture**: children learn to like chilli by eating with family.
- **Food safety**: the biologists Jennifer Billing and Paul Sherman (1998) found spice use is higher in hot countries, and argued spices help fight food spoilage; this idea is debated.

**Remember this:** Chilli fools the heat sensor; we enjoy the safe danger.

**Next:** Chilli feels so Indian. How did it reach India from America?

## COK-00.03 How did the chilli reach India if it came from America?

**Short answer:** Chilli was domesticated in Mexico thousands of years ago. After Columbus reached the Americas in 1492, Portuguese traders carried chilli to Goa and the Malabar coast in the early 1500s. Within a century it had spread across India, replacing long pepper and black pepper as the main source of heat.

**Keywords:** history of chilli in India, Columbian Exchange, Portuguese food India, origin of potato tomato India, foods that came to India

### The Columbian Exchange

The historian Alfred Crosby named it the **Columbian Exchange** (1972): the movement of plants, animals and diseases between the Americas and the rest of the world after 1492. From the Americas came chilli, potato, tomato, maize, groundnut, cashew, pineapple, papaya, guava, sweet potato, cocoa and vanilla. From the Old World went wheat, rice, sugarcane, cattle and many spices.

### India's "new" foods

- **Chilli**: Goa in the 1500s; the composer-saint Purandara Dasa (16th century) praises it in a Kannada song.
- **Potato**: grown in India by the 17th century, spread widely under British rule.
- **Tomato**: common only from the 19th century.
- **Cashew and pineapple**: Portuguese gifts to Goa and Kerala.

### Before chilli

Indian food was hot with black pepper (*Piper nigrum*, native to the Western Ghats), long pepper (*pippali*), ginger and mustard.

**Remember this:** Many "Indian" ingredients are immigrants; Indian cooks made them their own.

**Next:** Some foods are found everywhere. Why does every culture have a flatbread?

## COK-00.04 Why does every culture have a flatbread?

**Short answer:** Flatbread is the simplest way to turn ground grain into food: mix flour and water, flatten, and cook on a hot stone, griddle or oven wall. It needs no yeast, little fuel and no oven. Archaeologists found charred flatbread crumbs in Jordan (Shubayqa 1) about 14,400 years old, older than farming.

**Keywords:** history of bread, flatbread types, roti history, oldest bread, chapati naan tortilla

### A world of flatbreads

- **India**: roti, chapati, phulka, paratha, puri, bhakri, naan (oven), thepla, makki ki roti, bajre ki roti, appam and dosa (fermented batter).
- **West Asia**: pita, lavash (UNESCO intangible heritage), markook.
- **The Americas**: corn tortilla, made with nixtamalised maize (soaked in lime), which unlocks niacin.
- **Ethiopia**: injera, a spongy fermented teff bread.
- **Europe**: piadina, lefse, oatcakes; pizza began as a flatbread.
- **China**: bing and scallion pancakes.

### Why so many?

- **Simple tools**: a stone or iron griddle (*tawa*, *comal*, *saj*).
- **Fuel-saving**: cooks in a minute or two.
- **Portable**: it travels with herders, soldiers and workers.
- **A plate and spoon**: you scoop with it, so it doubles as cutlery.

**Remember this:** Flatbread is older than farming and is the world's most shared food idea.

**Next:** Bread tastes of grain. But what is the savoury taste in broth, cheese and tomato?

## COK-00.05 What makes umami?

**Short answer:** Umami is the savoury taste of glutamate and certain nucleotides (inosinate, guanylate). The Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda identified it in 1908 from kombu seaweed broth and named it *umami* ("delicious taste"). Science now accepts five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami.

**Keywords:** what is umami, five basic tastes, MSG, glutamate, kokumi, six rasas

### Ikeda's soup

Ikeda wondered why his wife's *dashi* (kombu broth) tasted so rich. He extracted glutamic acid, tasted its salt (monosodium glutamate, MSG) and launched the company Ajinomoto (1909). Taste receptors for umami (T1R1 and T1R3) were identified around 2000 to 2002.

### Umami foods

Tomatoes (especially ripe or sun-dried), parmesan, soy sauce, fish sauce, mushrooms, meat broth, aged cheese, and in India dried fish, fermented bamboo shoot and slow-cooked onion-tomato *masala*.

### Is MSG harmful?

Food-safety bodies, including the US FDA and the EU's EFSA, consider MSG safe at normal intake; the "Chinese restaurant syndrome" panic of 1968 has not held up in controlled studies. FSSAI requires it to be labelled.

### Beyond five

Scientists study **kokumi** (a "richness" sensation), fat taste and others. Ayurveda counts **six rasas**: sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent, which describe effects on the body as well as taste (COK-05).

**Remember this:** Umami is the taste of savoury depth, named in Japan in 1908.

**Next:** Taste is only part of the story. Why does grandma's food taste better?

## COK-00.06 Why does grandma's food taste better?

**Short answer:** Flavour is mostly smell, and smell signals travel to the brain's emotional and memory centres (the amygdala and hippocampus) more directly than other senses. So a familiar dish brings back the people, places and feelings around it. Marcel Proust made this famous with a madeleine dipped in tea (1913).

**Keywords:** food and memory, comfort food psychology, Proust madeleine, smell and memory, why home food tastes better

### Smell is flavour

When you chew, aromas rise from the back of the mouth to the nose (retronasal smell). Pinch your nose and a strawberry tastes merely sweet. Taste gives five signals; smell gives thousands.

### Memory and emotion

Smell pathways connect closely with the limbic system, which handles emotion and memory. Studies suggest smell-triggered memories feel more vivid and emotional and often come from childhood.

### The other ingredients

- **Familiarity**: we come to like what we eat often (the "mere exposure" effect).
- **Care**: knowing someone cooked for you changes how food feels.
- **Skill**: decades of practice; *andaaz*, cooking by eye and hand.
- **Context**: festival, family and home.

**Remember this:** Grandma's food tastes better because it carries memory, love and smell.
