# PART 4 — THE PLANT WORLD FOR GARDENERS

> **About this Part.** Enough botany to garden well: how plants are named and grouped, how long they live, what shapes they take, which plants are native to each part of India, and which crops belong to which season.

## GRD-07 Names, families, life cycles and plant types

> **Before you start — GRD-07.** Every plant has one scientific (botanical) name in two parts, genus and species, a system made standard by Carl Linnaeus in *Species Plantarum* (1753). Common names change from place to place: "brinjal", "aubergine" and "eggplant" are all *Solanum melongena*. Kew's *Plants of the World Online* is the reference for accepted names.

### ☐ GRD-07.1 Plant families every gardener meets

- ☐ **GRD-07.1a Solanaceae (nightshades)**: tomato, potato, brinjal, chilli, capsicum, petunia; share many pests and diseases, so rotate them together.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1b Fabaceae (legumes)**: peas, beans, lentils, moong, chana, groundnut, also trees like the Indian laburnum (amaltas); fix nitrogen through root nodules.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1c Brassicaceae (cabbage family)**: cabbage, cauliflower, broccoli, mustard, radish, turnip; cool-season crops in India.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1d Cucurbitaceae (gourds)**: pumpkin, cucumber, bottle gourd (lauki), bitter gourd (karela), ridge gourd, melons; warm-season climbers.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1e Poaceae (grasses)**: rice, wheat, maize, millets, sugarcane, bamboo, lawn grasses.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1f Lamiaceae (mint family)**: mint, tulsi and basil, rosemary, thyme, oregano; square stems and scented leaves.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1g Apiaceae (carrot family)**: carrot, coriander, cumin, fennel, ajwain; umbrella-shaped flower heads loved by beneficial insects.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1h Asteraceae (daisy family)**: marigold, sunflower, zinnia, chrysanthemum, lettuce; the largest flowering plant family alongside the orchids.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1i Amaryllidaceae (onion family, in the broad sense)**: onion, garlic, leek and ornamental bulbs.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1j Rosaceae (rose family)**: rose, apple, pear, strawberry, peach, almond.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1k Rutaceae (citrus family)**: lemon, orange, curry leaf, bael.
- ☐ **GRD-07.1l Zingiberaceae (ginger family)**: ginger, turmeric, cardamom.

### ☐ GRD-07.2 Life cycles

- ☐ **GRD-07.2a Annuals**: complete life in one season (marigold, coriander, most vegetables).
- ☐ **GRD-07.2b Biennials**: grow leaves in year one and flower in year two (carrot, onion and cabbage when grown for seed).
- ☐ **GRD-07.2c Perennials**: live for many years (curry leaf, roses, fruit trees, asparagus); herbaceous perennials die back and return.
- ☐ **GRD-07.2d "Grown as annual"**: tropical perennials like chilli and tomato are often grown for one season.

### ☐ GRD-07.3 Plant types

- ☐ **GRD-07.3a Trees**: one woody trunk, usually over about 5 m.
- ☐ **GRD-07.3b Shrubs**: several woody stems, under about 5 m (hibiscus, ixora).
- ☐ **GRD-07.3c Climbers and creepers**: twining (beans), tendril (gourds), scrambling (bougainvillea), clinging (money plant).
- ☐ **GRD-07.3d Grasses and bamboos**.
- ☐ **GRD-07.3e Bulbs, corms, tubers and rhizomes**: onion, gladiolus, potato, ginger; underground food stores.
- ☐ **GRD-07.3f Ferns, mosses and cycads**: older plant groups that reproduce by spores (ferns, mosses) or seeds in cones (cycads).
- ☐ **GRD-07.3g Succulents and epiphytes**: water-storers (aloe, cacti) and air-plants that grow on trees (many orchids).
- ☐ **GRD-07.3h Aquatic plants**: lotus, water lily, water chestnut (singhara).

## GRD-08 Native plants of India by region

> **Before you start — GRD-08.** Native plants are adapted to local soil, rain and insects, need less care, and feed local wildlife. The Botanical Survey of India (1890) documents India's flora, which BSI counts at over 21,000 species of flowering plants (check bsi.gov.in for the latest count). Exotic is not bad, but invasive species such as *Lantana camara*, *Prosopis juliflora* (vilayati babool) and water hyacinth damage ecosystems.

- ☐ **GRD-08.1 Himalaya and the hills**: deodar, rhododendron (buransh), oak (banj), blue pine, Himalayan cherry (padam), primulas; many orchids in the east.
- ☐ **GRD-08.2 North Indian plains**: neem, peepal, banyan, amaltas, palash (flame of the forest), shisham, arjun, kadamba, harsingar (parijat).
- ☐ **GRD-08.3 Thar and the dry west**: khejri (Rajasthan's state tree), rohida, ber, kair, phog, aak, desert grasses such as sewan.
- ☐ **GRD-08.4 Deccan plateau**: tamarind, jamun, karanj (pongamia), sandalwood, mahua, custard apple in the wild.
- ☐ **GRD-08.5 Western Ghats**: a biodiversity hotspot; many endemic species, wild pepper and cardamom, *Strobilanthes kunthiana* (neelakurinji), which blooms once in about 12 years.
- ☐ **GRD-08.6 Eastern and north-eastern India**: sal, bamboos, orchids (Arunachal and Sikkim), wild citrus (India's north-east is a centre of citrus diversity), the Assam tea plant.
- ☐ **GRD-08.7 Coast and islands**: coconut, mangroves (Sundarbans, Bhitarkanika), screwpine (kewda), sea almond (*Terminalia catappa*).

## GRD-09 Seasons and crop calendars

> **Before you start — GRD-09.** India has three cropping seasons named after the monsoon cycle, while temperate countries think in spring, summer, autumn and winter. Gardeners in the hills follow a temperate calendar; gardeners in the south can sow most crops nearly year-round. See GRD-25 for a month-by-month calendar.

- ☐ **GRD-09.1 Kharif (monsoon season)**: sown with the rains, June to July, harvested September to October: rice, maize, millets, moong, groundnut, cotton; garden okra, gourds, beans, brinjal.
- ☐ **GRD-09.2 Rabi (winter season)**: sown October to November, harvested March to April: wheat, mustard, chana, peas; garden cauliflower, cabbage, carrot, radish, spinach, methi, coriander, winter flowers.
- ☐ **GRD-09.3 Zaid (short summer season)**: sown February to March with irrigation: watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, bottle gourd, moong.
- ☐ **GRD-09.4 Temperate seasons**: spring sowing after the last frost, summer growth, autumn harvest and bulb planting, winter rest; used in the Himalaya and most of Europe and North America.
- ☐ **GRD-09.5 Day length**: some plants flower by day length (photoperiodism): onion varieties are bred as "short-day" for India's latitudes and "long-day" for northern Europe.

# PART 5 — SOIL, WATER, LIGHT AND CLIMATE

> **About this Part.** The four things every plant needs from its place. Get these right and most problems never appear.

## GRD-10 Soil

> **Before you start — GRD-10.** ICAR classifies India's soils into eight major groups (alluvial, black, red and yellow, laterite, arid, saline, peaty and forest soils); see the Geography index (GEO) of the Curriculum Index for the soil map. The Soil Health Card scheme (2015) gives farmers free soil tests and advice. **See also:** GRD-00.03.

- ☐ **GRD-10.1 Texture**: sand (drains fast, holds little), silt, clay (holds water and nutrients, drains slowly), loam (a balance; the gardener's ideal). Test: wet a pinch and rub; gritty is sandy, sticky is clay.
- ☐ **GRD-10.2 Structure**: how particles clump into crumbs; organic matter and roots build it, compaction and heavy tilling break it.
- ☐ **GRD-10.3 pH**: acidity scale from 0 to 14; most vegetables like 6.0 to 7.5; blueberries, tea and azaleas like acid soil; much of Rajasthan's soil is alkaline.
- ☐ **GRD-10.4 Nutrients**: the big three NPK (nitrogen for leaves, phosphorus for roots and flowers, potassium for overall strength), secondary (calcium, magnesium, sulphur) and micronutrients (iron, zinc, boron, manganese, copper, molybdenum).
- ☐ **GRD-10.5 Indian soil groups for gardeners**: alluvial (Indo-Gangetic plains, fertile), black or regur (Deccan, holds water, cracks when dry; good for cotton), red and laterite (south and east, often acidic and low in nutrients; add compost), arid desert soils (sandy, low organic matter; mulch heavily).
- ☐ **GRD-10.6 Soil testing**: home pH strips, state soil-testing labs, Soil Health Card portal (soilhealth.dac.gov.in).

## GRD-11 Compost, mulch and fertilisers

> **Before you start — GRD-11.** Compost is the gardener's gold: decomposed organic matter that feeds soil life, improves structure and holds water. A balanced heap mixes "greens" (kitchen scraps, fresh leaves: nitrogen) and "browns" (dry leaves, paper, straw: carbon).

- ☐ **GRD-11.1 Hot composting**: a large, turned heap that heats to about 55 to 65 °C, killing many weed seeds; ready in weeks to a few months. The Indore method was developed by Albert Howard and Yeshwant Wad at Indore in the 1920s and 1930s, and Howard's *An Agricultural Testament* (1940) helped start the organic movement.
- ☐ **GRD-11.2 Cold composting**: a slow pile left alone for six months to a year.
- ☐ **GRD-11.3 Vermicompost**: compost made by earthworms (usually red wigglers, *Eisenia fetida*); fine, rich castings.
- ☐ **GRD-11.4 Bokashi**: fermenting food waste with inoculated bran in a sealed bucket, then burying it; developed in Japan.
- ☐ **GRD-11.5 Leaf mould**: decomposed dry leaves; an excellent soil conditioner.
- ☐ **GRD-11.6 Home and community composting in India**: khamba (terracotta stacks), aerobic bins, Bengaluru's and Pune's resident-welfare composting.
- ☐ **GRD-11.7 Mulch**: a layer of straw, dry leaves, wood chips or cardboard on the soil; keeps moisture in, keeps weeds down and cools roots in Indian summers.
- ☐ **GRD-11.8 Organic inputs**: cow-dung manure, neem cake, bone meal, wood ash (potassium), *jeevamrut*, *panchagavya* and *kunapajala* (traditional ferments; evidence is still limited), seaweed extract.
- ☐ **GRD-11.9 Mineral fertilisers**: urea, DAP and NPK mixes; fast but easy to overuse; never put fertiliser on dry soil or touching roots.
- ☐ **GRD-11.10 Green manure and cover crops**: sunn hemp, dhaincha, cowpea grown and turned into the soil.

## GRD-12 Water, light and climate

> **Before you start — GRD-12.** Most plant deaths at home come from too much or too little water, not from pests. Water deeply and less often, so roots grow down.

### ☐ GRD-12.1 Water

- ☐ **GRD-12.1a Watering by hand**: early morning is best; water the soil, not the leaves.
- ☐ **GRD-12.1b Drip irrigation**: water dripped at the root zone; saves 30 to 60 per cent of water compared with flood irrigation, by many Indian studies; Israel's Netafim (1965) made it commercial. India's PM Krishi Sinchayee Yojana promotes "more crop per drop".
- ☐ **GRD-12.1c Wicking and self-watering pots**: a wick or reservoir under the soil.
- ☐ **GRD-12.1d Clay-pot irrigation**: buried unglazed pots (*olla* or *matka* irrigation) that seep water slowly; an old technique of many dry lands.
- ☐ **GRD-12.1e Rainwater harvesting**: barrels, recharge pits and the traditional tankas and johads of Rajasthan.
- ☐ **GRD-12.1f Signs of trouble**: wilting with wet soil and yellow leaves usually means overwatering and root rot; crisp, dry leaf edges mean underwatering or heat.

### ☐ GRD-12.2 Light

- ☐ **GRD-12.2a Full sun**: 6 hours or more of direct sun (most vegetables and flowers).
- ☐ **GRD-12.2b Partial sun or shade**: 3 to 6 hours (leafy greens, many herbs).
- ☐ **GRD-12.2c Full shade**: under 3 hours (ferns, many indoor plants).
- ☐ **GRD-12.2d Indoor light**: "bright indirect" means near a window but out of harsh direct rays; LED grow lights help.

### ☐ GRD-12.3 Climate

- ☐ **GRD-12.3a USDA hardiness zones**: a map of the coldest winter temperature in 13 zones; used worldwide in seed catalogues but built for cold climates.
- ☐ **GRD-12.3b RHS hardiness ratings**: H1a to H7, for the UK.
- ☐ **GRD-12.3c India's agro-climatic zones**: the Planning Commission defined 15 agro-climatic regions (1989), and ICAR's National Agricultural Research Project uses 127 agro-climatic zones; your state agricultural university's crop calendar is the best guide.
- ☐ **GRD-12.3d Microclimates**: a warm south-facing wall, a windy terrace or a shady courtyard can differ from the city's weather.
- ☐ **GRD-12.3e Climate change and gardening**: earlier springs, heatwaves and erratic rain; responses include mulch, shade nets, heat-tolerant varieties and rainwater storage.
