## GRD-00 The Gateway: How did a seed in the soil become the first step of every civilisation?

**Web address:** /gardening/seed-to-civilisation/ · **Keywords:** gardening for beginners, history of gardening, types of gardening, kitchen garden India, why gardening is good for you

Every city on Earth stands on a quiet invention: someone, about 11,000 years ago, saved a handful of seeds, put them in the ground and waited. Farming gave us villages, villages gave us cities, and cities gave us writing, mathematics and gardens made just for beauty. When you sow a coriander seed in a pot on your balcony, you are repeating the first step of civilisation.

This short course asks one question in six steps: **what happens between a seed and a garden, and why does it change us?** It goes from the first farmers of the Fertile Crescent and Mehrgarh to the four-part gardens of the Mughals, from the living world inside a spoonful of soil to the science of why gardeners feel calmer. ## GRD-00.01 Who planted the first garden?

**Short answer:** Nobody knows the first gardener's name, but farming began independently in several places: the Fertile Crescent of West Asia about 11,500 years ago, China, New Guinea, Mesoamerica, the Andes, Africa and South Asia. At Mehrgarh in today's Balochistan, people grew barley and wheat and herded cattle by about 7000 BCE.

**Keywords:** first farmers, Neolithic revolution, Mehrgarh, origin of agriculture, first garden in history

### The Neolithic revolution

The archaeologist V. Gordon Childe called it the "Neolithic revolution" (1936): the change from gathering wild food to growing it. It was not one sudden event but a slow partnership. Wild wheat drops its seeds when ripe; a few mutant plants kept them on the stalk. People harvested those, sowed them again and, without meaning to, bred crops that need humans to plant them.

### Many cradles

- **West Asia**: emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, lentils, peas, flax (about 9500 BCE).
- **China**: rice in the Yangtze valley, millet in the Yellow River valley.
- **New Guinea**: taro and bananas, about 7000 years ago.
- **The Americas**: maize, beans and squash (the "three sisters"), potatoes in the Andes.
- **Africa**: sorghum, pearl millet, African rice, yams.
- **South Asia**: Mehrgarh's barley and wheat; later rice, millets, mung bean, cotton and sesame in the Indus and Ganga lands.

### Gardens for beauty

Once food was secure, people began to grow plants simply to enjoy them. Egyptian tomb paintings show walled gardens with pools (about 1400 BCE), and Mesopotamian kings boasted of their parks.

**Remember this:** Farming began in many places at once, and South Asia was one of them.

**Next:** A seed has no eyes. How does it know which way is up?

## GRD-00.02 How does a seed know which way is up?

**Short answer:** Plants sense gravity with tiny starch grains called statoliths that settle at the bottom of special cells in the root tip. The plant hormone auxin then gathers on one side, so the root curves down and the shoot curves up. This is called gravitropism; growing towards light is phototropism.

**Keywords:** how seeds germinate, gravitropism, phototropism, auxin, seed germination for kids

### Waking up

A seed is a baby plant with a packed lunch, wrapped in a coat. It waits (dormancy) until water, warmth and air tell it to wake. It drinks water, swells, splits its coat and sends out a root (the radicle) first, then a shoot.

### Feeling gravity

In the root cap, cells called statocytes hold heavy starch grains. Whichever way you turn the seed, the grains sink to the "floor" of each cell, and the plant reads that as down. Charles Darwin and his son Francis showed in *The Power of Movement in Plants* (1880) that the tip of a seedling senses light and sends a signal down; that signal was later identified as auxin.

### Try it

Soak a few mung beans, place them in a clear glass between wet tissue and the glass wall, and turn the glass on its side after three days. Within a day the roots bend downwards again.

**Remember this:** Roots go down and shoots go up because the plant feels gravity with starch grains and steers with a hormone.

**Next:** Roots grow down into soil. But what is soil, and why do gardeners call it alive?

## GRD-00.03 Why is soil alive?

**Short answer:** Healthy soil is a living city. A single teaspoon can hold billions of microorganisms: bacteria, fungi, protozoa and tiny animals that break down dead matter, make nutrients available and build the crumbly structure that holds water and air.

**Keywords:** what is soil, soil health, living soil, soil microorganisms, compost and soil

### What soil is made of

Roughly, a good garden soil is about half solid (minerals from weathered rock plus a few per cent of organic matter) and half pore space, shared by water and air. The minerals come in three sizes: sand (gritty), silt (silky) and clay (sticky). The mix of the three is the soil's texture (GRD-10).

### The living part

- **Bacteria and fungi** decompose leaves and roots. Some bacteria (*Rhizobium*) live in the root nodules of peas and beans and fix nitrogen from the air.
- **Mycorrhizal fungi** join plant roots and stretch the root system many times over, trading water and phosphorus for sugar.
- **Earthworms**, which Darwin praised in his last book (1881), mix and aerate the soil.

### Why it matters

The FAO says about 95 per cent of our food comes directly or indirectly from soil, and that soil forms very slowly: a centimetre can take hundreds of years. That is why compost, mulch and not leaving soil bare are the gardener's first duties.

**Remember this:** Feed the soil, and the soil feeds the plant.

**Next:** Gardens are not only for food. Why did the Mughals divide theirs into four?

## GRD-00.04 Why do Mughal gardens have four parts?

**Short answer:** The Mughal *char bagh* ("four gardens") follows a Persian plan: two water channels cross at the centre and divide the garden into four. It echoes the four rivers of paradise described in the Qur'an (of water, milk, wine and honey) and in older Persian ideas; the word "paradise" itself comes from the Old Persian *pairidaēza*, a walled enclosure.

**Keywords:** char bagh, Mughal gardens, Persian garden, Shalimar Bagh, paradise garden meaning

### From Persia to India

The first Mughal emperor, Babur, missed the gardens of Central Asia and laid out the Ram Bagh (Aram Bagh) at Agra in the 1520s. His successors built the great ones: Humayun's Tomb garden in Delhi (1560s), the Shalimar Bagh and Nishat Bagh in Srinagar (1600s), and the garden of the Taj Mahal, where the tomb stands at the end rather than the centre.

### How to read a char bagh

- **Walls**: shelter from dust and the world outside.
- **Water**: channels, chutes (*chadar*) and fountains for coolness and sound; terraces on hillsides let water fall from level to level.
- **Four quarters**: fruit trees, flowers and shade.
- **The centre**: a pool, pavilion or tomb.

### India's own garden ideas

India had gardens long before the Mughals: sacred groves (GRD-05.2), the *vatika* of Sanskrit poetry, temple flower gardens, and the homegardens of Kerala. The Mughal style joined this story; it did not begin it.

**Remember this:** A char bagh is a picture of paradise drawn with water.

**Next:** Few of us have palace gardens. Can a balcony feed a family?

## GRD-00.05 Can you grow food on a balcony?

**Short answer:** Yes. A sunny balcony of a few square metres can give you herbs every day, a steady supply of greens, and seasonal tomatoes, chillies and beans, if it gets at least four to six hours of direct sun. It will not replace the market, but it can replace a lot of the herbs and greens you buy.

**Keywords:** balcony garden India, terrace garden, container vegetables, grow vegetables in pots, urban gardening

### What decides success

- **Sun**: leafy greens and herbs cope with 3 to 4 hours; fruiting plants (tomato, chilli, brinjal) want 6 or more.
- **Containers**: bigger is easier. Greens need about 15 cm of soil depth; tomatoes and brinjal about 30 to 45 cm.
- **Potting mix**: never plain garden soil, which packs hard in pots. A common mix is one part soil, one part compost and one part coco peat or similar.
- **Water**: pots dry fast; water deeply when the top 2 cm is dry, and mulch.
- **Weight**: check how much load the balcony can carry before using many large pots.

### Easy first crops

Coriander, methi (fenugreek), spinach, mint, curry leaf, tulsi, spring onion, green chilli, cherry tomato, and microgreens on a windowsill (ready in 7 to 14 days).

**Remember this:** Start with sun, a big pot and greens; harvest within a month.

**Next:** Gardeners often say gardening makes them calmer. Is there evidence?

## GRD-00.06 Why does gardening make people calmer?

**Short answer:** Studies suggest it does. A 2017 meta-analysis of 22 studies (Soga, Gaston and Yamaura, *Preventive Medicine Reports*) found gardening linked with lower depression and anxiety and better life satisfaction. Explanations include gentle exercise, time outdoors in daylight, a sense of achievement, social contact and what psychologists call "soft fascination".

**Keywords:** gardening and mental health, horticultural therapy, benefits of gardening, gardening and stress, attention restoration

### What the research says

- **Stress**: in a small Dutch study (Van den Berg and Custers, 2011), gardening after a stressful task lowered cortisol more than reading indoors.
- **Attention**: Rachel and Stephen Kaplan's *attention restoration theory* (1989) proposes that natural settings rest the tired, focused mind.
- **Views**: Roger Ulrich (*Science*, 1984) found that surgical patients with a window view of trees went home a little sooner and needed fewer strong painkillers than those facing a brick wall.
- **Community gardens** build friendships and food security.

### Horticultural therapy

Horticultural therapy is gardening guided by a trained therapist for health goals: in hospitals, prisons, schools and care homes. The American Horticultural Therapy Association (1973) and the UK charity Thrive (1978) train practitioners.

### A note of honesty

Many studies are small, and people who already feel well may garden more. The evidence is encouraging, not final.

**Remember this:** Gardening combines movement, nature, purpose and people, and each of these is good for the mind.
