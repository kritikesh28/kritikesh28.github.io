## SEW-00 The Gateway: How did a needle with a hole in it change human history?

**Web address:** /sewing/needle-with-a-hole/ · **Keywords:** sewing for beginners, history of sewing, how a sewing machine works, Indian tailoring, slow fashion

A sharp stone can make a hole in a hide. A needle with an eye does something more: it carries the thread through by itself. With that small idea, people could join skins tightly, make fitted clothes, survive ice ages, and later decorate what they wore. Thousands of years later, the same idea, moved to the tip of a machine needle, built the ready-made garment industry that dresses most of the world, including the tailors' shops on every Indian street.

This short course asks one question in five steps: **how did sewing shape the way we live?**

**By the end you will be able to:**

- Say how old eyed needles are and why they mattered.
- Explain why so much traditional Indian dress is unstitched, and where stitched clothes came from.
- Describe how a sewing machine ties a lockstitch with two threads.
- Explain why the sewing machine was called a "freedom machine", and where that story is too simple.
- Argue for and against the idea that sewing can fight fast fashion.

## SEW-00.01 Who made the first needle?

**Short answer:** Nobody knows the person, but we know the age. Bone needles with an eye appear at least about 40,000 years ago; one from Denisova Cave in Siberia is often reported at around 50,000 years, though that date is debated. Earlier people used pointed awls to punch holes and pushed sinew through by hand.

**Keywords:** first needle, oldest bone needle, Denisova needle, prehistoric sewing, eyed needle

### What the needles tell us

- **Awls first, eyes later**: an awl makes the hole; an eyed needle makes the hole and pulls the thread in one movement, so seams become finer and tighter.
- **Warm, fitted clothes**: tight seams kept out wind and cold, which helped people live in the far north during the ice ages.
- **More than survival**: a 2024 study in *Science Advances* (Gilligan and colleagues) argued that very fine eyed needles also point to decorated clothing, that is, dress as a sign of who you are.
- **Thread**: sinew, plant fibres and later spun yarn. Spinning and sewing grew up together.
- **India**: copper and bronze needles are found at Harappan sites, and later texts and sculptures show both stitched and draped dress.

**Remember this:** The eye of the needle is one of humanity's great small inventions: it turned "a hole and a string" into a seam.

## SEW-00.02 Why did India love unstitched clothes?

**Short answer:** In a hot climate, a long uncut cloth such as the sari, dhoti or lungi is cool, fits any body and any age, and can be re-wrapped many ways. In many communities an uncut, unstitched cloth was also felt to be pure and right for worship. Stitched clothes were always present too, and grew with contact with Central Asia, Persia and later Europe.

**Keywords:** unstitched clothing India, sari history, dhoti, stitched vs draped, history of Indian clothing

### The two traditions

- **Draped (unstitched)**: sari, dhoti, lungi, mundu, uttariya (upper cloth), turban (pagri, safa). One loom, one length, endless styles of wrapping.
- **Stitched**: tunics appear in Kushan-period sculpture; the jama, angarkha and pyjama spread under the Sultanate and Mughal courts; the sari blouse and petticoat became common in the 19th century (the blouse style is often credited to Jnanadanandini Devi in 1870s Bengal).
- **Purity and ritual**: many temples and rituals still ask for unstitched cloth, such as a dhoti for priests or the two white sheets of the Hajj *ihram*.
- **Today**: most Indians wear both, a stitched kurta or shirt with a draped dupatta or sari, and the local darzi (tailor) stitches the blouse to measure.

**Remember this:** India was not "behind" in sewing; it chose draping where draping worked better, and stitching where stitching did.

## SEW-00.03 How does a sewing machine tie a knot with two threads?

**Short answer:** The machine needle has its eye near the point. It pushes the top thread down through the cloth and makes a small loop underneath. A rotating hook (or a shuttle in old machines) catches that loop and passes it round the bobbin, which holds the second thread. The take-up lever then pulls the loop tight, so the two threads lock together inside the fabric. This is the **lockstitch**.

**Keywords:** how a sewing machine works, lockstitch, bobbin, rotary hook, chain stitch machine

### The parts that work together

- **Needle**: eye at the point, a groove for the thread, a flat side so it sits the right way.
- **Hook and bobbin**: the hook grabs the loop; the bobbin feeds the lower thread.
- **Take-up lever**: moves up and down to pull the stitch tight and draw new thread.
- **Feed dogs**: small teeth under the presser foot that move the cloth forward one stitch at a time.
- **Tension discs**: balance the two threads so the "knot" sits in the middle of the cloth, not on top or below.
- **Chain stitch, the other way**: the first machines and today's sacks and overlockers use loops of thread linked like a crochet chain, which can unravel if pulled from one end.

**Remember this:** A hand needle goes all the way through; a machine needle never does. The machine's genius is the needle with an eye at its point and the hook that catches its loop.

## SEW-00.04 Why was the sewing machine called a freedom machine?

**Short answer:** In the late 1800s the home sewing machine cut the hours of hand-stitching that fell mainly on women, and Singer's instalment plan let ordinary families buy one. Machines also let women earn money from home. But the same machine made factory sweatshops possible. It freed some women and tied others to long hours for low pay.

**Keywords:** history of the sewing machine, Singer, Elias Howe, women and sewing machine, sweatshop history

### Both sides of the story

- **Time saved**: a shirt that took many hours by hand could be stitched in about an hour by machine.
- **Credit**: Singer and his partner Edward Clark sold machines on hire purchase from the 1850s, one of the first big consumer-credit schemes.
- **Livelihood**: tailoring and stitching became home income for millions, including women in India today, where the sewing machine is a common gift in skill and welfare schemes.
- **The dark side**: garment factories with long hours, from New York's Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire (1911) to the Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh (2013), where more than 1,100 garment workers died.
- **Gandhi's view**: he is reported to have praised the Singer machine as one of the few useful things ever invented, because it served the person working it.

**Remember this:** A tool is "freedom" only when the person using it controls the time and the money it makes.

## SEW-00.05 Can sewing fight fast fashion?

**Short answer:** Partly. Fast fashion makes clothes cheap and short-lived; the Ellen MacArthur Foundation (2017) found that clothing production roughly doubled between 2000 and 2014, and that less than 1% of the material used for clothing is recycled into new clothing. Sewing helps by making clothes last longer through mending, altering, upcycling and buying fewer, better-made pieces. It cannot fix the system alone; brands and governments must change too.

**Keywords:** fast fashion, slow fashion, textile waste, visible mending, upcycling clothes

### What a needle can do

- **Mend**: a button, a seam, a patch. India's culture of repair (the darzi, the *rafoo* darner, the patched godhadi quilt) is a model.
- **Alter**: take in, let out, shorten; Indian tailors leave wide seam allowances for this.
- **Upcycle**: old saris into quilts, kurtas into cushion covers, jeans into bags.
- **Make slowly**: one garment that fits, in natural fabric, worn for years.
- **The limits**: home sewing is small next to global production; repair also needs time, skill and cheap thread, which many do not have.

**Remember this:** The greenest garment is the one you already own; the needle keeps it alive.
