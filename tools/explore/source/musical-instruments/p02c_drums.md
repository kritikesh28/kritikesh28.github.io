## INS-11 Drums: membranophones

> **Before you start — INS-11.** A drum is a skin (or plastic) stretched over a shell or frame. Most drums give unclear, "inharmonic" overtones, so they are felt as rhythm rather than melody. India's loaded-skin drums (tabla, mridangam, pakhawaj) are the great exception (INS-00.03). Avanaddha · H–S 2. **See also:** MUS-12 (tala), INS-02.2.

### ☐ INS-11.1 Indian classical drums

- ☐ **Tabla**: pair of drums, the wooden *dayan* (right) and the metal or clay *bayan* (left), each with a black syahi; the main drum of Hindustani music since the 18th century; gharanas of Delhi, Ajrara, Lucknow, Farukhabad, Benares and Punjab; **Ahmed Jan Thirakwa**, **Alla Rakha**, **Kishan Maharaj**, **Zakir Hussain** (1951–2024), Anindo Chatterjee, Swapan Chaudhuri, Anuradha Pal.
- ☐ **Pakhawaj**: barrel drum with two heads, ancestor-cousin of the tabla; accompanies dhrupad and temple music; **Kudau Singh**, Raja Chhatrapati Singh, Pandit Bhavani Shankar.
- ☐ **Mridangam**: Carnatic double-headed barrel drum of jackfruit wood, the right head with a permanent black paste, the left with a wet wheat paste applied before playing; **Palghat Mani Iyer**, **Umayalpuram Sivaraman**, Karaikudi Mani, Trichy Sankaran.
- ☐ **Thavil**: barrel drum played with a stick and capped fingers alongside the nadaswaram; Valayapatti Subramaniam.
- ☐ **Kanjira**: small frame drum with one pair of jingles; once covered with monitor-lizard skin (now banned under the Wildlife Protection Act, so goatskin is used); G. Harishankar, V. Selvaganesh.
- ☐ **Mardala** (Odisha, Odissi music), **khol** (clay drum of Bengal, Assam and Odisha kirtan), **pung** (Manipur; the *pung cholom* drum dance), **mizhavu** (large copper pot drum of Kerala's Koodiyattam).

### ☐ INS-11.2 Indian folk and ritual drums

- ☐ **Dhol**: big double-headed barrel drum played with sticks; Punjab bhangra, Gujarat garba, Assam Bihu, the **dhol-tasha** processions of Pune.
- ☐ **Dholak**: smaller two-headed hand drum of weddings, qawwali and film songs.
- ☐ **Nagara (naqqara)**: pair of kettle drums of Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and the royal *naubat* ensembles; played at temples and the dargah of Ajmer.
- ☐ **Chenda** (Kerala): cylindrical drum played with sticks; the thundering **chenda melam** of temple festivals and Kathakali; Peruvanam Kuttan Marar.
- ☐ **Edakka (idakka)**: Kerala hourglass drum whose pitch changes as the player squeezes its strings: it can play melodies.
- ☐ **Maddalam and timila**: barrel and hourglass drums of Kerala's *panchavadyam* (five instruments: timila, maddalam, ilathalam, idakka and kombu).
- ☐ **Damaru**: small two-headed hourglass drum rattled by beads on strings; Shiva's drum; the fourteen *Maheshwara Sutras* of Sanskrit grammar are said, in legend, to have sounded from it.
- ☐ **Hudka (hurka)**: hourglass drum of Uttarakhand and Himachal bards.
- ☐ **Dhak** (Bengal): large drum of Durga Puja, played with sticks and hung from the shoulder.
- ☐ **Chang (daf)**: frame drum of Rajasthan's Holi songs and Punjab; **halgi** of Maharashtra; **tasha** (kettle drum played with sticks).
- ☐ **Parai (thappu)**: Tamil frame drum, long linked to caste oppression and now reclaimed as a proud art of Dalit cultural movements.
- ☐ **Urumi** (Tamil Nadu, hourglass with a friction-like groan), **dollu** (Karnataka; *Dollu Kunitha* dance), **dappu** (Telangana and Andhra), **mandar** (Jharkhand), **nagada and tamak** (Adivasi drums of central India), **madal** (Nepal and the hills), **ghumot** (Goa's clay pot drum, declared Goa's heritage instrument in 2019).

### ☐ INS-11.3 Drums of the world

- ☐ **Djembe** (West Africa, Mali and Guinea): goblet drum of the Mandinka, carved from one log; bass, tone and slap strokes; Mamady Keïta.
- ☐ **Talking drum (dùndún)** (Yoruba, Nigeria): hourglass drum squeezed under the arm to copy the tones of speech.
- ☐ **Darbuka (doumbek, tabla in Arabic)**: goblet drum of the Middle East and North Africa.
- ☐ **Daf, bendir, riq, tar**: frame drums of Persia, North Africa and the Arab world; **bodhrán** (Ireland); **tambourine** (Europe); **pandeiro** (Brazil).
- ☐ **Taiko** (Japan): big barrel drums; *kumi-daiko* ensemble style since the 1950s (Daihachi Oguchi); Kodo.
- ☐ **Janggu** (Korea): hourglass drum of court and folk music.
- ☐ **Conga, bongo and timbales** (Cuba): Afro-Cuban drums of salsa and Latin jazz; Mongo Santamaría, Tito Puente.
- ☐ **Surdo, repinique, caixa** (Brazil): samba school drums; **atabaque** for candomblé and capoeira.
- ☐ **Timpani (kettle drums)**: tuned copper bowls with pedals; the orchestra's thunder.
- ☐ **Snare drum and bass drum**: military origins; the snare's wires give a crisp rattle.
- ☐ **Drum kit (drum set)**: one player with bass drum, snare, toms, hi-hat and cymbals; grew in New Orleans and vaudeville; the bass-drum pedal patented by **William F. Ludwig** (1909); Gene Krupa, Buddy Rich, Max Roach, John Bonham; in India **Sivamani**, Ranjit Barot.
- ☐ **Frame and hand drums of the Americas**: the Native American powwow drum, Hawaiian *pahu*, Peruvian *bombo*.
- ☐ **Plucked and friction drums**: the Baul **gopichand (ektara)**, **khamak** and Rajasthan's **bhapang** (plucked drums, INS-02.2); the Brazilian **cuíca** (friction drum, the "laughing" sound of samba).

## INS-12 Idiophones: gongs, bells, cymbals, xylophones and lamellophones

> **Before you start — INS-12.** In an idiophone the whole solid body vibrates: metal, wood, stone, clay, glass or bamboo. They include the oldest and the newest instruments (stone chimes and the handpan). Ghana · H–S 1.

### ☐ INS-12.1 Indian idiophones

- ☐ **Ghatam**: clay pot of the Carnatic ensemble, played with fingers, palms and the stomach pressing the mouth; the best are made at **Manamadurai** (Tamil Nadu); **T. H. "Vikku" Vinayakram** (Grammy 1992, *Planet Drum*), Suresh Vaidyanathan.
- ☐ **Morsing (morchang)**: jaw harp of Carnatic music (morsing) and Rajasthani folk (morchang).
- ☐ **Manjira, jhanj, kartal, tala, elathalam**: hand cymbals of bhajan, kirtan and temple music; **gini** (Odisha) and the large **cymbals of Manipuri** *cymbal cholom*.
- ☐ **Khartal** (kartal): pairs of wooden clappers of the Manganiyar and Langa musicians of Rajasthan, played in dazzling rhythms.
- ☐ **Chimta**: long fire-tongs with jingles; Punjabi folk and Sikh kirtan.
- ☐ **Jal tarang**: porcelain bowls filled with water to different levels and struck with sticks; Milind Tulankar.
- ☐ **Ghungroo**: ankle bells of Kathak and folk dancers.
- ☐ **Ghanta**: temple bells; bell metal (*kansa*) bells of Odisha and Bengal.
- ☐ **Musical pillars**: carved stone pillars that ring at different pitches, at the Vittala temple in Hampi, Nellaiappar temple in Tirunelveli and others.
- ☐ **Thali, kansya and kaasi**: bell-metal plates struck in folk music; the **gogona** (Assam's bamboo jaw harp of Bihu) and **toka** (split-bamboo clapper).

### ☐ INS-12.2 Gongs and bells of the world

- ☐ **Gamelan** (Indonesia: Java and Bali): orchestras of bronze gongs (gong ageng), kettle-gongs (bonang), metallophones (saron, gender) and drums; UNESCO 2021; inspired Debussy after the Paris Exposition of 1889.
- ☐ **Kulintang** (southern Philippines, Borneo), **khong wong** (Thailand), **gong cultural space of the Central Highlands** (Vietnam, UNESCO 2005/2008).
- ☐ **Bianzhong** (China): the set of 65 bronze bells from the tomb of **Marquis Yi of Zeng** (433 BCE); each bell gives two different notes depending on where it is struck.
- ☐ **Church and carillon bells** (Europe), **temple bells** (Japan's *bonshō*), **singing bowls** (Himalayas), **cowbells** (Swiss, Latin).
- ☐ **Cymbals**: the **Zildjian** company was founded in Constantinople in 1623 and now works in Massachusetts; crash, ride, hi-hat, China cymbals.
- ☐ **Triangle, tubular bells, crotales**: orchestral metal idiophones.

### ☐ INS-12.3 Xylophones and metallophones

- ☐ **Balafon** (West Africa): wooden-keyed xylophone with gourd resonators; the **Sosso Bala**, kept in Guinea since the 13th century, is in UNESCO's first list (2001/2008).
- ☐ **Marimba**: resonated xylophone of African origin, national instrument of Guatemala; concert marimba; the **timbila** of the Chopi of Mozambique (UNESCO 2005/2008).
- ☐ **Xylophone, glockenspiel, vibraphone** (vibraphone with motor-driven vibrato, 1920s; Lionel Hampton, Gary Burton).
- ☐ **Ranat ek** (Thailand), **gambang** (Indonesia), **amadinda** (Uganda).
- ☐ **Steelpan (steel drum)**: tuned from oil drums in **Trinidad and Tobago** in the 1930s–40s; national instrument; Ellie Mannette, Winston "Spree" Simon; steel orchestras at Carnival.

### ☐ INS-12.4 Lamellophones (plucked idiophones)

- ☐ **Mbira** (Zimbabwe, Shona): metal tongues on a wooden board, often inside a gourd with rattles; sacred *bira* ceremonies; UNESCO 2020 (Malawi and Zimbabwe). Relatives: **sanza**, **likembe**, **kalimba** (commercial version made popular by Hugh Tracey, 1950s–60s).
- ☐ **Jaw harp**: metal or bamboo tongue plucked in front of the mouth, which shapes the overtones; found in Rajasthan (morchang), Assam (gogona), Siberia (khomus), Austria (Maultrommel).
- ☐ **Music box**: a pinned cylinder plucks steel teeth; Switzerland, 18th century.

### ☐ INS-12.5 Rattles, scrapers, clappers and modern idiophones

- ☐ **Rattles**: maracas, shekere (gourd with beads), rain stick, ankle rattles; **angklung** (Sundanese bamboo tubes shaken in a frame, UNESCO 2010).
- ☐ **Scrapers and clappers**: güiro, washboard, claves, castanets (Spain), spoons, wood block, slapstick, the **cajón** (Afro-Peruvian box drum, sat on and slapped; Peru's national heritage since 2001).
- ☐ **Udu** (Nigeria): clay pot with a side hole, struck and cupped for a bass "bloop".
- ☐ **Friction idiophones**: the **glass harmonica** (Benjamin Franklin, 1761; Mozart wrote for it), the **musical saw**, rubbed singing bowls.
- ☐ **Handpan (Hang)**: steel sound sculpture invented by **Felix Rohner and Sabina Schärer** of PANArt, Bern (2000); hundreds of makers now build similar "handpans".
- ☐ **Tongue drum** (steel tongue drum), **waterphone**, **Array mbira**: new 20th–21st century idiophones.

## INS-13 Electronic and electric instruments: electrophones

> **Before you start — INS-13.** Electrophones turn electricity into sound or amplify a vibration electrically (INS-00.06). They became the fifth class in 1940. **See also:** TEC (audio and computing), MUS-15 (technology and listening).

### ☐ INS-13.1 Early electric and electronic instruments

- ☐ **Telharmonium**: **Thaddeus Cahill**'s 200-tonne machine (1897 patent; demonstrated 1906) sent music down telephone lines.
- ☐ **Theremin**: **Leon Theremin** (Lev Termen), around 1920; played without touch; Clara Rockmore; the eerie sound of 1950s science-fiction films.
- ☐ **Ondes Martenot**: **Maurice Martenot** (1928); a ring on a wire controls pitch; Messiaen's *Turangalîla-Symphonie*, film scores; Jonny Greenwood.
- ☐ **Trautonium** (1930): used for the bird sounds of Hitchcock's *The Birds* (1963).
- ☐ **Hammond organ**: **Laurens Hammond** (1935); spinning tonewheels; with the **Leslie** rotating speaker, the voice of gospel, jazz and rock (Jimmy Smith).

### ☐ INS-13.2 Electric guitars and basses

- ☐ **Rickenbacker "Frying Pan"** (George Beauchamp and Adolph Rickenbacker, 1931–32): early electric lap steel guitar.
- ☐ **Solid-body guitars**: Les Paul's "Log" (about 1941); the **Fender Telecaster** (sold from 1950–51 as the Broadcaster, then Telecaster), the **Gibson Les Paul** (1952), the **Fender Stratocaster** (1954); Chuck Berry, Jimi Hendrix, Eric Clapton, Jimmy Page, Prince.
- ☐ **Electric bass**: the **Fender Precision Bass** (1951); James Jamerson, Jaco Pastorius.
- ☐ **Amplifiers and effects**: valve amps (Marshall, Vox, Fender), distortion, wah, delay, looper pedals.
- ☐ **Electric guitar in India**: 1960s Calcutta and Bombay bands; film-song guitar; Indian rock (Indian Ocean, Parikrama); Carnatic electric guitar (Prasanna).

### ☐ INS-13.3 Synthesizers

- ☐ **Moog modular** (Robert Moog, 1964) and **Minimoog** (1970): voltage-controlled oscillators, filters and envelopes; **Wendy Carlos**, *Switched-On Bach* (1968).
- ☐ **Buchla** (Don Buchla, West Coast), **ARP**, **EMS VCS3**, **Prophet-5** (polyphonic, with memory, 1978).
- ☐ **Yamaha DX7** (1983): affordable digital FM synthesis, based on **John Chowning**'s research at Stanford; the sound of 1980s pop, including Indian film music.
- ☐ **Roland**, **Korg**, **Casio**: Japanese makers who put synths in every school and home; Ikutaro Kakehashi founded Roland (1972).
- ☐ **Synth artists**: Kraftwerk, Vangelis, Jean-Michel Jarre, Isao Tomita, Giorgio Moroder; in India, **Charanjit Singh**'s *Synthesizing: Ten Ragas to a Disco Beat* (1982), an early acid-house sound years before Chicago's.

### ☐ INS-13.4 Drum machines, samplers and controllers

- ☐ **Drum machines**: Wurlitzer Sideman (1959); **Roland TR-808** (1980) and **TR-909** (1983), the beats of hip-hop and house; Linn LM-1.
- ☐ **Samplers**: Mellotron (tape, 1960s), **Fairlight CMI** (1979), **E-mu Emulator**, **Akai MPC** (1988), the hip-hop producer's instrument.
- ☐ **MIDI** (1983): a shared language that lets keyboards, drum pads and computers control each other.
- ☐ **Pads and controllers**: the **Roland Octapad** (from 1985), central to Bollywood and Indian devotional music; Ableton Push, Launchpad; the ROLI Seaboard (2013) and other expressive controllers.
- ☐ **Indian electronic instruments**: the electronic tanpura and *tala* (tabla) machines (Radel, Raagini and others), Riyaz apps, and the digital shruti box.

### ☐ INS-13.5 Software, AI and new instruments

- ☐ **Software instruments**: DAWs (Ableton Live, FL Studio, Logic), virtual instruments and sample libraries of Indian instruments.
- ☐ **Auto-Tune** (Andy Hildebrand, Antares, 1997): pitch correction that became an effect (Cher's "Believe", 1998; T-Pain).
- ☐ **AI instruments**: neural synthesis such as Google Magenta's **NSynth** (2017); voice-to-instrument models; the questions they raise are discussed in MUS-00.08 and INS-29.
- ☐ **Live coding and laptop orchestras**: music made by writing code on stage (Sonic Pi, TidalCycles).

## INS-14 DIY, found and recycled instruments

> **Before you start — INS-14.** Anyone can build an instrument from what is at home. Making one is the best way to understand the physics in Part 7. **See also:** INS-26, SNT (physics of sound in the Curriculum Index).

- ☐ **INS-14.1 Water-glass xylophone**: glasses with different water levels; less water, higher note (a home jal tarang).
- ☐ **INS-14.2 Straw oboe and paper kazoo**: flatten and cut a straw to make a double reed; hum through comb and paper.
- ☐ **INS-14.3 Rubber-band and shoebox guitars, cigar-box guitars**: show length and tension (Mersenne's laws); the cigar-box guitar has a long history in American blues.
- ☐ **INS-14.4 PVC and bamboo flutes**: drill holes in a pipe; tune by enlarging.
- ☐ **INS-14.5 Washtub bass, jug and washboard**: the jug bands of the American South; skiffle in 1950s Britain.
- ☐ **INS-14.6 Bucket drumming, pots and pans**: street drumming; Indian folk musicians' use of the *matka* (water pot) and the steel *thali*.
- ☐ **INS-14.7 Recycled orchestras**: the **Recycled Orchestra of Cateura** (Paraguay), whose violins and cellos are made from landfill waste; the stage show *Stomp* (1991).
- ☐ **INS-14.8 Makerspace electronics**: Arduino synths, the Stylophone, contact microphones, circuit bending of toys.
- ☐ **INS-14.9 Safety and care**: sand sharp edges, use food-safe materials for mouthpieces, and keep volume safe for ears (MUS-22).
