# PART 2 — THE FAMILIES OF INSTRUMENTS

> **About this Part.** Every family, with every main type. Each entry gives, in one or two lines: what it is, what it is made of, how it sounds, where it comes from and who made it famous. The tag after the name shows the Indian class (Tata, Sushira, Avanaddha, Ghana) and the Hornbostel–Sachs class (H–S). Families run from wind to strings to drums to solids to electronics, then DIY.

## INS-04 Flutes: edge-blown aerophones

> **Before you start — INS-04.** In a flute the player's breath is split on a sharp edge, and the air column inside vibrates. Fingers or keys change the column's length. Sushira · H–S 421. **See also:** MUS-01 (bansuri in Hindustani music), INS-00.01 (oldest flutes).

### ☐ INS-04.1 Side-blown (transverse) flutes

- ☐ **Bansuri** (bansi, baanhi): North Indian bamboo flute with six or seven holes; **Pannalal Ghosh** (1911–1960) made a longer bass bansuri fit for classical music; **Hariprasad Chaurasia** took it to the world; Rakesh Chaurasia, Ronu Majumdar, Pravin Godkhindi today.
- ☐ **Venu** (pullanguzhal, Carnatic flute): South Indian bamboo flute, usually eight holes; the instrument of Krishna in temple art; **T. R. Mahalingam** ("Mali") remade its style in the 1930s–40s; N. Ramani, Shashank Subramanyam.
- ☐ **Murali**: a name for Krishna's flute in poetry; in practice the bansuri or venu.
- ☐ **Western concert flute**: silver, gold or nickel; the modern key system was designed by **Theobald Boehm** (cylindrical flute, 1847); Jean-Pierre Rampal, James Galway, Emmanuel Pahud.
- ☐ **Piccolo**: half-size concert flute, an octave higher; the brightest voice in the orchestra and marching band.
- ☐ **Dizi** (China): bamboo flute with an extra hole covered by a thin reed membrane (*dimo*) that gives a buzzing brightness.
- ☐ **Fife**: small high flute of military bands and folk music (Europe, Americas).
- ☐ **Irish wooden flute**: simple-system flute of Irish traditional music; Matt Molloy.
- ☐ **Ryuteki and shinobue** (Japan): transverse bamboo flutes of gagaku court music and festivals.

### ☐ INS-04.2 End-blown flutes

- ☐ **Shakuhachi** (Japan): bamboo, five holes, blown across a sharp edge at the top; played by Zen *komuso* monks as meditation (*suizen*).
- ☐ **Ney** (Persia, Turkey, the Arab world): reed flute of Sufi music; Rumi's *Masnavi* opens with "Listen to the ney".
- ☐ **Quena** (Andes): notched bamboo or bone flute of Peru and Bolivia; heard in "El Cóndor Pasa".
- ☐ **Kaval** (Balkans): long, end-blown shepherd's flute.
- ☐ **Native American (Plains) flute**: technically a duct flute with an external block; revived by R. Carlos Nakai.

### ☐ INS-04.3 Duct or fipple flutes (a built-in windway)

- ☐ **Recorder**: wooden or plastic duct flute, a family from sopranino to great bass; Renaissance and Baroque music; the classroom instrument of millions.
- ☐ **Tin whistle** (penny whistle): cheap metal duct flute of Irish and Scottish music.
- ☐ **Algoza** (alghoza, satara, jodiya pava): a pair of duct flutes played together, one as drone; Rajasthan, Punjab, Sindh, Kutch.
- ☐ **Flageolet and gemshorn**: historical European duct flutes.
- ☐ **Bamboo flutes of Adivasi India**: many tribal communities of central and eastern India have their own bamboo flutes, often played by herders; each will get a page once verified with local sources.

### ☐ INS-04.4 Vessel flutes (a hollow body, not a tube)

- ☐ **Ocarina**: clay or plastic egg-shaped flute; modern form by **Giuseppe Donati** of Budrio, Italy (1853); famous again through video games.
- ☐ **Xun** (China): one of the oldest Chinese instruments, clay egg with finger holes; examples about 7,000 years old.
- ☐ **Gemshorn and clay whistles**: animal horn or clay bird-whistles of many cultures; clay whistles from the Indus civilisation.

### ☐ INS-04.5 Panpipes (many tubes, one note each)

- ☐ **Siku / zampoña** (Andes): played in pairs that interlock notes; **nai** (Romania), made world-famous by **Gheorghe Zamfir**; **syrinx** (ancient Greece, the pipes of Pan); **paixiao** (China).

### ☐ INS-04.6 Nose flutes

- ☐ **Nose flutes** of Hawaii (*ʻohe hano ihu*), the Philippines, Borneo, Taiwan and Polynesia: breath from the nose, thought to be purer than breath from the mouth.

### ☐ INS-04.7 Correction from the brief

- ☐ **Pungi (been) is not a flute**: the snake-charmer's pungi blows into a gourd that feeds two cane pipes, each with a single beating reed. It is a reed aerophone (double clarinet, H–S 422.22), listed in INS-05. (The snake does not hear the tune; it follows the movement of the pungi.)

## INS-05 Reed instruments: reed aerophones and free reeds

> **Before you start — INS-05.** A reed is a thin strip (cane, palm leaf, metal or plastic) that opens and shuts many times a second, chopping the air. In a **double reed** two blades beat against each other; in a **single reed** one blade beats against a mouthpiece; in a **free reed** a metal tongue swings through a slot. Sushira · H–S 422 and 412.13.

### ☐ INS-05.1 Double reeds

- ☐ **Shehnai**: North Indian double-reed of weddings and temples, wooden tube with metal bell; **Ustad Bismillah Khan** (1916–2006, Bharat Ratna 2001) brought it to the concert stage and played at the Red Fort on 15 August 1947.
- ☐ **Nadaswaram** (nagaswaram): long South Indian double-reed, the most important temple instrument of Tamil Nadu, played with the thavil drum; **T. N. Rajarathinam Pillai**, Sheik Chinna Moulana.
- ☐ **Sundari**: small shehnai of Maharashtra (Solapur).
- ☐ **Kuzhal** (Kerala): short double-reed of temple ensembles.
- ☐ **Oboe, cor anglais, bassoon, contrabassoon**: the Western double reeds; the oboe gives the orchestra's tuning A.
- ☐ **Duduk** (Armenia): apricot-wood double reed with a very wide reed; dark and mournful; UNESCO lists it as Intangible Cultural Heritage (2005/2008); Djivan Gasparyan.
- ☐ **Zurna, surnay, suona, sorna**: loud outdoor double reeds of Turkey, Persia, Central Asia and China, family cousins of the shehnai.
- ☐ **Hichiriki** (Japan) and **piri** (Korea): short bamboo double reeds of court music.

### ☐ INS-05.2 Single reeds

- ☐ **Clarinet**: wooden single-reed developed by **Johann Christoph Denner** in Nuremberg around 1700; behaves like a closed tube (strong odd harmonics); Benny Goodman, Sabine Meyer. In India, A. K. C. Natarajan played Carnatic clarinet.
- ☐ **Saxophone**: brass body, single reed; patented by **Adolphe Sax** in 1846; the voice of jazz; **Kadri Gopalnath** (1949–2019) made it a Carnatic instrument.
- ☐ **Pungi / been**: gourd-and-reed double clarinet of snake charmers and the Kalbeliya of Rajasthan; the "been" sound in film songs is often a keyboard copy.
- ☐ **Launeddas** (Sardinia): triple clarinet of cane, played with circular breathing.
- ☐ **Arghul** (Egypt), **mijwiz** (Levant): double clarinets.

### ☐ INS-05.3 Bagpipes (reeds fed from a bag)

- ☐ **Mashak** (mashakbeen): bagpipe of Rajasthan, Uttarakhand and Himachal; goatskin bag; played at weddings and in the Kumaon *chholiya* dance.
- ☐ **Great Highland bagpipe** (Scotland), **uilleann pipes** (Ireland, bellows-blown, quiet and sweet), **gaita** (Galicia, Asturias), **zampogna** (Italy), **musette** (France), **tulum** (Turkey).

### ☐ INS-05.4 Free reeds

- ☐ **Sheng** (China): mouth organ of bamboo pipes in a cup, each with a metal free reed; over 3,000 years old; the ancestor of the harmonica and accordion idea.
- ☐ **Khaen** (Laos, north-east Thailand): bamboo free-reed mouth organ; UNESCO 2017.
- ☐ **Shō** (Japan): court mouth organ, a cousin of the sheng.
- ☐ **Harmonium**: free-reed keyboard pumped by bellows; patented in France by **Alexandre Debain** (1840); the hand-pumped, floor-played version was made in Calcutta by **Dwarkanath Ghose** (Dwarkin & Son, 1875). Banned on All India Radio from 1940 to 1971 as "foreign" and "out of tune" with Indian microtones; now central to qawwali, bhajan, Sikh kirtan and natya sangeet.
- ☐ **Shruti box**: a harmonium without keys, giving a drone.
- ☐ **Harmonica** (mouth organ): small free-reed instrument, made popular by Matthias Hohner (Germany) from 1857; blues, folk; R. D. Burman's film songs used it.
- ☐ **Accordion**: patented in Vienna by **Cyrill Demian** (1829); Bombay film orchestras used it widely (Kersi Lord was among its best-known players).
- ☐ **Bandoneón**: square concertina from Germany (named after Heinrich Band) that became the soul of Argentine tango; Astor Piazzolla.
- ☐ **Concertina** (England, Germany) and **melodica** (a keyboard you blow).

## INS-06 Brass and lip-vibrated instruments

> **Before you start — INS-06.** The player's lips buzz into a cup or opening, and the tube's air column picks the note. Valves or a slide add tube length. They need not be made of brass: the didgeridoo is wood and the shankh is shell. Sushira · H–S 423.

### ☐ INS-06.1 Natural and ritual horns

- ☐ **Shankh** (conch): shell trumpet blown in Hindu and Buddhist rituals and in battle in the *Mahabharata* (Krishna's Panchajanya).
- ☐ **Karnal, ransingha (narsingha), turhi, bhongal, nagphani**: long and curved brass horns of the Himalayan states, Rajasthan, Gujarat and Maharashtra; ritual, wedding and war signals.
- ☐ **Kombu** (Kerala): curved horn of the panchavadyam temple ensemble.
- ☐ **Dungchen** (Tibet): copper long horns of monasteries, up to three metres.
- ☐ **Shofar** (Jewish ritual): ram's horn, sounded on Rosh Hashanah.
- ☐ **Didgeridoo** (yidaki, Aboriginal Australia): eucalyptus trunk hollowed by termites; played with circular breathing for a continuous drone; at least 1,000 years old (rock art).
- ☐ **Alphorn** (Swiss Alps), **vuvuzela** (South Africa), **lur** (Bronze Age Scandinavia), **carnyx** (Iron Age Celts), **cornu** (ancient Rome).

### ☐ INS-06.2 Valved and slide brass

- ☐ **Trumpet**: the high brass; valves invented by **Heinrich Stölzel** and **Friedrich Blühmel** (1810s); Louis Armstrong, Miles Davis, Wynton Marsalis; Indian film brass bands; Chic Chocolate in 1950s Bombay.
- ☐ **Cornet, flugelhorn, bugle**: cousins with a softer or simpler sound; the bugle calls of armies.
- ☐ **French horn**: coiled horn with a wide bell; hand in the bell; the warmest brass.
- ☐ **Trombone**: a sliding tube; the only brass that glides between notes easily; Jack Teagarden, Christian Lindberg.
- ☐ **Tuba, euphonium, sousaphone**: low brass; the tuba was patented by **Wilhelm Wieprecht** and **Carl Moritz** (Berlin, 1835); the sousaphone wraps around a marching player.
- ☐ **Saxhorns** (Adolphe Sax's brass family), **ophicleide** and **serpent** (older bass instruments).
- ☐ **Indian wedding bands**: the brass band (*baaja*) with trumpets, clarinets and drums, from British military bands, now a lively Indian tradition of its own.
