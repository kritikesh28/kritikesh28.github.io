## INS-00 The Gateway: How did humans teach wood, skin, metal and air to sing?

**Web address:** /instruments/how-instruments-sing/ · **Keywords:** musical instruments, how instruments work, types of musical instruments, Indian musical instruments, instruments for beginners

A hollow bone, a stretched skin, a twisted gut string, a bent sheet of brass. None of these is music on its own. Yet humans have found ways to make every one of them sing, cry, thunder and laugh. There are thousands of instruments on Earth, and every culture has its own.

This short course asks one big question in seven steps: **what is a musical instrument, and how does it turn a material into music?** It goes from Ice Age bone flutes to the sitar's buzz, from the Natyashastra's four classes to the synthesizer, and ends with advice on choosing your own first instrument. Each page takes about two minutes.

## INS-00.01 What is the oldest instrument ever found?

**Short answer:** The oldest certain instruments are flutes made of bird bone and mammoth ivory from caves in south-west Germany, about 40,000 to 43,000 years old. A 50,000-year-old bone from Slovenia may be a Neanderthal flute, but scientists still argue about it.

**Keywords:** oldest musical instrument, oldest flute, Hohle Fels flute, Divje Babe flute, prehistoric music

### The flutes of the Swabian Jura

In 2008 archaeologists in the **Hohle Fels** cave found a flute carved from the wing bone of a griffon vulture, with five finger holes and a notched end. Pieces of ivory and bone flutes also came from nearby **Geissenklösterle** and **Vogelherd**. Radiocarbon dating puts them at about 40,000 to 43,000 years old (Conard and colleagues, *Nature*, 2009; Higham and colleagues, 2012). Making an ivory flute meant splitting a tusk, hollowing both halves and gluing them airtight: these were skilled makers.

### The Divje Babe puzzle

A cave bear's thigh bone with two round holes, found at **Divje Babe** in Slovenia in 1995, is about 50,000 to 60,000 years old. Its finder, Ivan Turk, argued it is a Neanderthal flute. Many other scientists think the holes are bite marks from a hyena or bear. The question is still open, which is itself worth learning: good science says "we are not sure" when it is not sure.

### Instruments that left no trace

Clapping, stamping, rattles of seeds, drums of skin and wood rot away. So the first instruments were almost certainly older than any we have found. In India, rock shelters at **Bhimbetka** show dancers, and some granite boulders and temple pillars ring when struck, like the musical pillars of the Vittala temple at Hampi.

**Remember this:** The oldest instruments we can hold are flutes, but the first music was surely the voice and the body.

## INS-00.02 Why does a guitar string make a note at all?

**Short answer:** A stretched string vibrates in a regular pattern, many times a second. A regular, repeating vibration is what our ears hear as a definite pitch. Its length, tension and weight set how fast it vibrates.

**Keywords:** how a string makes sound, frequency and pitch, standing waves, Mersenne's laws, physics of music

### Noise and notes

Drop a bunch of keys and you get noise: a jumble of vibrations at random speeds. Pluck a string and you get a note, because it vibrates back and forth at one steady rate, called its **frequency**, measured in hertz (Hz: vibrations per second). The A above middle C is tuned to 440 Hz in most of the world today.

### Three dials on every string

In 1636 the French friar **Marin Mersenne** wrote down three rules, now called Mersenne's laws:

- **Shorter** string, higher note (press a guitar fret or a sitar string and you shorten it).
- **Tighter** string, higher note (that is what tuning pegs do).
- **Lighter** (thinner) string, higher note (so a bass guitar has thick strings).

### Standing waves and overtones

A string fixed at both ends can only vibrate in whole patterns: the whole length, two halves, three thirds and so on. These are the **fundamental** and its **overtones** (harmonics), all sounding at once. The same idea works for air in a tube: a flute or a trumpet is a column of air vibrating in standing waves.

### The body that makes it loud

A bare string is quiet. The wooden body of a guitar, the gourd of a sitar or the skin of a banjo picks up the vibration and moves much more air. That is called **resonance**.

**Remember this:** A note is a regular vibration; length, tension and weight are the three dials that set it.

## INS-00.03 Why do a flute and a violin sound different on the same note?

**Short answer:** Because each instrument mixes its overtones in a different recipe and starts and ends its sound differently. This "colour" of a sound is called timbre (say TAM-ber).

**Keywords:** timbre, tone colour, overtones, harmonics, why instruments sound different

### The recipe of overtones

Every real note is a chord in disguise: the fundamental plus many quieter overtones. A flute's sound has strong fundamental and few overtones, so it sounds pure and breathy. A violin's sound has a rich spread of overtones, so it sounds bright and full. A clarinet, because its tube behaves as if closed at one end, is strong in the odd-numbered harmonics, which gives its hollow, woody sound.

### Attack, sustain, decay

Timbre is also about time. A piano note hits hard and fades away; a violin can grow louder in the middle of a note; a tabla stroke cracks and rings. Scientists call this the **envelope**. Cut the attack off a recorded piano note and many people cannot tell it is a piano.

### Materials and players

Wood, metal, skin, gut, nylon and steel each colour the overtones. So does the player. Ustad Bismillah Khan and another shehnai player sound different on the same instrument because of breath, lips and taste.

### An Indian discovery

In 1920 **C. V. Raman** showed that the Indian **mridangam** and **tabla** are unusual drums. Most drums give overtones that clash (they are "inharmonic"), so they sound like thumps. The black spot of paste on these drums (the *syahi* or *soru*) loads the skin so that its overtones line up almost like a string's. That is why the tabla can be tuned to the tonic Sa and "sings".

**Remember this:** Same note, different recipe: timbre is the mix and the timing of overtones.

## INS-00.04 How did India classify instruments 2,000 years ago?

**Short answer:** The *Natyashastra*, a Sanskrit treatise on drama attributed to Bharata (compiled roughly between 200 BCE and 200 CE), sorts instruments into four classes by how they make sound: tata (strings), sushira (hollow, wind), avanaddha (covered, drums) and ghana (solid). Europe's modern system follows the same logic.

**Keywords:** Natyashastra instruments, tata sushira avanaddha ghana, classification of Indian instruments, Bharata Muni

### The four classes

- **Tata vadya** (stretched): instruments with strings, like the veena and sitar.
- **Sushira vadya** (hollow): instruments played by blowing air, like the flute and shehnai.
- **Avanaddha vadya** (covered): instruments with a skin stretched over them, like the mridangam and tabla.
- **Ghana vadya** (solid): instruments that sound by themselves when struck, like cymbals, bells and the clay ghatam.

### Why this was clever

Bharata did not sort instruments by what they look like, or by what region they come from. He sorted them by **what vibrates**: a string, air, a skin or the solid body. That rule works for any instrument in any country, even ones he never saw.

### The link to Europe

In 1880 **Victor-Charles Mahillon**, curator of the instrument museum of the Brussels Conservatory, used four very similar classes for his catalogue. He knew the Indian system through instruments and writings sent to Brussels by the Bengali scholar **Sourindro Mohun Tagore**. In 1914 **Erich von Hornbostel** and **Curt Sachs** refined Mahillon's scheme into the system museums still use (INS-02). Other traditions have their own systems too: China's ancient **bayin** sorts instruments by eight materials (metal, stone, silk, bamboo, gourd, clay, skin and wood).

**Remember this:** String, air, skin, solid: India's four-fold system is the grandparent of the world's museum system.

**Next:** Now listen to one Indian instrument closely. Why does a sitar buzz?

## INS-00.05 Why does a sitar buzz?

**Short answer:** The sitar's strings rest on a wide, gently curved bridge called the *jawari*. As each string vibrates it brushes the curve, which shortens and lengthens it many times a second and throws energy into high overtones. That gives the bright, shimmering buzz.

**Keywords:** sitar jawari, why sitar sounds like that, sympathetic strings, tanpura buzz, Indian string instruments

### A bridge that touches back

On a guitar or violin the bridge is a sharp edge: the string leaves it at one point. On the sitar, tanpura, Saraswati veena and rudra veena, the bridge is a flat, slightly curved plate of bone, horn or wood. The string grazes it. Raman studied this too, in the early 1920s. Makers "open" the jawari by sanding the curve with great care; a good jawari is a sitar's soul.

### Strings that play themselves

Under the main strings a sitar has eleven to thirteen thin **taraf** (sympathetic) strings, tuned to the notes of the raga. They are never plucked. When a main string sounds a note, the matching taraf string vibrates by itself, giving a halo of sound. The sarangi, the Norwegian hardanger fiddle and the Swedish nyckelharpa use the same trick.

### Many instruments, one taste

The buzz is not an accident of one instrument. It is a taste shared across the subcontinent, and across Africa too: the kora's and mbira's buzzing rattles do a similar job. The point is to make each note rich and alive, to suit music that dwells long on single notes.

**Remember this:** The buzz is designed, not a fault: a curved bridge plus sympathetic strings make the sitar shimmer.

## INS-00.06 How did electricity change instruments?

**Short answer:** Electricity let instruments make sound with no vibrating material of their own (the theremin, the synthesizer) and let quiet instruments become loud (the electric guitar). It added a fifth class, the electrophones, and later turned the computer into an instrument.

**Keywords:** electronic instruments history, theremin, electric guitar invention, Moog synthesizer, electrophones

### Amplifying the old

Pickups turn a string's vibration into an electric signal that an amplifier makes loud. The Rickenbacker "Frying Pan" lap steel guitar (early 1930s) was among the first commercial electric guitars; the solid-body Fender Telecaster (1950) and Gibson Les Paul (1952) followed. Loudness changed music itself: rock, blues and Bollywood orchestras grew around it.

### Making sound from nothing

The **theremin** (Leon Theremin, around 1920) is played without touch: the hands move near two antennas. The **Moog synthesizer** (Robert Moog, 1964) builds sound from electronic oscillators and filters. The **Yamaha DX7** (1983) made digital synthesis cheap, and the **MIDI** standard (1983) let instruments and computers talk.

### Machines that keep time and copy sound

The **Roland TR-808** drum machine (1980) shaped hip-hop and pop. Samplers let any recorded sound become an instrument. In India, the electronic tanpura and tabla boxes became practice tools, and the **Roland Octapad** pads became a fixture of film-music percussion.

### Today

A laptop, a phone app or an AI model can now be an instrument. Curt Sachs added the class "electrophones" in 1940; in 2011 the MIMO project revised it again.

**Remember this:** Electricity added a fifth way to sound: no string, air, skin or solid needed.

## INS-00.07 Which instrument should you learn first?

**Short answer:** The one whose music you love most and will practise daily. For many beginners a keyboard, ukulele, recorder, bansuri or harmonium is a gentle start, because a note comes easily. Your own voice is the instrument many Indian gurus begin with.

**Keywords:** best first instrument, easiest instrument to learn, learn music beginner, which instrument for my child

### Five questions to ask

1. **What music do you love?** Love keeps you practising. A tabla lover will not stay with a violin.
2. **How fast do you want a pleasant sound?** Keyboard, ukulele and harmonium are quick; violin, sarangi and the French horn are slow at first.
3. **Your body and age**: small hands suit a ukulele or a quarter-size violin; children's teeth matter for brass.
4. **Budget and space**: a recorder costs very little; a piano needs a room.
5. **Is there a teacher or a group?** Learning with others helps most people.

### The Indian way

Many Hindustani and Carnatic gurus ask students to sing first, because the voice teaches pitch and phrasing that every instrument needs. "Gayaki ang" (a singing style) is praised on the sitar and sarangi alike.

### The honest truth

Every instrument becomes hard at a high level. Ten minutes of focused daily practice usually beats two hours once a week. Part 10 (INS-33) gives a fuller guide, with learning paths and buying tips.

**Remember this:** Pick by love, then by ease; practise a little every day.

## INS-00.08 Recap: the story of instruments in one page

**Short answer:** Instruments turn vibration into music. They make sound in four ancient ways (string, air, skin, solid) and one modern way (electricity). From 40,000-year-old flutes to synthesizers, every culture has built its own, and India gave the world its first lasting classification.

**Keywords:** musical instruments summary, history of instruments, types of instruments explained

1. **Body and voice** came first: clapping, stamping, singing.
2. **Bone flutes** (about 40,000 years ago) are the oldest instruments found.
3. **Ancient civilisations**: harps and lyres of Ur (about 2500 BCE), the Chinese bells of Marquis Yi (433 BCE), the vina of ancient India.
4. **Classification**: the *Natyashastra*'s four classes; China's eight materials; Hornbostel–Sachs (1914).
5. **The great inventions**: the violin family (Cremona, 16th century), the piano (Cristofori, around 1700), the saxophone (1840s), the drum kit (early 1900s).
6. **Electricity**: theremin, electric guitar, synthesizer, drum machine, computer.
7. **Today**: thousands of instruments, many at risk, many being revived; the choice of your first one is yours.

**Remember this:** Every instrument is a human idea about how to make a material sing.
