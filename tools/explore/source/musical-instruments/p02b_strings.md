## INS-07 Plucked lutes

> **Before you start — INS-07.** A lute has a neck along which the strings run parallel to a resonating body; the player shortens the strings by pressing them, with or without frets. Plucked with the fingers, nails or a plectrum (*mizrab*, pick). Tata · H–S 321.3. **See also:** INS-00.05 (jawari), MUS-01.

### ☐ INS-07.1 Indian plucked lutes

- ☐ **Sitar**: long-necked lute with a gourd body, movable curved frets, 6–7 main and 11–13 sympathetic strings, and a jawari bridge; developed in its present form in 18th-century North India (the old story crediting Amir Khusrau is not supported by evidence); **Ravi Shankar**, **Vilayat Khan**, Nikhil Banerjee, Anoushka Shankar, Shahid Parvez.
- ☐ **Surbahar**: bass sitar for slow, deep alap; Annapurna Devi, Imrat Khan.
- ☐ **Sarod**: fretless lute with a skin belly and a polished steel fingerboard, plucked with a coconut-shell plectrum; descended from the Afghan rabab; **Allauddin Khan**, **Ali Akbar Khan**, **Amjad Ali Khan**, Buddhadev Das Gupta.
- ☐ **Saraswati veena**: the great Carnatic lute of jackfruit wood with 24 fixed frets, four playing and three drone strings; held across the lap; **Veena Dhanammal**, S. Balachander, E. Gayathri, Jayanthi Kumaresh. Thanjavur and Bobbili (Andhra Pradesh) veenas carry Geographical Indication tags.
- ☐ **Chitra veena (gottuvadyam)**: fretless Carnatic lute played with a slide; Narayan Iyengar, **N. Ravikiran**.
- ☐ **Tanpura (tambura)**: four- to six-string drone lute, never melodic; the "sound carpet" behind all Indian classical music; Miraj (Maharashtra) sitars and tanpuras received a GI tag in 2024.
- ☐ **Rudra veena** (been) is a stick zither, see INS-09; **mohan veena** is a modified slide guitar (INS-07.3).
- ☐ **Rabab** (rubab): short-necked, skin-bellied lute of Afghanistan, Kashmir and Punjab; Bhai Mardana played the rabab for Guru Nanak.
- ☐ **Dotara** (dotar): two- or four-string folk lute of Bengal and Assam, of the Baul and Bhawaiya singers.
- ☐ **Tumbi**: single-string Punjabi lute with a high, bright twang; Lal Chand Yamla Jatt.
- ☐ **Sursingar, swarbat, kachapi veena**: rarer historical lutes of North and South India, now mostly in museums.

### ☐ INS-07.2 Lutes of Asia, the Middle East and Africa

- ☐ **Oud** (ud): short-necked, fretless, pear-shaped lute of the Arab world, Turkey and Persia; the word gave Europe "lute" (*al-ud*); Munir Bashir, Anouar Brahem.
- ☐ **Saz / bağlama** (Turkey), **tar** and **setar** (Iran, Azerbaijan), **tanbur** (Kurdish and Persian), **dutar** (Central Asia), **dombra** (Kazakhstan, the national instrument), **komuz** (Kyrgyzstan).
- ☐ **Pipa** (China): pear-shaped lute played upright, with rapid tremolo; Wu Man. **Biwa** (Japan) is its relative, used for epic tales.
- ☐ **Shamisen** (Japan): three-string, long-necked, skin-covered lute plucked with a large plectrum; kabuki and folk (Tsugaru-jamisen). Its ancestor is the Chinese **sanxian**.
- ☐ **Ngoni** (West Africa): small lute of the griots, possible ancestor of the banjo; Bassekou Kouyaté. **Gimbri / guembri** (Morocco): bass lute of Gnawa trance music.

### ☐ INS-07.3 Lutes of Europe and the Americas

- ☐ **Lute** (European): Renaissance favourite, with a bowl back; John Dowland's songs.
- ☐ **Classical guitar**: Spanish six-string; the modern body was set by **Antonio de Torres** (1850s–1880s); **Andrés Segovia** made it a concert instrument; Julian Bream, John Williams.
- ☐ **Flamenco guitar**: lighter, brighter; Paco de Lucía.
- ☐ **Steel-string acoustic guitar**: C. F. Martin & Co. (founded 1833 in New York, later Nazareth, Pennsylvania); folk, country, Bollywood strumming.
- ☐ **Slide and resonator guitars**: Hawaiian (lap) steel guitar, Dobro; in India, **Brij Bhushan Kabra** (Hindustani slide guitar) and **Vishwa Mohan Bhatt** (mohan veena; Grammy 1994 with Ry Cooder for *A Meeting by the River*).
- ☐ **Mandolin**: Italian, four double courses tuned like a violin; **U. Srinivas** ("Mandolin Srinivas", 1969–2014) made it a Carnatic instrument; bluegrass (Bill Monroe).
- ☐ **Banjo**: skin-headed lute from African ancestors (the akonting and others) brought by enslaved people to the Americas; bluegrass (Earl Scruggs), Béla Fleck. In India the "banjo" is the Japanese *taishōgoto* keyed zither (see INS-09).
- ☐ **Ukulele**: small four-string lute made in Hawaii from the Portuguese *machete* brought by Madeiran immigrants in 1879; easy first instrument.
- ☐ **Balalaika** (Russia, triangular), **bouzouki** (Greece), **Portuguese guitar** (fado), **charango** (Andes; small, ten strings, once made of armadillo shell), **cuatro** (Venezuela, Puerto Rico), **tres** (Cuba), **vihuela** and **guitarrón** (Mexican mariachi), **cavaquinho** (Portugal, Brazil).
- ☐ **Bass guitar and double bass**: see INS-08 (bowed) and INS-13 (electric).

## INS-08 Bowed strings

> **Before you start — INS-08.** A bow of horsehair coated with rosin grips and releases the string hundreds of times a second (a "stick-slip" motion), so the note can last and swell. Bowed instruments probably began in Central Asia around the 9th–10th century. Tata · H–S 321 or 31 with bowed suffix (-71).

### ☐ INS-08.1 Indian bowed instruments

- ☐ **Sarangi**: carved from a single block of wood (often tun), covered with goatskin, played with the fingernails pressing the strings from the side; about 35–40 sympathetic strings; the closest instrument to the human voice; **Pandit Ram Narayan** (1927–2024) made it a solo concert instrument; Sultan Khan, Kamal Sabri. Folk sarangis: the Sindhi *sindhi sarangi*, *gujratan* and *jogiya* sarangis of Rajasthan.
- ☐ **Kamaicha**: bowed lute of the Manganiyar community of western Rajasthan, with a large round skin-covered belly and deep, booming tone.
- ☐ **Ravanhatha**: one of the oldest bowed instruments of India; bamboo neck, coconut-shell body; played by the Bhopa singers of Rajasthan and Gujarat telling the tale of Pabuji; named in legend after Ravana.
- ☐ **Esraj and dilruba**: fretted bowed instruments with sympathetic strings; esraj in Bengal (Rabindra Sangeet, Santiniketan), dilruba in Punjab and Sikh kirtan; the **taus** (peacock-shaped) is said to have been designed for Guru Hargobind.
- ☐ **Violin in India**: adopted into Carnatic music around 1800, credited to **Baluswami Dikshitar**; played sitting, scroll resting on the foot; **Lalgudi Jayaraman**, M. S. Gopalakrishnan, **L. Subramaniam**, Kanyakumari; in Hindustani music **N. Rajam** and V. G. Jog.
- ☐ **Pena** (Manipur): coconut-shell bowed lute with a bamboo neck, played by Meitei bards.
- ☐ **Banam** (Santal and other Adivasi communities), **chikara** (Rajasthan, Uttarakhand), **sarinda** (Bengal, Assam, Punjab): folk fiddles.

### ☐ INS-08.2 The violin family (Europe)

- ☐ **Violin**: four strings, no frets; the oldest surviving violins come from **Andrea Amati** in Cremona (1550s–60s); peak of craft with **Antonio Stradivari** (c. 1644–1737) and **Giuseppe Guarneri "del Gesù"** (1698–1744); Paganini, Heifetz, Menuhin, Perlman, Mutter, Hilary Hahn.
- ☐ **Viola**: larger, a fifth lower, a darker voice.
- ☐ **Cello**: played between the knees; Pablo Casals (Bach suites), Jacqueline du Pré, Mstislav Rostropovich, Yo-Yo Ma.
- ☐ **Double bass**: lowest bowed string (from the viol family in shape); jazz plucks it (Charles Mingus, Ray Brown).
- ☐ **Viols (viola da gamba)**: fretted, older family played resting on the leg; revived by Jordi Savall.
- ☐ **Hardanger fiddle** (Norway, with sympathetic strings), **nyckelharpa** (Sweden, keyed fiddle), **gusle** (Balkans, epic singing), **rebec** and **vielle** (medieval), **hurdy-gurdy** (a wheel bows the strings, turned by a crank).

### ☐ INS-08.3 Bowed instruments of Asia, the Middle East and Africa

- ☐ **Erhu** (China): two strings with the bow trapped between them; snake-skin resonator; **Abing**'s *Erquan Yingyue* (Moon Reflected on the Second Spring).
- ☐ **Morin khuur** (Mongolia): horse-head fiddle; UNESCO Intangible Heritage (2003/2008); its legend tells of a horse turned into music.
- ☐ **Kamancheh** (Iran, Azerbaijan), **kemençe** (Turkey, Greece), **rebab** (Arab world, Java, Malaysia; ancestor of many fiddles), **haegeum** (Korea), **kokyū** (Japan), **sarinda** (Afghanistan).
- ☐ **Masenqo** (Ethiopia; one-string fiddle of the *azmari* singers), **imzad** (one-string fiddle played by Tuareg women; UNESCO 2013), **goje** (West Africa).

## INS-09 Zithers, harps and lyres

> **Before you start — INS-09.** In a zither the strings run across a board, box, tube or stick with no neck; in a harp they rise from the body at an angle; in a lyre they run from the body to a crossbar. Tata · H–S 31 (zithers), 322 (harps), 321.2 (lyres).

### ☐ INS-09.1 Zithers of India

- ☐ **Santoor**: hammered trapezoid zither of Kashmir with about 100 strings in groups; once used in Sufiana music; **Pandit Shivkumar Sharma** (1938–2022) made it a classical solo instrument; Bhajan Sopori, Rahul Sharma.
- ☐ **Rudra veena (been)**: stick zither with two large gourds and raised frets; the most sacred instrument of Hindustani music and the dhrupad style; Asad Ali Khan, Zia Mohiuddin Dagar, Bahauddin Dagar.
- ☐ **Vichitra veena**: fretless stick zither played with a glass slide; Lalmani Misra, Gopal Krishna.
- ☐ **Swarmandal**: plucked board zither used by singers for a shimmering backdrop (Bade Ghulam Ali Khan).
- ☐ **Bulbul tarang (Indian banjo)**: a keyed zither from the Japanese taishōgoto, popular in folk and qawwali; in Pakistan's Balochistan called *benju*.
- ☐ **Yazh**: ancient Tamil harp or lyre praised in Sangam literature; now lost, with modern reconstructions.
- ☐ **Kinnari, gettuvadyam and onavillu**: folk stick zithers and musical bows of the south (the kinnari of Telangana bards; the *onavillu* is struck during Onam at the Padmanabhaswamy temple, Kerala).

### ☐ INS-09.2 Zithers of the world

- ☐ **Guqin** (China): seven-string fretless zither, the instrument of scholars and Confucius; about 3,000 years old; UNESCO 2003/2008.
- ☐ **Guzheng** (China), **koto** (Japan, 13 strings; Michio Miyagi), **gayageum** (Korea, 12 strings; Hwang Byungki), **đàn tranh** (Vietnam): the plucked long zither family with movable bridges.
- ☐ **Đàn bầu** (Vietnam): one-string zither played by bending a flexible rod, giving gliding notes.
- ☐ **Qanun** (Arab world, Turkey): trapezoid plucked zither with small levers for microtones.
- ☐ **Santur** (Iran), **yangqin** (China), **cimbalom** (Hungary; Kodály, Liszt), **hammered dulcimer** (Britain, the Americas): the hammered zither family, cousins of the santoor.
- ☐ **Kantele** (Finland; the *Kalevala*'s magic instrument), **kanklės** (Lithuania), **gusli** (Russia), **psaltery** (medieval).
- ☐ **Concert zither** (Alpine), **autoharp** (USA folk), **Appalachian dulcimer** (fretted, played on the lap).
- ☐ **Valiha** (Madagascar): bamboo tube zither, strings cut from the bamboo skin or made of wire; national instrument.

### ☐ INS-09.3 Harps

- ☐ **Lyres and harps of Ur** (Iraq): found by **Leonard Woolley** in the Royal Cemetery about 1927–29, dated around 2500 BCE; the "Bull-headed Lyre" is among the oldest stringed instruments.
- ☐ **Ancient Indian vina (arched harp)**: the early "vina" was a bow-shaped harp, shown in Bharhut, Sanchi and Amaravati sculptures and on the gold coins of **Samudragupta** (4th century CE), who is shown playing it.
- ☐ **Saung-gauk** (Myanmar): boat-shaped arched harp, the national instrument, a living relative of the old Indian vina.
- ☐ **Concert pedal harp**: 47 strings and seven pedals; the double-action pedal was patented by **Sébastien Érard** (1810).
- ☐ **Celtic harp (cláirseach)**: Irish wire-strung harp; the "Brian Boru" harp at Trinity College Dublin (around the 14th–15th century) is Ireland's national symbol; Turlough O'Carolan.
- ☐ **Paraguayan and Andean harps**, **Welsh triple harp**, **angle harps** of ancient Egypt and Persia (chang).
- ☐ **Kora** (West Africa): 21-string harp-lute of the Mandinka griots, with a calabash body; **Toumani Diabaté** (1965–2024); Sona Jobarteh.

### ☐ INS-09.4 Lyres

- ☐ **Kithara and lyra** (ancient Greece): Apollo's instrument; the word "lyric" comes from it.
- ☐ **Krar and begena** (Ethiopia): bowl lyre and large box lyre for prayer.
- ☐ **Obokano** (Kenya), **simsimiyya** (Egypt, Yemen), **Anglo-Saxon lyre** (Sutton Hoo, 7th century).

## INS-10 Keyboard instruments

> **Before you start — INS-10.** A keyboard is a control system, not a sound source: keys can open organ pipes, pluck or strike strings, or switch circuits. So keyboards cross the classes: the organ is an aerophone, the piano a struck zither, the harmonium a free reed and the synthesizer an electrophone.

- ☐ **INS-10.1 Hydraulis**: water organ invented by **Ctesibius** of Alexandria (3rd century BCE); the first keyboard instrument.
- ☐ **INS-10.2 Pipe organ**: pipes blown by bellows, played from keyboards and pedals; church and cathedral music; J. S. Bach. UNESCO lists German organ craft and music (2017).
- ☐ **INS-10.3 Clavichord**: strings struck by small brass blades; quiet, expressive; favourite for home practice in the 18th century.
- ☐ **INS-10.4 Harpsichord**: strings plucked by quills; Baroque music; Domenico Scarlatti, François Couperin; Wanda Landowska revived it in the 20th century.
- ☐ **INS-10.5 Piano**: invented by **Bartolomeo Cristofori** in Florence around 1700 as the *gravicembalo col piano e forte* (harpsichord with soft and loud); three Cristofori pianos survive, the oldest (1720) at the Metropolitan Museum of Art. The cast-iron frame (1820s–40s), cross-stringing and **Steinway & Sons** (New York, 1853) made the modern grand. Upright, baby grand, concert grand (88 keys since about 1880).
- ☐ **INS-10.6 Celesta**: hammers strike steel plates; patented by **Auguste Mustel** (1886); the "Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy" (Tchaikovsky, 1892).
- ☐ **INS-10.7 Harmonium, accordion, melodica**: free-reed keyboards (see INS-05.4).
- ☐ **INS-10.8 Electric and electronic keyboards**: Hammond organ (1935), Fender Rhodes electric piano, Wurlitzer, Hohner Clavinet, Mellotron (tape playback, 1960s), synthesizers, digital pianos, and the home keyboards (Casio, Yamaha) that taught a generation in India. See INS-13.
- ☐ **INS-10.9 Keyboards in Indian music**: harmonium; the piano in Calcutta and Bombay homes and church music; **A. R. Rahman** and other composers working from keyboards and synths; the *swarmandal*-like use of synth pads.
