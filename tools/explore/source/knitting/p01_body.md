# PART 2 — TOOLS AND MATERIALS

## KNT-02 Needles and machines

> **Before you start — KNT-02.** Needle sizes are best given in **millimetres**. US sizes use numbers (US 8 = 5 mm); old UK and Indian sizes run backwards (UK 6 = 5 mm; Indian shops often still sell needles by old numbers such as 8, 10, 12). Always check the millimetres.

- ☐ **KNT-02.1 Straight needles**: pairs with a knob at the end; flat pieces; steel, aluminium, plastic, bamboo, wood.
- ☐ **KNT-02.2 Circular needles**: two tips joined by a cable; for knitting in the round and for heavy flat pieces.
- ☐ **KNT-02.3 Double-pointed needles (DPNs)**: sets of four or five, for socks, sleeves and hat tops.
- ☐ **KNT-02.4 Interchangeable needles**: tips that screw onto cables of different lengths.
- ☐ **KNT-02.5 Cable needles and stitch holders**: to hold stitches aside for cables and pockets.
- ☐ **KNT-02.6 Knitting looms and boards**: pegs instead of needles; easy for children.
- ☐ **KNT-02.7 Arm and finger knitting**: chunky loops made on the arms or fingers.
- ☐ **KNT-02.8 Knitting machines**: hand-operated flatbed machines (Usha, Brother), circular sock machines, industrial whole-garment machines (KNT-00.04).

## KNT-03 Yarns and notions

- ☐ **KNT-03.1 Yarn weights**: the Craft Yarn Council system 0 to 7 (CRO-03.1); in India, wool is often sold as "2-ply", "3-ply" and "4-ply".
- ☐ **KNT-03.2 Fibres**: wool (merino, Shetland, Indian Deccani and Himalayan wools), cashmere (*pashm* from Changthangi goats of Ladakh), angora, alpaca, mohair, cotton, linen, silk, acrylic and blends; superwash wool for machine washing.
- ☐ **KNT-03.3 Yarn construction**: plies, twist, woollen versus worsted spinning, hand-dyed and variegated yarns.
- ☐ **KNT-03.4 Notions**: stitch markers, row counter, tapestry needle, scissors, tape measure, blocking pins and mats, needle gauge, point protectors.

# PART 3 — STITCHES, TECHNIQUES AND HOLDING STYLES

## KNT-04 Cast-ons, basic stitches and fabrics

- ☐ **KNT-04.1 Long-tail cast-on**: the all-rounder; stretchy and neat.
- ☐ **KNT-04.2 Cable cast-on**: firm edge, good for adding stitches mid-work.
- ☐ **KNT-04.3 Provisional cast-on**: temporary, removed later to knit the other way.
- ☐ **KNT-04.4 Tubular cast-on**: invisible, elastic edge for ribbing.
- ☐ **KNT-04.5 Knit and purl**: KNT-00.01.
- ☐ **KNT-04.6 Stockinette (stocking stitch)**: smooth; curls at the edges.
- ☐ **KNT-04.7 Garter stitch**: ridged, flat, does not curl; ideal first fabric.
- ☐ **KNT-04.8 Ribbing**: knit and purl columns (1×1, 2×2); stretchy cuffs and collars.
- ☐ **KNT-04.9 Seed (moss) stitch**: alternating knit and purl, shifted each row; bumpy texture.

## KNT-05 Shaping, patterns and colourwork

- ☐ **KNT-05.1 Increases**: knit front and back (kfb), make one (M1L, M1R), yarn over (adds a decorative hole).
- ☐ **KNT-05.2 Decreases**: knit two together (k2tog, leans right), slip-slip-knit (ssk, leans left), central double decreases.
- ☐ **KNT-05.3 Cables**: crossing groups of stitches using a cable needle; the heart of Aran knitting.
- ☐ **KNT-05.4 Lace**: patterns of yarn-overs and decreases; Shetland and Estonian lace.
- ☐ **KNT-05.5 Fair Isle and stranded colourwork**: two colours per row, the unused one carried behind as a "float".
- ☐ **KNT-05.6 Intarsia**: separate yarns for blocks of colour; no floats.
- ☐ **KNT-05.7 Double knitting**: two layers at once, reversible colours.
- ☐ **KNT-05.8 Mosaic (slip-stitch) knitting**: one colour per row, patterns made by slipped stitches (Barbara Walker popularised it).
- ☐ **KNT-05.9 Brioche**: tucked stitches that make a lofty, reversible rib; two-colour brioche is a modern favourite.
- ☐ **KNT-05.10 Short rows**: turning before the end of a row to shape heels, shoulders and curves (wrap and turn, German short rows).
- ☐ **KNT-05.11 Entrelac**: a basketweave of small knitted squares.

## KNT-06 Finishing and reading patterns

- ☐ **KNT-06.1 Bind-offs (cast-offs)**: standard, stretchy (Jeny's surprisingly stretchy bind-off), three-needle bind-off for seams.
- ☐ **KNT-06.2 Seaming**: mattress stitch, Kitchener stitch (grafting) for invisible sock toes.
- ☐ **KNT-06.3 Picking up stitches**: for necklines and button bands.
- ☐ **KNT-06.4 Blocking**: wet, steam or spray blocking to even stitches and open lace.
- ☐ **KNT-06.5 Gauge (tension)**: stitches and rows per 10 cm; swatch first.
- ☐ **KNT-06.6 Reading patterns and charts**: abbreviations, repeats, sizes in brackets; charts read right to left on right-side rows; Japanese charts use standard symbols.
- ☐ **KNT-06.7 Fixing mistakes**: tinking (unknitting stitch by stitch), dropping down a column to fix one stitch, lifelines (a thread through a safe row), frogging.

## KNT-07 Ways of holding the yarn

- ☐ **KNT-07.1 English (throwing)**: yarn in the right hand, wrapped around the needle; common in the UK, USA and much of India.
- ☐ **KNT-07.2 Continental (picking)**: yarn in the left hand, picked with the needle; often faster; common in Germany and Northern Europe.
- ☐ **KNT-07.3 Portuguese (around-the-neck)**: yarn tensioned around the neck or a pin and flicked with the thumb; easy purling; also used in Peru, Turkey, Greece and Egypt.
- ☐ **KNT-07.4 Combination knitting**: Continental knits with "Eastern" purls; Annie Modesitt teaches it.
- ☐ **KNT-07.5 Knitting belts and sheaths**: Shetland and Fair Isle knitters used a stuffed leather belt to hold one needle, for speed.

# PART 4 — TRADITIONS OF THE WORLD AND INDIA

## KNT-08 Traditions of the world

- ☐ **KNT-08.1 Fair Isle (Scotland)**: small, colourful stranded patterns, often only two colours per row; made famous when the Prince of Wales wore a Fair Isle pullover in the 1920s.
- ☐ **KNT-08.2 Shetland lace**: shawls so fine they could pass through a wedding ring ("wedding ring shawls"); spun and knitted in Unst and Lerwick.
- ☐ **KNT-08.3 Aran (Ireland)**: cables, honeycomb and diamonds on cream wool (KNT-00.03).
- ☐ **KNT-08.4 Gansey (British and Channel Islands)**: fishermen's working sweaters; Guernsey and Jersey gave their names to garments.
- ☐ **KNT-08.5 Norwegian Selbu**: the eight-petal "Selbu rose" mittens, popularised by Marit Gulsetbrua Emstad (1857).
- ☐ **KNT-08.6 Icelandic *lopapeysa***: round-yoke sweaters of unspun lopi wool, 20th century.
- ☐ **KNT-08.7 Sanquhar (Scotland)**: two-colour gloves with checked patterns named after local families or places.
- ☐ **KNT-08.8 Estonian lace**: Haapsalu shawls with *nupps* (bobbles).
- ☐ **KNT-08.9 Andean *ch'ullu***: colourful hats with ear flaps; in Taquile, Peru, men knit, and the craft is on UNESCO's intangible heritage list (Taquile textile art, 2008).
- ☐ **KNT-08.10 Turkish and Balkan socks**: richly patterned socks knitted toe-up with symbolic motifs.
- ☐ **KNT-08.11 Japanese knitting**: precise chart-based pattern books (Nihon Vogue), Hitomi Shida's stitch dictionaries.
- ☐ **KNT-08.12 Cowichan (Canada)**: sweaters of the Coast Salish peoples, knitted in thick undyed wool with bold motifs.

## KNT-09 Knitting in India

- ☐ **KNT-09.1 Indian winter knitting culture**: from the mid-20th century, hand-knitted sweaters, monkey caps, mufflers and socks made by mothers and grandmothers became a symbol of care; pattern sharing between neighbours and in women's magazines.
- ☐ **KNT-09.2 Kashmir and the Himalaya**: hand-knitted woollens of Himachal, Uttarakhand, Ladakh and Sikkim; Kullu caps and shawls are woven, not knitted, but sit beside knitted socks and gloves in hill markets; pashmina yarn.
- ☐ **KNT-09.3 Tibetan refugee knitting**: sweaters and caps sold by Tibetan communities in Indian winter markets.
- ☐ **KNT-09.4 Indian brands and yarns**: Oswal, Vardhman and other wool brands; the Ludhiana hosiery and knitwear industry; Tiruppur's cotton knitwear cluster (exports worldwide).
- ☐ **KNT-09.5 Knitting in schools and institutions**: needlework once taught in Indian girls' schools and Home Science; now open to all.
- ☐ **KNT-09.6 The machine-knitting home business**: Usha and other hand-operated knitting machines that let women run small home businesses.

# PART 6 — HISTORY, PEOPLE AND COMMUNITY

## KNT-11 The history of knitting

| Year | Event | Where | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3rd to 5th centuries CE | Nalbinding socks with split toes | Egypt | Mistaken for knitting; an ancestor |
| c. 11th century | Earliest true knitted cotton socks | Egypt | Oldest known knitting |
| 13th century | Knitted silk cushions in the Las Huelgas tombs | Burgos, Spain | Knitting in Europe |
| c. 1400 | "Knitting Madonna" paintings | Germany, Italy | Knitting in art |
| 1527 | Knitting guild in Paris (one of the early guilds) | France | Professional knitting |
| 1589 | William Lee's stocking frame | England | Machine knitting begins |
| 1600s | Hand-knitted stockings industry in the Yorkshire Dales and elsewhere | England | Knitting as rural income |
| 1811 to 1816 | Luddite protests by frame-work knitters | Nottingham | Workers and automation |
| 1847 | Latch needle (Matthew Townsend) | England | Faster machines |
| 1914 to 1918, 1939 to 1945 | Wartime knitting for soldiers ("Knit for Victory") | Britain, USA, Canada, Australia, India | Civilian war effort |
| 1950s to 1970s | Home knitting peak; Indian winter-sweater culture | Worldwide, India | Knitting at home |
| 1980s | Decline as cheap machine knitwear spreads | Worldwide | Fewer home knitters |
| 2005 | First World Wide Knit in Public Day | Worldwide | Knitting as public, social activity |
| 2007 | Ravelry opens | USA | Online knitting community |
| 2020s | Pandemic knitting boom; celebrity knitters | Worldwide | Young knitters return |

## KNT-12 Designers, teachers and community

- ☐ **KNT-12.1 Elizabeth Zimmermann (1910 to 1999)**: *Knitting Without Tears* (1971), *Knitter's Almanac*; the Baby Surprise Jacket; "knit on, with confidence and hope, through all crises".
- ☐ **KNT-12.2 Kaffe Fassett (born 1937)**: designer of bold colour knitting and patchwork; first living textile artist given a solo show at the V&A (1988).
- ☐ **KNT-12.3 Barbara G. Walker**: the *Treasury of Knitting Patterns* series (from 1968), the stitch dictionaries every designer uses.
- ☐ **KNT-12.4 Alice Starmore**: Fair Isle and Aran research and design.
- ☐ **KNT-12.5 Modern designers**: Kate Davies, Stephen West, Andrea Mowry, Norah Gaughan, Jared Flood (Brooklyn Tweed), Hitomi Shida, Arne & Carlos.
- ☐ **KNT-12.6 Knitters in public life**: Olympic diver Tom Daley (knitting in the stands at Tokyo 2020); the Pussyhat Project (2017).
- ☐ **KNT-12.7 Indian knitters**: generations of home knitters, whose patterns lived in notebooks; Indian knitting teachers and designers online who credit patterns.
- ☐ **KNT-12.8 Guilds and events**: The Knitting and Crochet Guild (UK, 1978), The Knitting Guild Association (USA), Edinburgh Yarn Festival, Shetland Wool Week (2010), Rhinebeck Sheep and Wool Festival, World Wide Knit in Public Day (second Saturday in June; begun 2005).
- ☐ **KNT-12.9 Records**: speed-knitting and largest-knit records are listed by Guinness World Records; check for current holders.

# PART 7 — IDEAS: PHILOSOPHY, PSYCHOLOGY AND SOCIETY

## KNT-13 Knitting and the mind

- ☐ **KNT-13.1 Mental health**: a survey of 3,545 knitters (Riley, Corkhill and Morris, *British Journal of Occupational Therapy*, 2013) found that most felt calmer and happier after knitting; Betsan Corkhill's "Stitchlinks" work on therapeutic knitting.
- ☐ **KNT-13.2 Knitting and the ageing brain**: a Mayo Clinic study (Geda and colleagues, 2011) linked crafting in later life with lower odds of mild cognitive impairment; association, not proof.
- ☐ **KNT-13.3 Knitting as code**: KNT-00.05; patterns, binary and algorithms.

## KNT-14 Knitting and society

- ☐ **KNT-14.1 Craftivism**: craft as gentle activism; Betsy Greer coined the word (2003); yarn bombing; the Pussyhat Project.
- ☐ **KNT-14.2 Women's history and unpaid work**: knitting for families and soldiers, rarely counted as work; Rozsika Parker's ideas (CRO-12.1).
- ☐ **KNT-14.3 Sustainability**: long-lasting, repairable garments; mending and darning; natural and recycled fibres; mulesing-free and responsibly sourced wool.
- ☐ **KNT-14.4 Knitting and men**: men knitted professionally in guilds, at sea and in the Andes; knitting is for everyone.

# PART 8 — THE PRACTICAL GUIDE

## KNT-15 First projects, fixing, care and buying

- ☐ **KNT-15.1 The first projects in order**: dishcloth, garter scarf, ribbed muffler, beanie in the round, mittens, baby cardigan, socks, a first sweater.
- ☐ **KNT-15.2 Fixing common mistakes**: dropped stitches (crochet hook up the ladder), extra stitches (count each row), twisted stitches, holes from accidental yarn-overs, uneven tension (practise and block).
- ☐ **KNT-15.3 Care**: hand-wash wool in cool water with wool wash; never wring; dry flat in shade; fold, don't hang; store with neem or cedar against moths; de-pill with a sweater comb.
- ☐ **KNT-15.4 Buying yarn ethically**: read fibre content; ask about animal welfare (mulesing), fair wages and dyes; buy from local spinners and cooperatives; reuse yarn by unravelling old sweaters.
- ☐ **KNT-15.5 Selling handmade**: price for time and materials; respect pattern licences; label fibres honestly.
- ☐ **KNT-15.6 Safety and comfort**: breaks for hands and wrists; good light; keep needles away from small children.
