## BOK-02 Literatures of Asia

> **Before you start — BOK-02.** Asia holds the oldest written literatures after Mesopotamia and Egypt, and several of the world's great classical traditions: Chinese, Persian, Arabic, Japanese. Many share forms across borders: the ghazal travelled from Arabic to Persian, Urdu and now English; the Ramayana travelled across South-East Asia. **See also:** BOK-01 India, BOK-06 Poetry forms, MOV-04 Asian cinema.

### ☐ BOK-02.1 Chinese literature

- ☐ **Classics**: the *Shijing* (Classic of Poetry), Confucius's *Analects*, the *Daodejing* (attributed to Laozi), *Zhuangzi*, Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* (about 94 BCE).
- ☐ **Tang poetry** (618–907): Li Bai, Du Fu, Wang Wei, Bai Juyi, the golden age of Chinese verse; Song ci lyrics (Su Shi, Li Qingzhao).
- ☐ **The Four Great Classical Novels**: *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, *Water Margin*, *Journey to the West* (Wu Cheng'en; the Monkey King, based on the monk Xuanzang's real journey to India) and *Dream of the Red Chamber* (Cao Xueqin, 18th century).
- ☐ **Modern**: Lu Xun ("A Madman's Diary", 1918; *The True Story of Ah Q*), Lao She, Ba Jin, Eileen Chang, Mo Yan (Nobel 2012), Gao Xingjian (Nobel 2000, French citizen), Yu Hua, Can Xue, Liu Cixin (*The Three-Body Problem*; Hugo Award 2015), Jin Yong (wuxia novels).

### ☐ BOK-02.2 Japanese literature

- ☐ **Classical**: the *Kojiki* (712) and *Man'yōshū* poetry anthology (8th century); Murasaki Shikibu's *The Tale of Genji* (about 1010); Sei Shōnagon's *The Pillow Book*; *The Tale of the Heike*.
- ☐ **Forms**: waka and tanka, renga, haiku (Matsuo Bashō, Buson, Issa, Shiki), noh and kabuki drama, Chikamatsu's puppet plays.
- ☐ **Modern**: Natsume Sōseki (*Kokoro*), Ryūnosuke Akutagawa ("Rashōmon"), Yasunari Kawabata (Nobel 1968), Yukio Mishima, Kenzaburō Ōe (Nobel 1994), Haruki Murakami, Banana Yoshimoto, Yoko Ogawa, Sayaka Murata, Kazuo Ishiguro (British, born in Japan; Nobel 2017).
- ☐ **Manga and light novels**: see BOK-10.9.

### ☐ BOK-02.3 Korean literature

- ☐ **Classical**: hyangga and sijo poetry; the *Samguk yusa*; *The Tale of Chunhyang* (pansori story); the Hangul alphabet created under King Sejong (promulgated 1446) made literacy easy.
- ☐ **Modern**: Yi Sang, Pak Kyongni (*Land*), Ko Un, Shin Kyung-sook (*Please Look After Mom*), Han Kang (*The Vegetarian*, International Booker 2016 with translator Deborah Smith; Nobel Prize 2024, the first Korean and first Asian woman laureate), Cho Nam-joo (*Kim Jiyoung, Born 1982*), webtoons.

### ☐ BOK-02.4 Persian literature

- ☐ **Epic**: Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh* (completed 1010), the national epic of Iran, about 50,000 couplets.
- ☐ **Mystical and lyric masters**: Rumi (*Masnavi*), Hafez (ghazals; consulted for omens in Iranian homes), Saadi (*Gulistan*, *Bustan*), Attar (*The Conference of the Birds*), Omar Khayyam (*Rubaiyat*, made famous in English by Edward FitzGerald, 1859), Nizami (*Layla and Majnun*).
- ☐ **India's Persian**: Amir Khusrau, Bedil, Ghalib's Persian works; Persian was a court language of India for centuries.
- ☐ **Modern**: Sadegh Hedayat (*The Blind Owl*), Forugh Farrokhzad, Simin Daneshvar, Shahrnush Parsipur.

### ☐ BOK-02.5 Arabic literature

- ☐ **Pre-Islamic poetry**: the *Mu'allaqat* (the "hanging odes"), qasida form.
- ☐ **The Qur'an** (7th century) as the supreme model of Arabic prose and rhyme (see BOK-11).
- ☐ **Classical**: al-Mutanabbi, Abu Nuwas, al-Jahiz; the *Maqamat* of al-Hariri; *One Thousand and One Nights* (stories gathered from Indian, Persian and Arab sources; Scheherazade); Ibn Battuta's travels; Ibn Khaldun's *Muqaddimah* (1377).
- ☐ **Andalusia**: Ibn Hazm (*The Ring of the Dove*), Ibn Arabi, Ibn Rushd.
- ☐ **Modern (Nahda and after)**: Naguib Mahfouz (Cairo Trilogy; Nobel 1988), Taha Hussein, Mahmoud Darwish, Nizar Qabbani, Adonis, Tayeb Salih (*Season of Migration to the North*), Ghassan Kanafani, Jokha Alharthi (*Celestial Bodies*, International Booker 2019).

### ☐ BOK-02.6 Turkish and Central Asian literature

- ☐ **Turkic epics**: the *Book of Dede Korkut*; *Manas* (Kyrgyz, one of the longest epics); Yunus Emre's Sufi poems.
- ☐ **Ottoman divan poetry**: Fuzuli, Baki, Nedim.
- ☐ **Modern Turkish**: Nazım Hikmet, Yaşar Kemal (*Memed, My Hawk*), Orhan Pamuk (Nobel 2006; *My Name Is Red*), Elif Şafak.
- ☐ **Central Asia**: Alisher Navoi (Chagatai), Chinghiz Aitmatov (Kyrgyz).

### ☐ BOK-02.7 South and South-East Asian neighbours

- ☐ **Pakistan**: Faiz, Manto, Intizar Husain, Bapsi Sidhwa, Mohammed Hanif, Kamila Shamsie (Women's Prize 2018).
- ☐ **Bangladesh**: Kazi Nazrul Islam (national poet), Jasimuddin, Humayun Ahmed, Selina Hossain.
- ☐ **Sri Lanka**: Sinhala classics; Martin Wickramasinghe; Michael Ondaatje (*The English Patient*, Booker 1992), Shehan Karunatilaka (Booker 2022).
- ☐ **Nepal**: Bhanubhakta, Devkota, Parijat.
- ☐ **Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos**: Ramayana retellings (Thai *Ramakien*, Khmer *Reamker*), Thai poet Sunthorn Phu.
- ☐ **Indonesia and Malaysia**: wayang stories, *Hikayat*, Pramoedya Ananta Toer (*Buru Quartet*), Chairil Anwar, Eka Kurniawan.
- ☐ **Vietnam**: Nguyễn Du's *The Tale of Kiều* (national poem), Bảo Ninh (*The Sorrow of War*).
- ☐ **Philippines**: José Rizal (*Noli Me Tángere*, 1887), Nick Joaquín, F. Sionil José.

### ☐ BOK-02.8 Hebrew and Jewish literatures

- ☐ The Hebrew Bible (Tanakh; see BOK-11), Talmud, medieval poets (Yehuda Halevi), Yiddish literature (Sholem Aleichem, Isaac Bashevis Singer, Nobel 1978), modern Hebrew (S. Y. Agnon, Nobel 1966; Amos Oz; David Grossman, International Booker 2017).

## BOK-03 Literatures of Europe

> **Before you start — BOK-03.** Europe's literatures grew from Greek and Latin roots, the Bible, medieval epics and the printing press. National literatures rose with national languages from the 1300s. **See also:** BOK-13 Literary movements, MOV-03 European cinema.

### ☐ BOK-03.1 Ancient Greek and Latin

- ☐ **Greek**: Homer (*Iliad*, *Odyssey*), Hesiod, Sappho (lyric), Aesop (fables), the tragedians Aeschylus, Sophocles (*Oedipus the King*) and Euripides, the comic Aristophanes, historians Herodotus and Thucydides, philosophers Plato and Aristotle (*Poetics*).
- ☐ **Latin**: Virgil (*Aeneid*), Ovid (*Metamorphoses*), Horace, Catullus, Cicero, Seneca, Augustine (*Confessions*).

### ☐ BOK-03.2 English literature (Britain and Ireland)

- ☐ **Old and Middle English**: *Beowulf*, Chaucer (*The Canterbury Tales*, about 1387–1400), *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*.
- ☐ **Renaissance**: Shakespeare (37 to 39 plays and 154 sonnets), Marlowe, Spenser, Donne, the King James Bible (1611), Milton (*Paradise Lost*, 1667).
- ☐ **18th century and the rise of the novel**: Defoe (*Robinson Crusoe*, 1719), Swift (*Gulliver's Travels*), Richardson, Fielding, Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755).
- ☐ **Romantics**: Blake, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron, Shelley, Keats, Mary Shelley (*Frankenstein*, 1818).
- ☐ **Victorians**: Jane Austen (just before, 1811–1817), the Brontës, Dickens, George Eliot (*Middlemarch*), Thomas Hardy, Lewis Carroll, Tennyson, Browning.
- ☐ **Modernists and after**: Joseph Conrad, Virginia Woolf, James Joyce (*Ulysses*, 1922), T. S. Eliot, W. B. Yeats (Nobel 1923), George Orwell, Samuel Beckett (Nobel 1969), Doris Lessing, Seamus Heaney, Hilary Mantel, Zadie Smith, Sally Rooney.

### ☐ BOK-03.3 French literature

- ☐ *The Song of Roland*; Rabelais; Montaigne (invented the essay, 1580); Molière, Racine, Corneille; Voltaire (*Candide*), Rousseau; Victor Hugo (*Les Misérables*), Balzac, Flaubert (*Madame Bovary*), Zola, Baudelaire (*Les Fleurs du mal*), Rimbaud; Proust (*In Search of Lost Time*), Colette; Sartre, Camus (*The Stranger*), Simone de Beauvoir; the nouveau roman; Annie Ernaux (Nobel 2022); Francophone writers from Africa, the Caribbean and Quebec.

### ☐ BOK-03.4 German-language literature

- ☐ *Nibelungenlied*; Luther's Bible; Goethe (*Faust*), Schiller; the Brothers Grimm (folk tales, 1812); Heine; Thomas Mann (*Buddenbrooks*; Nobel 1929), Rilke, Kafka (*The Metamorphosis*), Hermann Hesse (*Siddhartha*, 1922; Nobel 1946), Brecht, Günter Grass, W. G. Sebald, Herta Müller, Jenny Erpenbeck (International Booker 2024).

### ☐ BOK-03.5 Russian literature

- ☐ Pushkin (*Eugene Onegin*), Gogol, Lermontov; the great novelists Tolstoy (*War and Peace*, *Anna Karenina*) and Dostoevsky (*Crime and Punishment*, *The Brothers Karamazov*); Turgenev; Chekhov (stories and plays); Gorky; Akhmatova, Tsvetaeva, Mandelstam, Pasternak (*Doctor Zhivago*; Nobel 1958), Bulgakov (*The Master and Margarita*), Solzhenitsyn (Nobel 1970), Nabokov.
- ☐ **Tolstoy and India**: Tolstoy's *A Letter to a Hindu* (1908) and letters with Gandhi influenced non-violent resistance.

### ☐ BOK-03.6 Spanish, Portuguese and Italian literatures

- ☐ **Spain**: *El Cantar de mio Cid*, Cervantes (*Don Quixote*), Lope de Vega, Calderón, Lorca, the Generation of '27, Carmen Laforet, Javier Marías.
- ☐ **Portugal**: Camões (*The Lusiads*, 1572, about Vasco da Gama's voyage to India), Fernando Pessoa (his heteronyms), José Saramago (Nobel 1998).
- ☐ **Italy**: Dante (*Divine Comedy*, early 1300s), Petrarch (sonnet), Boccaccio (*Decameron*), Ariosto, Machiavelli; Manzoni (*The Betrothed*); Pirandello, Italo Calvino, Primo Levi, Umberto Eco (*The Name of the Rose*), Elena Ferrante.

### ☐ BOK-03.7 Nordic, Central and Eastern European literatures

- ☐ **Icelandic sagas** and the *Eddas* (13th century); Ibsen and Strindberg (drama); Hans Christian Andersen (fairy tales); Selma Lagerlöf (first woman Nobel in literature, 1909); Knut Hamsun; Astrid Lindgren (*Pippi Longstocking*); Tove Jansson (Moomins); Jon Fosse (Nobel 2023); Nordic noir crime.
- ☐ **Central and Eastern Europe**: Kafka and Hašek (Czech), Milan Kundera, Wisława Szymborska and Olga Tokarczuk (Polish Nobels 1996 and 2018), Ivo Andrić (Yugoslav Nobel 1961), Imre Kertész and László Krasznahorkai (Hungarian Nobels 2002 and 2025), Georgi Gospodinov (Bulgaria, International Booker 2023), Taras Shevchenko and Lesya Ukrainka (Ukraine).

## BOK-04 Literatures of the Americas

> **Before you start — BOK-04.** The Americas combine Indigenous oral and written traditions (the Maya *Popol Vuh*), the colonial languages (English, Spanish, Portuguese, French) and African diaspora traditions. **See also:** MOV-02 Cinemas of the Americas.

### ☐ BOK-04.1 Indigenous literatures

- ☐ The K'iche' Maya *Popol Vuh* (written down in the 16th century), Aztec (Nahuatl) poetry of Nezahualcóyotl, Inca oral traditions, North American Native storytelling; modern writers N. Scott Momaday (Pulitzer 1969), Louise Erdrich (Pulitzer 2021), Joy Harjo (US Poet Laureate 2019–2022), Tommy Orange.

### ☐ BOK-04.2 Literature of the United States

- ☐ **19th century**: Washington Irving, Poe (inventor of the detective story, "The Murders in the Rue Morgue", 1841), Hawthorne, Melville (*Moby-Dick*, 1851), Emerson and Thoreau (*Walden*; influenced Gandhi's civil disobedience), Walt Whitman (*Leaves of Grass*), Emily Dickinson, Harriet Beecher Stowe, Mark Twain (*Huckleberry Finn*), Frederick Douglass's narrative.
- ☐ **20th century**: Henry James, Edith Wharton, Jack London, F. Scott Fitzgerald (*The Great Gatsby*), Hemingway, Faulkner, John Steinbeck, the Harlem Renaissance (Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston), Richard Wright, Ralph Ellison, James Baldwin, Harper Lee, the Beats, Sylvia Plath, Kurt Vonnegut, Philip Roth, Toni Morrison (Nobel 1993), Maya Angelou, Cormac McCarthy, Bob Dylan (Nobel 2016, as songwriter), Louise Glück (Nobel 2020).
- ☐ **Today**: Colson Whitehead, Jhumpa Lahiri, Ocean Vuong, Percival Everett (*James*, Pulitzer 2025), Daniel Kraus (*Angel Down*, Pulitzer 2026).

### ☐ BOK-04.3 Canada

- ☐ L. M. Montgomery (*Anne of Green Gables*), Margaret Atwood (*The Handmaid's Tale*; Booker 2000 and 2019), Alice Munro (Nobel 2013), Michael Ondaatje, Rohinton Mistry and M. G. Vassanji (Indian and South Asian diaspora), Yann Martel (*Life of Pi*, Booker 2002, set in Pondicherry), Anne Carson (poet, classicist; Nobel Prize 2026), French-Canadian (Québécois) literature.

### ☐ BOK-04.4 Latin America

- ☐ **Colonial and early**: Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz (Mexico, 17th century).
- ☐ **Modernismo**: Rubén Darío (Nicaragua), José Martí (Cuba).
- ☐ **The great poets**: Gabriela Mistral (Chile; first Latin American Nobel, 1945), Pablo Neruda (Chile, Nobel 1971), César Vallejo (Peru), Octavio Paz (Mexico, Nobel 1990; ambassador to India 1962–1968, wrote *In Light of India*).
- ☐ **The Boom (1960s–70s)**: Gabriel García Márquez (Colombia; *One Hundred Years of Solitude*; Nobel 1982), Julio Cortázar, Carlos Fuentes, Mario Vargas Llosa (Peru, Nobel 2010).
- ☐ **Also**: Jorge Luis Borges (Argentina, *Ficciones*), Juan Rulfo (*Pedro Páramo*), Isabel Allende, Roberto Bolaño, Mariana Enríquez.
- ☐ **Brazil (Portuguese)**: Machado de Assis, Jorge Amado, Clarice Lispector, Paulo Coelho (*The Alchemist*, among the most translated books by a living author).

### ☐ BOK-04.5 The Caribbean

- ☐ Aimé Césaire and the Négritude movement (Martinique), Derek Walcott (St Lucia, Nobel 1992; *Omeros*), V. S. Naipaul (Trinidad, of Indian descent; Nobel 2001; *A House for Mr Biswas*), Jean Rhys (*Wide Sargasso Sea*), Jamaica Kincaid, Marlon James (Jamaica; Booker 2015), Edwidge Danticat (Haiti), Indo-Caribbean writing of the girmitiya descendants.

## BOK-05 Literatures of Africa and Oceania

> **Before you start — BOK-05.** Africa has the world's richest living oral literatures and some of the oldest written ones (Egyptian, Ge'ez in Ethiopia, Swahili in Arabic script, the Timbuktu manuscripts). Modern African literature is written in English, French, Portuguese, Arabic and African languages (Yoruba, Gikuyu, Swahili, Zulu, Amharic). Oceania includes Aboriginal Australian, Māori and Pacific traditions. **See also:** MOV-05 Africa and Oceania.

### ☐ BOK-05.1 Ancient Egypt and Ethiopia

- ☐ Egyptian *Book of the Dead*, *The Tale of Sinuhe*, love songs; the Ethiopian *Kebra Nagast* (14th century, Ge'ez).

### ☐ BOK-05.2 West and Central Africa

- ☐ **Oral epics**: *Sundiata* (Mali), the griot tradition, Yoruba ifá poetry.
- ☐ **Timbuktu manuscripts**: hundreds of thousands of Arabic and Ajami manuscripts on law, astronomy and poetry.
- ☐ **Modern**: Chinua Achebe (*Things Fall Apart*, 1958), Wole Soyinka (Nobel 1986, first African laureate), Amos Tutuola, Buchi Emecheta, Ben Okri (Booker 1991), Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, Ama Ata Aidoo (Ghana), Léopold Sédar Senghor and Mariama Bâ (Senegal), Ahmadou Kourouma.

### ☐ BOK-05.3 East and Southern Africa

- ☐ Swahili poetry (utenzi), Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o (who switched from English to Gikuyu; *Decolonising the Mind*, 1986), Abdulrazak Gurnah (Tanzania/UK, Nobel 2021), Nadine Gordimer (South Africa, Nobel 1991), J. M. Coetzee (Booker 1983 and 1999; Nobel 2003), Damon Galgut (Booker 2021), Alan Paton (*Cry, the Beloved Country*), Zakes Mda; Gandhi's *Indian Opinion* newspaper and *Hind Swaraj* (1909) were written in South Africa.

### ☐ BOK-05.4 North Africa and the Maghreb

- ☐ Ibn Khaldun, Naguib Mahfouz (see BOK-02.5), Assia Djebar, Kateb Yacine, Tahar Ben Jelloun, Kamel Daoud (Prix Goncourt 2024).

### ☐ BOK-05.5 Australia, New Zealand and the Pacific

- ☐ Aboriginal songlines (the world's oldest continuous storytelling cultures), Māori whakapapa and oral literature; Patrick White (Nobel 1973), Peter Carey and Richard Flanagan (Booker), Kate Grenville, Les Murray, Alexis Wright (Waanyi; Miles Franklin 2007 and 2024), Katherine Mansfield, Janet Frame, Keri Hulme (*The Bone People*, Booker 1985), Witi Ihimaera (*The Whale Rider*), Albert Wendt (Samoa).

### ☐ BOK-05.6 How the registers grow

The landmark registers above are the trunk. On the website each literature gets year-wise lists, built in this order:

1. **Award lists first** (Sahitya Akademi, Jnanpith, Nobel, Booker and the national awards in Part 5), because they are official and dated.
2. **National bibliographies and library catalogues** (National Library of India, Library of Congress, British Library, WorldCat) for complete publication data.
3. **Canon lists with stated sources** (school and university syllabi, Sahitya Akademi's *Encyclopaedia of Indian Literature*, Norton and Penguin anthologies), always named.
4. **Readers' suggestions**, checked against a source before they are added.

Each entry keeps the same fields as the registers, so lists can later move into a Google Sheet, like the Curriculum Index.
