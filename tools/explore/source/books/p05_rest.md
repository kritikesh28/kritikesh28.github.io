# PART 5 — THE CRAFT AND THE BUSINESS OF BOOKS

> **About this Part.** How a book comes into being: written, edited, designed, printed, translated, sold, kept in libraries and protected by law. This is the "behind the scenes" of literature.

## BOK-18 The craft of writing

> **Before you start — BOK-18.** Every book is built from a few basic tools: story, character, point of view, voice, structure and style. Knowing them makes you a sharper reader and a better writer. **See also:** MOV-15 (screenwriting), BOK-20 Theory.

### ☐ BOK-18.1 Elements of a story

- ☐ **Plot**: the order of events; Aristotle's beginning, middle and end; Freytag's pyramid (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution).
- ☐ **Character**: round and flat (E. M. Forster, *Aspects of the Novel*, 1927), protagonist and antagonist, the character arc.
- ☐ **Setting**: time and place; Malgudi, Macondo and Middle-earth as places readers "visit".
- ☐ **Point of view**: first person ("I"), third person limited or omniscient, second person ("you"), unreliable narrator.
- ☐ **Theme**: the big idea underneath; love, power, death, freedom, identity.
- ☐ **Conflict**: person against person, nature, society, self or fate.
- ☐ **Structure patterns**: the hero's journey (Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, 1949), three-act structure, frame tales, non-linear stories.

### ☐ BOK-18.2 Language and style

- ☐ **Figures of speech (alankara)**: simile (upama), metaphor (rupaka), personification, hyperbole (atishayokti), alliteration (anuprasa), pun (shlesha), irony.
- ☐ **Imagery and symbol**; **tone and mood**; **diction** (word choice); **rhythm** in prose.
- ☐ **Dialogue**: how people talk on the page; dialect writing (Renu, Twain).
- ☐ **Show, don't tell**: let readers infer feelings from action and detail.

### ☐ BOK-18.3 The writing life

- ☐ **Drafting and revising**: most books go through many drafts; famous writers' routines (Premchand wrote daily; Hemingway stopped mid-sentence to restart next day).
- ☐ **Writing workshops and programmes**: Iowa Writers' Workshop (1936), creative writing courses in India (Ashoka, Jadavpur), Sahitya Akademi workshops.
- ☐ **Writer's block, reading as training, keeping a notebook**.
- ☐ **Writing in your mother tongue**: why many writers, from Ngũgĩ to Premchand (who moved from Urdu to Hindi), thought deeply about language choice.

## BOK-19 Publishing, libraries and the law

> **Before you start — BOK-19.** A manuscript becomes a book through a long chain of people: agents, editors, designers, printers, distributors, booksellers and librarians. **See also:** TEC (printing and e-book technology).

### ☐ BOK-19.1 How a book is published

- ☐ **Literary agent**: finds a publisher for the author (common in the UK and USA, growing in India).
- ☐ **Acquisition and contract**: advance against royalties; royalty rates; rights (translation, film, audio).
- ☐ **Editing**: structural editing, line editing, copy-editing, proofreading, fact-checking.
- ☐ **Design and production**: cover design, typesetting, typefaces (Garamond, Devanagari fonts such as Mukta), paper, binding (hardback, paperback, saddle-stitch), the ISBN and barcode.
- ☐ **Printing**: offset printing, print-on-demand; India's printing hub in Sivakasi and Delhi NCR.
- ☐ **Marketing and launch**: reviews, festivals, social media, BookTok and Bookstagram.
- ☐ **Distribution and bookselling**: distributors, bookshops (Higginbothams, Chennai, 1844, among India's oldest; College Street, Kolkata, one of the world's biggest second-hand book markets), online stores.
- ☐ **Self-publishing**: Kindle Direct Publishing, Notion Press (India), Pothi.

### ☐ BOK-19.2 Publishers of India and the world

- ☐ **World**: the "Big Five" (Penguin Random House, HarperCollins, Simon & Schuster, Hachette, Macmillan); university presses (Oxford University Press, oldest roots in 1478; Cambridge University Press, 1534, the oldest publishing house in continuous operation).
- ☐ **India (English)**: Penguin Random House India, HarperCollins India, Rupa, Westland, Aleph, Juggernaut, Speaking Tiger, Seagull Books (Kolkata, translation), Tulika and Karadi Tales (children's), Zubaan (feminist, from Kali for Women).
- ☐ **India (Indian languages)**: Gita Press (Gorakhpur), Rajkamal and Vani Prakashan (Hindi), DC Books (Malayalam, 1974), Ananda Publishers (Bengali), Kalachuvadu (Tamil), Mauj Prakashan (Marathi), Navajivan (Gujarati, Gandhi's works), Rajpal & Sons, National Book Trust, Sahitya Akademi, Publications Division.

### ☐ BOK-19.3 Translation

- ☐ **Why translation matters**: most people read world literature in translation; India reads itself through translation between its languages.
- ☐ **Ideas of translation**: word-for-word versus sense-for-sense; "domestication" and "foreignisation" (Lawrence Venuti); anuvad in the Indian tradition (literally "saying after").
- ☐ **Translation institutions**: Sahitya Akademi translation programme, National Translation Mission (CIIL Mysore), Indian Literature Abroad; UNESCO's *Index Translationum*.

### ☐ BOK-19.4 Libraries and archives

- ☐ **Kinds of library**: public, academic, national, school, special, digital, mobile (book vans, camel libraries in Rajasthan and Mongolia).
- ☐ **India**: National Library (Kolkata, from the Imperial Library, 1903), Delhi Public Library (1951, with UNESCO help), Connemara Public Library (Chennai, 1896), state central libraries, Raja Rammohun Roy Library Foundation (1972).
- ☐ **World**: Library of Congress, British Library, Bibliothèque nationale de France, New York Public Library, Bibliotheca Alexandrina (2002, built in memory of the ancient library).
- ☐ **Library science**: S. R. Ranganathan's *Five Laws* (books are for use; every reader his or her book; every book its reader; save the time of the reader; the library is a growing organism); Dewey Decimal Classification (1876); Colon Classification (1933).

### ☐ BOK-19.5 Copyright, public domain and censorship

- ☐ **Copyright**: the author's right over copying; in India it lasts for the author's life plus 60 years (Copyright Act, 1957); life plus 70 years in the EU and USA.
- ☐ **Public domain**: works free for all once copyright ends; that is why Project Gutenberg can share classics. Tagore's works entered the public domain in India in 2002.
- ☐ **Fair use / fair dealing**: limited quoting for review, study and criticism; the rule this index follows.
- ☐ **Open licences**: Creative Commons (2001); Wikipedia and open textbooks.
- ☐ **AI and copyright**: lawsuits over training AI on books without permission (2023 onward); questions of AI authorship; how Curious Sparoows labels AI-assisted work.
- ☐ **Censorship and banned books**: Ovid's exile, *Ulysses* (banned in the USA until 1933), *Lady Chatterley's Lover* (trial 1960), *The Satanic Verses* (import banned in India, 1988), the American Library Association's Banned Books Week (since 1982).

# PART 6 — IDEAS: THEORY, PHILOSOPHY, PSYCHOLOGY AND SOCIETY

> **About this Part.** Why literature matters and how people have thought about it, from Bharata and Aristotle to neuroscience. This is where the books section meets philosophy and psychology.

## BOK-20 Literary theory: the big ideas

> **Before you start — BOK-20.** Literary theory asks: what is literature, how does meaning work, and whose voice is heard? India had a sophisticated theory of literature (alankara shastra) well before modern Europe. **See also:** MOV-18 Film theory, MUS (rasa in music).

### ☐ BOK-20.1 Indian poetics (kavya shastra)

- ☐ **Rasa theory** (Bharata's *Natyashastra*): art creates "flavours" of emotion; the eight rasas (love, laughter, compassion, anger, heroism, fear, disgust, wonder), with peace (shanta) added later as a ninth.
- ☐ **Alankara school** (Bhamaha, Dandin): beauty comes from figures of speech.
- ☐ **Riti school** (Vamana): "riti is the soul of poetry", style.
- ☐ **Dhvani theory** (Anandavardhana's *Dhvanyaloka*, 9th century; Abhinavagupta's commentary): the best poetry suggests more than it says.
- ☐ **Vakrokti** (Kuntaka): poetry as "crooked", striking speech.
- ☐ **Auchitya** (Kshemendra): propriety, fitness of every element.
- ☐ **Tamil poetics** (*Tolkappiyam*): the tinai landscapes that match each mood of love to a landscape, flower and season.

### ☐ BOK-20.2 Classical Western poetics

- ☐ **Plato**: poets imitate imitations and stir dangerous emotions.
- ☐ **Aristotle's *Poetics***: mimesis (imitation), plot as the soul of tragedy, catharsis (purging of pity and fear).
- ☐ **Horace**: poetry should "delight and instruct".
- ☐ **Longinus**: the sublime.

### ☐ BOK-20.3 Modern schools of criticism

- ☐ **Formalism and New Criticism** (early 20th century): close reading of the text itself; Russian formalists (Shklovsky's "defamiliarisation"), I. A. Richards, Cleanth Brooks.
- ☐ **Structuralism** (from Saussure): literature as a system of signs; Roland Barthes, Vladimir Propp's *Morphology of the Folktale* (1928; 31 functions of fairy tales).
- ☐ **Post-structuralism and deconstruction**: meaning is unstable; Derrida, Barthes's "The Death of the Author" (1967), Foucault.
- ☐ **Reader-response**: meaning made by readers; Wolfgang Iser, Stanley Fish.
- ☐ **Marxist criticism**: literature and class; Georg Lukács, Raymond Williams, Terry Eagleton.
- ☐ **Psychoanalytic criticism**: Freud, Jung's archetypes, Lacan.
- ☐ **Feminist criticism**: Virginia Woolf's *A Room of One's Own* (1929), Simone de Beauvoir, Elaine Showalter, Gilbert and Gubar's *The Madwoman in the Attic*.
- ☐ **Postcolonial theory**: Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), Homi K. Bhabha (hybridity), Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak ("Can the Subaltern Speak?", 1988), Ngũgĩ's *Decolonising the Mind*.
- ☐ **Dalit aesthetics**: Sharankumar Limbale's *Towards an Aesthetic of Dalit Literature* (2004): literature judged by its truth to lived experience and its call for liberation.
- ☐ **Ecocriticism**: literature and nature; Amitav Ghosh's *The Great Derangement* (2016) on why fiction ignores climate change.
- ☐ **Digital humanities**: studying literature with computers (distant reading, Franco Moretti).

### ☐ BOK-20.4 Myth and archetype

- ☐ **Comparative mythology**: Max Müller, James Frazer's *The Golden Bough*, Joseph Campbell's monomyth, Devdutt Pattanaik's popular retellings.
- ☐ **Archetypal characters**: the hero, the trickster (Tenali Raman, Birbal, Anansi), the wise old guide, the shadow.

## BOK-21 Literature, mind and society

> **Before you start — BOK-21.** What reading does to people, and what people do with books: the psychology and sociology of literature. Claims are given with their evidence, and debates are flagged. **See also:** MOV-19 Cinema, mind and society.

### ☐ BOK-21.1 The psychology of reading and stories

- ☐ **Narrative transportation**: being "lost in a book" (Green and Brock, 2000); transported readers change their beliefs more.
- ☐ **Theory of mind and empathy**: studies by Keith Oatley, Raymond Mar, and Kidd and Castano (2013) link literary fiction to understanding others; some replications found smaller effects, so the debate continues.
- ☐ **Bibliotherapy**: reading as a support for mental health; used in libraries and clinics.
- ☐ **Reading and children**: reading aloud builds vocabulary and attention; India's ASER reports measure children's reading levels every few years.
- ☐ **Flow and deep reading**: Csikszentmihalyi's "flow"; Maryanne Wolf's concern for attention in the digital age.

### ☐ BOK-21.2 Philosophy and literature

- ☐ **Why we read fiction**: the "paradox of fiction" (why we feel for people who do not exist); Indian answer through rasa and sadharanikarana (universalisation of emotion, Bhatta Nayaka).
- ☐ **Literature as moral education**: the Panchatantra was written to teach princes; Martha Nussbaum argues novels train moral imagination.
- ☐ **Existentialist literature**: Camus, Dostoevsky, Kafka on meaning and freedom.
- ☐ **The Upanishadic dialogue and Socratic dialogue**: philosophy written as conversation.

### ☐ BOK-21.3 Literature and society

- ☐ **Literature and freedom movements**: "Vande Mataram", Bharati, Tagore, Premchand's *Soz-e-Watan* (1907, seized by the British), Faiz in prison, Uncle Tom's Cabin and abolition.
- ☐ **Literature and social reform**: Jyotirao Phule's *Gulamgiri* (1873), Ambedkar's writings, Rokeya's *Sultana's Dream*, Dalit and feminist literatures.
- ☐ **Literacy and access**: who gets to read and write; women's education (Savitribai Phule); Kerala's library movement (P. N. Panicker, the Granthasala Sangham, 1945; National Reading Day on 19 June).
- ☐ **Books and nations**: national epics (*Shahnameh*), national poets, the role of print in building nations (Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities*, 1983).
- ☐ **Banned books and burnings**: Nazi book burnings (1933), Heinrich Heine's warning that "where they burn books, they will end up burning people".

## BOK-E Editor's notes (corrections and decisions on the brief)

1. **"Every book" is not possible in one booklet**: more than a hundred million books have been published. The booklet gives a sourced landmark register per region and a clear plan ("How the registers grow", BOK-05.6) to fill year-wise lists on the website.
2. **Indian literature is many literatures**: the brief asked for India in depth, so every Eighth Schedule language has its own entry, plus Rajasthani, Bhojpuri, Awadhi, Braj, tribal literatures and Indian English. Smaller languages are thinner here and will grow.
3. **Classical languages updated**: the five languages added in October 2024 (Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese, Bengali) are included.
4. **Sales figures** are labelled as estimates; many "best-seller" numbers in popular lists cannot be verified.
5. **Sacred texts** are listed respectfully as literature and heritage, without ranking faiths.
6. **Latest winners** were checked on 3 November 2026. The 2026 Booker Prize is due on 9 November 2026 and will be added after the announcement. The latest Neustadt and JCB results were not verified, and the entries say so.
7. **Reviews**: critics are summarised in my own words with links; never copied.
