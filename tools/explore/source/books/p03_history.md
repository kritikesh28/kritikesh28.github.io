# PART 3 — HISTORY: THE STORY OF THE BOOK AND OF LITERATURE

> **About this Part.** The book's history is told twice. First as **material history** (what people wrote on and how copies were made), then as **literary history** (the movements and ages of writing). The intro twig in Part 0 is the two-minute version; this is the full map. Dates for ancient events are scholarly estimates.

## BOK-12 Timeline of the book: from memory to machine

> **Before you start — BOK-12.** Every age changed the body of the book: what it was made of, who could copy it and who could read it. Follow India and the world side by side. **See also:** TEC (printing, paper and digital technology), BOK-19 Publishing.

### ☐ BOK-12.1 Before writing (to about 3300 BCE)

- ☐ **Oral literature** everywhere: myths, songs and law kept in memory; cave art and symbols as early storytelling.
- ☐ **Vedic oral transmission** in India from about 1500 BCE, still alive today.

### ☐ BOK-12.2 Early writing systems (about 3300 BCE to 500 BCE)

- ☐ **Cuneiform** on clay (Sumer, about 3300–3100 BCE); the library of Ashurbanipal (7th century BCE).
- ☐ **Hieroglyphs and papyrus** (Egypt, from about 3200 BCE).
- ☐ **Indus script** (about 2600–1900 BCE): undeciphered seals.
- ☐ **Chinese oracle-bone script** (about 1200 BCE), ancestor of today's characters.
- ☐ **The alphabet**: Proto-Sinaitic and Phoenician scripts (about 1900–1050 BCE), parent of Greek, Latin, Arabic and Hebrew; Aramaic influenced Kharosthi.

### ☐ BOK-12.3 Scripts and manuscripts of India (about 300 BCE to 1800 CE)

- ☐ **Brahmi** in the edicts of Ashoka (3rd century BCE), parent of almost all Indian and South-East Asian scripts; **Kharosthi** in the north-west.
- ☐ **Tamil-Brahmi** cave inscriptions (from about the 3rd–2nd century BCE).
- ☐ **Palm leaf and birch bark** manuscripts; copper-plate grants; Jain bhandars; the **Gilgit** and **Bakhshali** manuscripts.
- ☐ **Great libraries**: Nalanda (5th–12th century) with its library called Dharmaganja; Vikramashila; Mughal imperial library; Saraswati Mahal Library, Thanjavur (one of Asia's oldest surviving libraries); Khuda Bakhsh Library, Patna (1891); Raza Library, Rampur.
- ☐ **Illustrated manuscripts**: Jain *Kalpa Sutra* paintings, Mughal *Hamzanama* and *Razmnama* (Persian Mahabharata), Rajput and Pahari painted Gita Govindas.

### ☐ BOK-12.4 Scroll to codex and the medieval book (about 1st to 14th century)

- ☐ **Codex**: bound pages, spreading in the Roman world from the 1st century CE and favoured by early Christians.
- ☐ **Paper** invented in China (traditionally Cai Lun, 105 CE); spread to Samarkand (751), Baghdad (about 794), Spain and later Europe.
- ☐ **Monastic scriptoria** and illuminated manuscripts (the *Book of Kells*, about 800).
- ☐ **The Islamic book**: Baghdad's House of Wisdom, calligraphy, bookbinding, paper mills; Timbuktu manuscripts.

### ☐ BOK-12.5 Printing (868 to 1800)

- ☐ **Woodblock printing** in China; the *Diamond Sutra* (868, oldest dated printed book).
- ☐ **Movable type**: Bi Sheng (clay, about 1040); Korea's *Jikji* (metal, 1377).
- ☐ **Gutenberg** (Mainz, about 1450; 42-line Bible about 1455); the "incunabula" (books printed before 1501).
- ☐ **Printing in India**: Goa (1556), the Tamil *Thambiran Vanakkam* (1578), Danish missionaries at Tranquebar (1712; Ziegenbalg's Tamil Bible), Calcutta presses (1777 onward), Serampore Mission Press (1800), Fort William College (1800) and its Hindi and Urdu prose books.
- ☐ **Newspapers**: *Hicky's Bengal Gazette* (1780), *Samachar Darpan* (Bengali, 1818), *Udant Martand* (first Hindi newspaper, 30 May 1826, now Hindi Journalism Day), *Jam-e-Jahan-Numa* (Urdu/Persian, 1822).

### ☐ BOK-12.6 The age of mass print (1800 to 1990)

- ☐ **Steam presses and cheap paper**: books and magazines for everyone; serialised novels (Dickens).
- ☐ **Lithography** made printing Urdu nastaliq and Indian scripts easy; Nawal Kishore Press, Lucknow (1858), printed thousands of titles in Urdu, Hindi, Persian, Sanskrit and Arabic.
- ☐ **Copyright laws**: Statute of Anne (Britain, 1710); Berne Convention (1886); India's Copyright Act (1957).
- ☐ **Public libraries**: Andrew Carnegie's libraries; India's library movement led by S. R. Ranganathan ("father of library science", Colon Classification, *Five Laws of Library Science*, 1931); the Madras Public Libraries Act (1948).
- ☐ **Paperback revolution**: Penguin Books (1935, Allen Lane); Indian publishers such as Rajkamal Prakashan (1947), Hind Pocket Books (1958, pioneered Hindi paperbacks), National Book Trust (1957).
- ☐ **Literacy**: India's literacy rose from about 18% (1951 census) to 74% (2011 census).

### ☐ BOK-12.7 The digital book (1971 to today)

- ☐ **Project Gutenberg** (1971), the first e-library; the **ISBN** (international from 1970).
- ☐ **Online bookselling**: Amazon (1995); Flipkart started as an online bookshop (2007).
- ☐ **E-readers**: Sony Reader (2006), Amazon Kindle (2007); Google Books scanning (2004).
- ☐ **Audiobooks and podcasts**: Audible (1995), Storytel; Indian-language audio platforms.
- ☐ **Web fiction and self-publishing**: Kindle Direct Publishing (2007), Wattpad (2006), Pratilipi (2014), Chinese web literature.
- ☐ **Digital libraries**: National Digital Library of India (IIT Kharagpur), Internet Archive, HathiTrust, Europeana, the Digital Library of India.
- ☐ **AI era (2020s)**: AI writing, translation and narration tools; lawsuits and debates on training data and authorship (BOK-19.5).

## BOK-13 Literary ages and movements

> **Before you start — BOK-13.** Movements are groups of writers who share a style, a purpose or a rebellion. Indian literature has its own movements, which often ran in parallel with or answered European ones. **See also:** MOV-08 Film movements, MUS (song movements).

### ☐ BOK-13.1 Indian movements and ages

- ☐ **Sangam age** (Tamil, about 300 BCE to 300 CE).
- ☐ **Classical Sanskrit kavya** (about 1st to 12th century).
- ☐ **Bhakti movement** (Tamil Nadu from the 6th century, then across India to the 17th): devotion in the people's languages; Alvars, Nayanars, Basavanna, Dnyaneshwar, Kabir, Ravidas, Surdas, Tulsidas, Meera, Chaitanya, Sankardev, Guru Nanak.
- ☐ **Sufi poetry**: Amir Khusrau, Bulleh Shah, Shah Latif, Lal Ded's mystic kin.
- ☐ **Riti kal** (Hindi court poetry, about 1650–1850).
- ☐ **Bengal Renaissance** (19th century): Rammohun Roy, Vidyasagar, Bankim, Tagore.
- ☐ **Nationalist literature**: "Vande Mataram", Bharati, Maithili Sharan Gupt (*Bharat-Bharati*), Makhanlal Chaturvedi, Subhadra Kumari Chauhan ("Jhansi ki Rani").
- ☐ **Chhayavad** (Hindi romanticism, 1918–1936): Prasad, Nirala, Pant, Mahadevi Verma.
- ☐ **Progressive Writers' Movement** (1936): literature for social change; Premchand, Faiz, Mulk Raj Anand, Sajjad Zaheer.
- ☐ **Prayogvad and Nayi Kavita** (from 1943): Agyeya's *Tar Saptak*, experimental Hindi poetry.
- ☐ **Nayi Kahani** (1950s): Mohan Rakesh, Kamleshwar, Rajendra Yadav.
- ☐ **Hungry Generation** (Bengali, 1961) and **Digambara poets** (Telugu, 1965): angry avant-garde poetry.
- ☐ **Navya** (Kannada modernism): Gopalakrishna Adiga, U. R. Ananthamurthy.
- ☐ **Dalit literature** (Marathi from the 1960s, then all languages): Ambedkarite writing of dignity and protest.
- ☐ **Feminist and women's writing**: Ismat Chughtai, Kamala Das, Mahasweta Devi, Ambai, Mridula Garg; Kali for Women (1984), India's first feminist press.
- ☐ **Indian English "Midnight's children" generation** (from 1981).
- ☐ **Adivasi and North-east literatures** (21st century).

### ☐ BOK-13.2 World movements, in order

- ☐ **Classical** (Greece and Rome): order, balance, imitation of nature.
- ☐ **Medieval** (about 500–1400): romance, allegory, courtly love, the troubadours.
- ☐ **Renaissance** (about 1300–1650): humanism; Petrarch, Shakespeare, Cervantes.
- ☐ **Baroque and Neoclassicism** (17th–18th century): Molière, Pope, Racine.
- ☐ **Enlightenment** (18th century): reason and satire; Voltaire, Swift.
- ☐ **Romanticism** (about 1790–1850): feeling, nature, the individual; Wordsworth, Goethe, Pushkin. Inspired by translated Sanskrit literature (Shakuntala and the Gita).
- ☐ **Realism and Naturalism** (about 1830–1900): Balzac, Flaubert, Tolstoy, Zola.
- ☐ **Symbolism and Aestheticism** (late 19th century): Baudelaire, Mallarmé, Oscar Wilde.
- ☐ **Modernism** (about 1900–1945): breaking forms; Joyce, Woolf, Eliot, Kafka, Proust.
- ☐ **Harlem Renaissance** (1920s): Black American voices.
- ☐ **Négritude** (1930s): Césaire, Senghor.
- ☐ **Existentialism and the Absurd** (1940s–50s): Sartre, Camus, Beckett.
- ☐ **The Beat Generation** (1950s): Ginsberg, Kerouac; some travelled in India.
- ☐ **Latin American Boom and magical realism** (1960s–70s).
- ☐ **Postmodernism** (from the 1960s): irony, play, metafiction; Pynchon, Calvino, Rushdie.
- ☐ **Postcolonial literature** (from the 1950s): Achebe, Ngũgĩ, Walcott, Rushdie, Roy.
- ☐ **Contemporary trends** (21st century): autofiction (Knausgård, Annie Ernaux), climate fiction, translated fiction boom, BookTok-driven bestsellers.

## BOK-14 Authors: the people of literature

> **Before you start — BOK-14.** A selection of writers whose work shaped literature, sorted by role. Each name gets a profile page on the website with life, major works, a short reading path and sources. Many more appear in the language sections of Part 1. **See also:** BOK-15 Nobel laureates, BOK-16.1 Jnanpith winners.

### ☐ BOK-14.1 Founders and ancient masters

- ☐ **Enheduanna** (Sumer): first named author. **Homer** (Greece): the epics. **Valmiki** and **Vyasa** (India, by tradition): the epics. **Confucius** and **Laozi** (China). **Sappho** (Greece): lyric. **Kalidasa** (India): poet-dramatist. **Thiruvalluvar** (Tamil): the *Kural*.

### ☐ BOK-14.2 Poets of the bhakti and Sufi worlds

- ☐ Andal, Basavanna, Akka Mahadevi, Lal Ded, Kabir, Ravidas, Meera, Surdas, Tulsidas, Tukaram, Guru Nanak, Rumi, Hafez, Amir Khusrau, Bulleh Shah: poets who spoke directly to God in the people's language.

### ☐ BOK-14.3 Great novelists of the world

- ☐ Murasaki Shikibu, Cervantes, Cao Xueqin, Austen, Dickens, Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, George Eliot, Flaubert, Hugo, Twain, Proust, Joyce, Woolf, Kafka, Thomas Mann, Faulkner, García Márquez, Achebe, Mahfouz, Morrison, Kawabata, Murakami.

### ☐ BOK-14.4 Great Indian novelists and storytellers

- ☐ Bankim Chandra, Tagore, Sarat Chandra, Premchand, Fakir Mohan Senapati, Bibhutibhushan, R. K. Narayan, Manto, Ismat Chughtai, Qurratulain Hyder, Thakazhi, Basheer, Shivaram Karanth, U. R. Ananthamurthy, Mahasweta Devi, Krishna Sobti, Nirmal Verma, Vijaydan Detha, O. V. Vijayan, Salman Rushdie, Amitav Ghosh, Arundhati Roy, Perumal Murugan, Geetanjali Shree, Banu Mushtaq.

### ☐ BOK-14.5 Women writers who changed literature

- ☐ Enheduanna, Sappho, Murasaki Shikibu, Akka Mahadevi, Meera, Mary Wollstonecraft, Jane Austen, Mary Shelley, the Brontës, George Eliot, Emily Dickinson, Virginia Woolf, Mahadevi Verma, Amrita Pritam, Ismat Chughtai, Toni Morrison, Kamala Das, Mahasweta Devi, Maya Angelou, Margaret Atwood, Wisława Szymborska, Han Kang, Annie Ernaux; and Tarabai Shinde, whose *Stri Purush Tulana* (1882) is an early Indian feminist text.

### ☐ BOK-14.6 Translators: the invisible authors

- ☐ Kumarajiva and Xuanzang (Sanskrit to Chinese), Ibn al-Muqaffa (Panchatantra into Arabic), Dara Shikoh (Upanishads into Persian, 1657), William Jones and Charles Wilkins (Sanskrit into English), Edward FitzGerald, Constance Garnett (Russian), A. K. Ramanujan, Gregory Rabassa (García Márquez), Daisy Rockwell, Deepa Bhasthi, Lin King. Since 2016 the International Booker splits its prize equally between author and translator.

### ☐ BOK-14.7 Best-selling and popular authors

- ☐ Agatha Christie (often listed by Guinness as the best-selling fiction writer of all time, with an estimated 2 billion copies), Shakespeare, Barbara Cartland, Danielle Steel, J. K. Rowling, Stephen King, Paulo Coelho, Enid Blyton, Dr. Seuss; in India Gulshan Nanda, Ibn-e-Safi, Ved Prakash Sharma (*Vardi Wala Gunda*), Surender Mohan Pathak, Ruskin Bond, Chetan Bhagat, Amish Tripathi, Sudha Murty.
