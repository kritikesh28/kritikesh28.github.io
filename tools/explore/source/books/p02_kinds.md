# PART 2 — KINDS OF LITERATURE: FORMS AND GENRES

> **About this Part.** The second great classification is what kind of writing a book is. Literature has two great trunks, **poetry** and **prose**, plus **drama** and the **oral tradition**, which mix both. Prose divides into **fiction** and **non-fiction**, and fiction into many **genres**. Sacred texts are a category of their own. Each entry gives a one- or two-line definition and examples from India and the world. Indian poetics (kavya, rasa, dhvani) is covered in Part 6 (BOK-20).

## BOK-06 Poetry: the first trunk

> **Before you start — BOK-06.** Poetry is language shaped by rhythm, sound and image, usually in lines. Forms are like containers: a sonnet has fourteen lines, a haiku three, a doha two. Learning a few forms helps you hear poems better. **See also:** MUS (songs and lyrics), BOK-20 Literary theory.

### ☐ BOK-06.1 Long and narrative poetry

- ☐ **Epic (mahakavya)**: a long story of heroes, gods and nations in elevated verse; *Mahabharata*, *Ramayana*, *Iliad*, *Shahnameh*, *Silappatikaram*, *Paradise Lost*.
- ☐ **Mock epic**: epic style for trivial things; Pope's *The Rape of the Lock*.
- ☐ **Ballad**: a short sung story in simple stanzas; English and Scottish ballads, Rajasthani and Punjabi folk ballads (Heer Ranjha).
- ☐ **Verse novel**: a novel in verse; Pushkin's *Eugene Onegin*, Vikram Seth's *The Golden Gate* (1986).
- ☐ **Akhyana and kathakavya**: Indian narrative poems sung by storytellers; Premanand's Gujarati akhyanas.

### ☐ BOK-06.2 Lyric poetry

- ☐ **Lyric**: a short poem of personal feeling, originally sung to the lyre; Sappho, Mirabai, Keats.
- ☐ **Ode**: a formal poem of praise or reflection; Pindar, Keats's "Ode to a Nightingale".
- ☐ **Elegy and marsiya**: poems of mourning; Gray's "Elegy", Urdu marsiya on Karbala (Mir Anis, Mirza Dabeer).
- ☐ **Pastoral**: idealised country life; Theocritus, Virgil's *Eclogues*.
- ☐ **Pada and bhajan**: devotional songs of the bhakti saints (Surdas, Meera, Tukaram's abhangas).

### ☐ BOK-06.3 Fixed forms of Europe

- ☐ **Sonnet**: fourteen lines; Petrarchan (8 + 6) and Shakespearean (4 + 4 + 4 + 2).
- ☐ **Villanelle**: nineteen lines with two repeating refrains; Dylan Thomas's "Do not go gentle".
- ☐ **Terza rima**: interlocking three-line stanzas; Dante.
- ☐ **Blank verse**: unrhymed iambic pentameter; Shakespeare, Milton; brought into Bengali by Michael Madhusudan Dutt (amitrakshar chhanda).
- ☐ **Limerick**: five comic lines; Edward Lear.

### ☐ BOK-06.4 Forms of the Persian and Urdu world

- ☐ **Ghazal**: rhyming couplets (sher), each complete in itself, with a repeated refrain (radif) and rhyme (qafiya), the poet's pen name (takhallus) in the last couplet (maqta); Hafez, Mir, Ghalib, Faiz; now written in Hindi, English and other languages.
- ☐ **Qasida**: a long ode of praise.
- ☐ **Masnavi**: rhyming couplets for long narratives; Rumi.
- ☐ **Rubai**: a four-line quatrain (AABA); Omar Khayyam.
- ☐ **Nazm**: Urdu poem on a single theme, freer than the ghazal; Iqbal, Faiz.
- ☐ **Qawwali and kafi**: Sufi poems for singing; Amir Khusrau, Bulleh Shah.

### ☐ BOK-06.5 Forms of India

- ☐ **Shloka (anushtubh)**: Sanskrit couplet of 32 syllables, the metre of the epics.
- ☐ **Doha**: two-line Hindi couplet (13 + 11 matras per half); Kabir, Rahim, Bihari.
- ☐ **Chaupai**: four-part metre used with dohas in the *Ramcharitmanas* and the Hanuman Chalisa.
- ☐ **Soratha, kavitt, savaiya, chhappay**: other Hindi metres of the riti and bhakti poets.
- ☐ **Venba and kural**: Tamil metres; the *Thirukkural* couplet.
- ☐ **Vachana**: Kannada free prose-poem of the 12th century.
- ☐ **Abhanga and ovi**: Marathi devotional metres.
- ☐ **Vakh and shruk**: Kashmiri mystic verses of Lal Ded and Nund Rishi.
- ☐ **Shayari and mushaira**: the culture of reciting couplets at public poetry gatherings; kavi sammelan in Hindi.

### ☐ BOK-06.6 Forms of East Asia

- ☐ **Haiku**: Japanese three-phrase poem (5–7–5 sounds) with a season word (kigo); Bashō.
- ☐ **Tanka and waka**: five-line Japanese poem (5–7–5–7–7).
- ☐ **Renga**: linked verse written by several poets in turn.
- ☐ **Sijo**: Korean three-line poem.
- ☐ **Shi and ci**: Chinese regulated verse and song lyrics.

### ☐ BOK-06.7 Modern and new poetry

- ☐ **Free verse**: no fixed metre or rhyme; Whitman, Nirala's mukt chhand, Nayi Kavita.
- ☐ **Prose poem**: poetry in paragraphs; Baudelaire, Tagore's later work.
- ☐ **Concrete and visual poetry**: shape on the page as meaning.
- ☐ **Spoken word and slam**: poetry for performance; India's open-mic scene.
- ☐ **Song lyrics and rap**: Bob Dylan's Nobel (2016); Gulzar, Javed Akhtar and Sahir in film (MUS).
- ☐ **Instapoetry**: short poems shared on social media; Rupi Kaur.

## BOK-07 Prose fiction: the forms of storytelling

> **Before you start — BOK-07.** Fiction is invented narrative in prose. Its forms are mainly sorted by length. Word counts are common publishing guides (for example, those used by the Hugo and Nebula awards), not strict laws. **See also:** BOK-10 Genre fiction, BOK-18 The craft of writing.

### ☐ BOK-07.1 Forms by length

- ☐ **Novel**: a long prose fiction, usually over 40,000 words; room for many characters and years.
- ☐ **Novella**: 17,500 to 40,000 words (Hugo/Nebula guide); Kafka's *The Metamorphosis*, Hemingway's *The Old Man and the Sea*.
- ☐ **Novelette**: 7,500 to 17,500 words.
- ☐ **Short story**: readable in one sitting; Chekhov, Premchand, Manto, Munro, O. Henry, Maupassant.
- ☐ **Flash fiction and micro-fiction**: under about 1,000 words; the six-word story.
- ☐ **Fable, parable and fairy tale**: short moral or magical tales; Aesop, Panchatantra, Jataka, Grimm, Andersen.
- ☐ **Frame story**: stories inside a story; *Arabian Nights*, *Panchatantra*, *Decameron*, *Kathasaritsagara*.

### ☐ BOK-07.2 Kinds of novel by form

- ☐ **Epistolary**: told in letters; *Dracula*, *Frankenstein* in part.
- ☐ **Bildungsroman**: the growing-up novel; *Great Expectations*, *Pather Panchali*.
- ☐ **Picaresque**: adventures of a rogue on the road; *Lazarillo de Tormes*.
- ☐ **Historical novel**: fiction set in a real past; Walter Scott, *Ponniyin Selvan*, Mantel's *Wolf Hall*.
- ☐ **Saga and roman-fleuve**: a long family or multi-volume story; *Buddenbrooks*, *The Forsyte Saga*.
- ☐ **Stream of consciousness**: following thoughts as they flow; Woolf, Joyce.
- ☐ **Metafiction**: fiction aware of being fiction; Calvino's *If on a winter's night a traveler*.
- ☐ **Anchalik (regional) novel**: Hindi novels rooted in one region's dialect and life; Renu's *Maila Anchal*.
- ☐ **Partition literature**: stories of 1947; Manto, Bhisham Sahni, Amrita Pritam, Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan*.

### ☐ BOK-07.3 Modes of fiction

- ☐ **Realism**: life as it is; Premchand, Tolstoy, George Eliot.
- ☐ **Naturalism**: people shaped by heredity and environment; Zola.
- ☐ **Magical realism**: magic told as normal; García Márquez, Rushdie.
- ☐ **Satire**: ridicule to expose folly; Swift, Shrilal Shukla, Harishankar Parsai (vyangya).
- ☐ **Allegory**: story with a second, symbolic meaning; Bunyan, Orwell's *Animal Farm*.
- ☐ **Absurdism**: life without meaning shown through strangeness; Kafka, Camus, Beckett.

## BOK-08 Non-fiction: true stories and ideas

> **Before you start — BOK-08.** Non-fiction claims to be true. Its value depends on evidence, honesty and clear writing. Many of the best-selling books in India are non-fiction: exam guides, biographies and self-help. **See also:** Curriculum Index (subject books), BOK-17 Best-sellers.

### ☐ BOK-08.1 Life writing

- ☐ **Biography**: a life written by someone else; Boswell's *Life of Johnson*, Rajmohan Gandhi's lives of Gandhi and Patel.
- ☐ **Autobiography**: one's own life; Gandhi's *The Story of My Experiments with Truth*, Nehru's *An Autobiography*, Ambedkar's *Waiting for a Visa*, A. P. J. Abdul Kalam's *Wings of Fire*.
- ☐ **Memoir**: a part of one's life or one theme; Maya Angelou, Michelle Obama's *Becoming*, Mamta Kalia's *Jeete Jee Allahabad* (Sahitya Akademi 2025).
- ☐ **Diary and letters**: Anne Frank's diary; Nehru's *Letters from a Father to His Daughter*; Ghalib's letters.
- ☐ **Charita and tazkira**: Indian royal biographies (*Harshacharita*) and Persian biographical dictionaries of poets; the Mughal memoirs *Baburnama* and Jahangir's *Tuzuk*.

### ☐ BOK-08.2 Knowledge and argument

- ☐ **Essay**: a short exploration of one idea; Montaigne invented the word ("attempt"); Bacon, Orwell, Hazari Prasad Dwivedi, Ramachandra Shukla (Hindi nibandh).
- ☐ **Philosophy**: Plato's dialogues, the Upanishads, Confucius, Kant, Simone de Beauvoir, Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste* (1936).
- ☐ **History**: Herodotus, Sima Qian, Kalhana, Ibn Khaldun, Abul Fazl's *Akbarnama*, Romila Thapar, Ramachandra Guha (*India after Gandhi*), William Dalrymple.
- ☐ **Popular science**: Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859), Carl Sagan's *Cosmos*, Stephen Hawking's *A Brief History of Time*, Richard Dawkins, Siddhartha Mukherjee (*The Emperor of All Maladies*, Pulitzer 2011), Jayant Narlikar.
- ☐ **Politics and economics**: Kautilya's *Arthashastra*, Machiavelli's *The Prince*, Adam Smith, Marx and Engels, Amartya Sen (*The Argumentative Indian*), Thomas Piketty.
- ☐ **Self-help and personal development**: Dale Carnegie, Stephen Covey, James Clear's *Atomic Habits*; read with a critical eye.

### ☐ BOK-08.3 Narrative and practical non-fiction

- ☐ **Travel writing**: Xuanzang, Ibn Battuta, Marco Polo, Al-Biruni's *Kitab al-Hind* (about 1030), Rahul Sankrityayan ("father of Hindi travel writing"), Pico Iyer.
- ☐ **Journalism and reportage**: P. Sainath's *Everybody Loves a Good Drought*, Katherine Boo's *Behind the Beautiful Forevers*, Svetlana Alexievich (Nobel 2015, oral history).
- ☐ **True crime**: Truman Capote's *In Cold Blood*.
- ☐ **Nature writing**: Gilbert White, Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962, launched modern environmentalism), Salim Ali's *The Book of Indian Birds*, Jim Corbett.
- ☐ **Reference books**: dictionaries, encyclopedias (Diderot's *Encyclopédie*, Britannica since 1768, Wikipedia since 2001), atlases, almanacs, panchangs.
- ☐ **How-to, cookbooks and craft books**: links to COK, GRD, CRO, KNT, EMB and SEW indexes.
- ☐ **Textbooks and exam guides**: NCERT books, competitive-exam guides (links to the Curriculum Index and Exam Guide).

## BOK-09 Drama and the oral tradition

> **Before you start — BOK-09.** Drama is literature written to be performed, and oral literature is literature that lives in performance. Both are often missed when people think "books". **See also:** MOV-18 (rasa in cinema), MUS.

### ☐ BOK-09.1 Drama on the page and stage

- ☐ **Sanskrit nataka and prakarana**: heroic and social plays; Kalidasa, Shudraka; rules in the *Natyashastra*.
- ☐ **Greek tragedy and comedy**: the origin of Western drama.
- ☐ **Noh, kabuki and Chinese opera**: East Asian theatre forms.
- ☐ **Shakespearean and Elizabethan drama**.
- ☐ **Modern realist drama**: Ibsen, Chekhov, Arthur Miller, Tennessee Williams.
- ☐ **Theatre of the Absurd**: Beckett (*Waiting for Godot*), Ionesco.
- ☐ **Modern Indian theatre**: Tagore, Mohan Rakesh (*Ashadh Ka Ek Din*), Vijay Tendulkar, Badal Sircar (Third Theatre), Girish Karnad (*Tughlaq*, *Hayavadana*), Habib Tanvir (*Charandas Chor*), Safdar Hashmi (street theatre).
- ☐ **Folk drama**: Yakshagana, Jatra, Tamasha, Nautanki, Khyal (Rajasthan), Bhavai, Terukkuttu, Ramlila.

### ☐ BOK-09.2 Oral literature

- ☐ **Oral epics**: Pabuji, Devnarayan, Alha-Udal, the *Manas*, *Sundiata*.
- ☐ **Folk tales**: collected by the Grimms, Verrier Elwin, A. K. Ramanujan (*Folktales from India*, 1991), Vijaydan Detha.
- ☐ **Proverbs, riddles and lullabies**: the smallest literature, and among the oldest.
- ☐ **Myth**: sacred stories of origins (see BOK-11 and BOK-20.4).
- ☐ **Urban legends and internet folklore**: new oral literature online.

## BOK-10 Genre fiction: the shelves of the bookshop

> **Before you start — BOK-10.** Genres are families of stories with shared expectations: a mystery promises a solution, a romance promises love at the centre. "Literary fiction" is itself sometimes treated as a genre. Genres mix freely. **See also:** MOV-06 Film genres (many films adapt genre books).

### ☐ BOK-10.1 Mystery, crime and detective fiction

A crime, an investigation and a solution. Poe's Dupin (1841), Wilkie Collins's *The Moonstone* (1868), Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes (1887), Agatha Christie, Georges Simenon; India's Byomkesh Bakshi (Sharadindu Bandyopadhyay), Feluda (Satyajit Ray), Ibn-e-Safi's Urdu spy novels, Surender Mohan Pathak, Hindi "pulp" (lugdi) novels.

- ☐ **Sub-genres**: cozy, hard-boiled (Chandler, Hammett), police procedural, Nordic noir, legal thriller, locked-room mystery, historical mystery.

### ☐ BOK-10.2 Thriller and suspense

Danger and a race against time. John Buchan, Ian Fleming (James Bond), John le Carré (espionage), Frederick Forsyth, Dan Brown, Lee Child; India's Ashwin Sanghi, Mukul Deva. Sub-genres: spy, techno-thriller, psychological thriller, political thriller.

### ☐ BOK-10.3 Romance

A central love story with an emotionally satisfying ending. Jane Austen as ancestor; Mills & Boon (1908) and Harlequin; Nora Roberts, Colleen Hoover; India's Anuja Chauhan, Durjoy Datta and Hindi and Urdu romances. Sub-genres: historical, contemporary, romantic comedy, paranormal, "romantasy".

### ☐ BOK-10.4 Fantasy

Magic and invented worlds. Roots in myth and epic; George MacDonald, Tolkien (*The Lord of the Rings*), C. S. Lewis, Ursula K. Le Guin, Terry Pratchett, J. K. Rowling, George R. R. Martin, N. K. Jemisin (three Hugos in a row, 2016–2018); Indian mythological fantasy: Amish's Shiva Trilogy, Samit Basu, Anand Neelakantan. Sub-genres: high/epic, urban, sword and sorcery, mythic, grimdark, portal fantasy.

### ☐ BOK-10.5 Science fiction

Stories driven by science, technology and "what if". Mary Shelley, Jules Verne, H. G. Wells, Asimov, Arthur C. Clarke, Philip K. Dick, Octavia Butler, Liu Cixin; India's Jagadish Chandra Bose (*Niruddesher Kahini*, 1896), Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain (*Sultana's Dream*, 1905, feminist utopia), Satyajit Ray's Professor Shonku, Jayant Narlikar, Vandana Singh. Sub-genres: hard SF, space opera, cyberpunk, dystopia, climate fiction (cli-fi), solarpunk, afrofuturism.

### ☐ BOK-10.6 Horror and the gothic

Fear, the uncanny and the supernatural. Horace Walpole (*The Castle of Otranto*, 1764), Bram Stoker (*Dracula*, 1897), H. P. Lovecraft, Stephen King, Shirley Jackson; Indian ghost stories (bhoot katha), Ruskin Bond's ghost tales, Rabindranath's "Kshudhita Pashan" (The Hungry Stones).

### ☐ BOK-10.7 Historical and adventure fiction

Swashbucklers, quests and the past re-imagined. Dumas (*The Three Musketeers*), Stevenson (*Treasure Island*), Kalki's *Ponniyin Selvan*, Devaki Nandan Khatri's *Chandrakanta* (1888, a Hindi bestseller that made people learn Hindi to read it).

### ☐ BOK-10.8 Children's and young adult (YA) books

- ☐ **Picture books**: Eric Carle, Maurice Sendak; Tulika and Pratham Books in India.
- ☐ **Children's classics**: *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, *The Jungle Book* (Kipling, set in India), *Pinocchio*, Enid Blyton, Roald Dahl, *Panchatantra* retold, *Amar Chitra Katha* (1967, Anant Pai), *Chandamama* (1947), *Champak*, *Tinkle*.
- ☐ **Young adult**: S. E. Hinton's *The Outsiders* (1967) as an early landmark; *Harry Potter*, *The Hunger Games*; Paro Anand.
- ☐ **Award**: Hans Christian Andersen Award, Newbery and Carnegie medals, Sahitya Akademi Bal Sahitya Puraskar.

### ☐ BOK-10.9 Comics, graphic novels and manga

- ☐ **Comics**: comic strips and books; *Tintin* (Hergé), *Asterix*, Marvel and DC superheroes; India's *Amar Chitra Katha*, *Chacha Chaudhary* (Pran, 1971), Raj Comics, Diamond Comics.
- ☐ **Graphic novel**: long-form comics; Art Spiegelman's *Maus* (Pulitzer special citation 1992), Marjane Satrapi's *Persepolis*; Sarnath Banerjee's *Corridor* (2004, often called India's first graphic novel), Orijit Sen's *River of Stories* (1994).
- ☐ **Manga and manhwa**: Japanese and Korean comics; Osamu Tezuka ("god of manga"), *One Piece*, webtoons.

### ☐ BOK-10.10 Literary fiction and other shelves

- ☐ **Literary fiction**: focused on style, character and ideas more than plot; most Booker winners.
- ☐ **Speculative fiction**: umbrella term for SF, fantasy and horror.
- ☐ **Western, sea story, war novel, campus novel, sports fiction (SPT), food fiction (COK)**.
- ☐ **Fan fiction and web novels**: readers writing in others' worlds; Wattpad, Archive of Our Own (Hugo Award 2019 for Best Related Work), Pratilipi in Indian languages.

## BOK-11 Sacred and scriptural texts

> **Before you start — BOK-11.** Sacred texts are some of the oldest, most read and most translated books in the world. This index lists them as literature and as cultural heritage, respectfully and without ranking faiths. Each tradition's own understanding of its texts comes first. **See also:** BOK-21 Literature and society.

### ☐ BOK-11.1 Texts of Indian traditions

- ☐ **Hindu**: shruti (the Vedas and Upanishads) and smriti (epics, Puranas, Dharmashastras, Agamas); the *Bhagavad Gita*.
- ☐ **Buddhist**: the Pali Tipitaka, Mahayana sutras (such as the *Lotus Sutra*, *Heart Sutra*), the Tibetan Kangyur and Tengyur.
- ☐ **Jain**: Agamas; Umaswati's *Tattvartha Sutra*; the *Kalpa Sutra*.
- ☐ **Sikh**: Sri Guru Granth Sahib (compiled 1604, installed as eternal Guru in 1708), with verses of six Gurus and fifteen bhagats including Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas and Farid.

### ☐ BOK-11.2 Texts of West Asian traditions

- ☐ **Jewish**: the Tanakh (Torah, Prophets, Writings) and Talmud.
- ☐ **Christian**: the Bible (Old and New Testaments); the most printed and translated book in history (full Bible in over 700 languages, according to Wycliffe Global Alliance).
- ☐ **Islamic**: the Qur'an (revealed in Arabic in the 7th century) and the Hadith collections.
- ☐ **Zoroastrian / Parsi**: the Avesta, including the Gathas of Zarathustra.
- ☐ **Bahá'í**: the Kitáb-i-Aqdas.

### ☐ BOK-11.3 Texts of East Asian and other traditions

- ☐ **Chinese**: the Confucian classics and the *Daodejing*; the *I Ching*.
- ☐ **Japanese Shinto**: *Kojiki* and *Nihon Shoki*.
- ☐ **Indigenous sacred narratives**: the *Popol Vuh*, Aboriginal Dreaming stories, African creation myths.
