## BOK-00 The Gateway: How can a few marks on a page put another person's mind inside yours?

**Web address:** /books/how-books-work/ · **Keywords:** history of books for beginners, why we read, how writing began, what is literature

Open any book and something strange happens. Black marks on paper turn into a voice in your head. That voice may belong to someone who died two thousand years ago, or who lives on the other side of the world, and for a few hours you think their thoughts.

## BOK-00.01 How did humans tell stories before writing?

**Short answer:** With memory, rhythm and community. For most of human history, literature was oral: epics, songs, riddles and laws were memorised and performed. Rhyme, metre and repetition were the "storage technology".

**Keywords:** oral tradition, oral literature, how the Vedas were preserved, bards, storytelling before writing

### The oldest library was a human head

Writing is only about 5,000 years old, but people have told stories for as long as they have had language. Before writing, a story survived only if someone remembered it and someone else wanted to hear it. So stories were shaped to be remembered: they used **rhythm, rhyme, refrains, stock phrases and vivid images**.

### India's extraordinary memory

India is the world's great example. The **Vedas** were composed from roughly 1500 BCE and passed down orally for centuries before they were written. Reciters used patterns such as **padapatha** (word by word), **kramapatha** and **ghanapatha**, which repeat words in different orders like an error-checking code. UNESCO recognised Vedic chanting as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008 (proclaimed 2003).

### Bards everywhere

Greek bards sang the Homeric epics. West African **griots** (jeli) still carry the history of families and kingdoms, including the *Epic of Sundiata*. In Rajasthan, **bhopas** sing the epic of Pabuji in front of a painted phad scroll. Tribal communities across India, from the Gonds to the Bhils, keep creation stories and songs in memory.

### What oral literature does well

Oral stories change a little with every telling. They belong to the community rather than to one author. They are performed, not just read, so music, gesture and audience all matter.

**Remember this:** Literature is older than writing; memory and rhythm were the first books.

**Next:** If stories lived in memory, who first wrote one down, and what was the first "book"?

## BOK-00.02 Who wrote the first book?

**Short answer:** Writing began in Mesopotamia (cuneiform, around 3300–3100 BCE) and Egypt (hieroglyphs) for accounts and records. Among the oldest literary works are the Sumerian hymns of **Enheduanna** (around 2300 BCE), often called the first named author, and the **Epic of Gilgamesh**.

**Keywords:** first book ever written, oldest literature, Epic of Gilgamesh, Enheduanna, history of writing

### Writing began with accounts, not poems

The first writing in Sumer recorded grain, sheep and debts on clay tablets. Writing was invented independently in a few places: Mesopotamia, Egypt, China (oracle-bone script, around 1200 BCE) and Mesoamerica. The Indus script (around 2600–1900 BCE) is still undeciphered, so we do not know what it says.

### The first named author

**Enheduanna**, a priestess and daughter of King Sargon of Akkad, wrote hymns to the goddess Inanna around 2300 BCE. She is usually called the first author known by name, and she was a woman.

### The first great story

The **Epic of Gilgamesh** tells of a king who loses his friend Enkidu and searches for immortality. Its stories circulated from around 2100 BCE, and the best-known version was found in the library of King Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (7th century BCE). It even contains a flood story.

### What counts as a "book"?

A clay tablet is not a book as we know it. Over time, writing moved to **papyrus scrolls** (Egypt), **bamboo and silk** (China), **palm leaves and birch bark** (India), **parchment** and finally the **codex**, the bound book with pages, which spread in the Roman world from the 1st century CE.

**Remember this:** The first writing was bookkeeping; the first named author was a woman poet, Enheduanna.

**Next:** India did not use clay. Why did it write on palm leaves?

## BOK-00.03 Why did India write on palm leaves?

**Short answer:** Because palm leaves were cheap, plentiful and durable in the Indian climate. Scribes wrote on dried talipot or palmyra leaves, and on birch bark (bhojpatra) in the north. Leaves were tied with string through holes; the word **grantha** (book) comes from "tying".

**Keywords:** palm leaf manuscripts, bhojpatra, how ancient Indian books were made, grantha meaning, National Mission for Manuscripts

### Making a palm-leaf book

Leaves were dried, boiled, smoothed and cut to size. In the south and east, scribes **scratched** letters with an iron stylus and rubbed in soot or lamp-black so the grooves turned black; this helped shape rounded scripts like Odia and Malayalam, because straight lines would split the leaf. In the north, people often **wrote with ink** on birch bark or palm leaf.

### The Gilgit and Bakhshali manuscripts

The **Gilgit manuscripts** (birch bark, about the 5th–7th centuries CE) are among the oldest surviving manuscripts in India and include Buddhist texts. The **Bakhshali manuscript** of mathematics, also on birch bark, contains a dot used as zero; radiocarbon tests in 2017 dated some of its leaves to the 3rd–4th century CE, though scholars still debate its exact age.

### A living heritage

Leaves decay, so texts were copied again and again by families, monasteries, temples and Jain **bhandars** (libraries) such as those at Patan and Jaisalmer. India's **National Mission for Manuscripts** (2003) estimates that India holds millions of manuscripts, many still uncatalogued.

### Paper arrives

Paper, invented in China (traditionally credited to Cai Lun, 105 CE), reached India through Central Asia and was common by the Sultanate and Mughal periods. Mughal workshops produced illustrated manuscripts such as the *Hamzanama*.

**Remember this:** India's "books" were tied bundles of leaves, copied again and again, and its scripts were shaped by the stylus.

**Next:** Copying by hand was slow. How did printing change the world?

## BOK-00.04 How did printing change the world?

**Short answer:** Printing made copies cheap and identical. China printed first (woodblock; the *Diamond Sutra* of 868 CE is the oldest dated printed book). Bi Sheng made movable type around 1040. **Johannes Gutenberg's** press (around 1450) spread across Europe and helped power the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution and newspapers.

**Keywords:** history of printing, Gutenberg press, Diamond Sutra, first printing press in India, movable type

### Asia first

The **Diamond Sutra** found at Dunhuang is dated 868 CE. **Bi Sheng** made clay movable type in Song China, and Korea printed the *Jikji* with metal movable type in 1377, before Gutenberg.

### Gutenberg's revolution

Around 1450 in Mainz, Gutenberg combined metal type, oil-based ink and a screw press. His **Bible** appeared about 1455. Within fifty years, presses were running in more than 200 European cities, and millions of books had been printed. Martin Luther's pamphlets of 1517 spread fast because of print.

### Printing reaches India

Portuguese Jesuits set up a press in **Goa in 1556**. The first book printed in an Indian script was Tamil: *Thambiran Vanakkam* (1578). In 1800 the **Serampore Mission Press** (William Carey) and Fort William College began printing in Bengali, Hindi and many other languages. Newspapers followed: *Hicky's Bengal Gazette* (1780).

### What changed

Ideas could now travel faster than rulers could stop them. Spelling and languages became more standard. Readers could compare identical copies, which helped science. It also made censorship a bigger business.

**Remember this:** Printing turned books from rare treasures into everyday tools, first in Asia, then explosively in Europe.

**Next:** Once books were cheap, a new form appeared that worried parents and priests. Why was the novel once called dangerous?

## BOK-00.05 Why was the novel once called dangerous?

**Short answer:** Because it let ordinary readers, especially young women, live other lives in private. In 18th- and 19th-century Europe and India, critics warned that novels corrupted morals and wasted time. The same fear later attached to comics, television, video games and phones.

**Keywords:** history of the novel, first novel ever, Tale of Genji, Don Quixote, first Indian novel

### Where the novel began

There is no single first novel. **Murasaki Shikibu's** *The Tale of Genji* (Japan, around 1010) is often called the world's first novel. **Cervantes's** *Don Quixote* (1605 and 1615) is called the first modern European novel. Sanskrit had long prose romances: **Banabhatta's** *Kadambari* (7th century), whose name became the word for "novel" in Kannada and Marathi.

### The fear of novels

In 18th-century England, sermons and newspapers warned that novels made girls dreamy and disobedient. In 19th-century Bengal and Maharashtra, reformers argued about whether novels taught good conduct or bad.

### India's first novels

Bengali's ***Durgeshnandini*** (Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay, 1865) is often called the first major Indian novel. Earlier candidates include *Alaler Gharer Dulal* (Peary Chand Mitra, 1857) in Bengali, *Pariksha Guru* (Lala Srinivas Das, 1882) in Hindi, *Indulekha* (O. Chandu Menon, 1889) in Malayalam and *Rajmohan's Wife* (Bankim, 1864) in English.

### Why the fear was wrong, and right

Novels did change people. They taught readers to imagine the inner life of someone else, which researchers today link to empathy (BOK-21). That is exactly why rulers and moralists feared them.

**Remember this:** The novel is a machine for living other lives, and every new storytelling machine is feared at first.

**Next:** Is poetry older than all of this?

## BOK-00.06 Is poetry older than prose?

**Short answer:** In literature, yes, almost everywhere. The oldest literary works (the Vedas, Gilgamesh, Homer, the Chinese *Shijing*, Tamil Sangam poems) are in verse, because rhythm helps memory. Prose literature grows later, once writing is common.

**Keywords:** oldest poetry, why poetry came first, chhanda, metre in poetry, Shijing

### Why verse came first

Verse is easier to remember. A line with a fixed beat tells you when you have forgotten a syllable. India built a full science of metre, **chhandas**, one of the six Vedangas; **Pingala's** *Chandahshastra* (before 200 BCE) even uses binary-like patterns of short and long syllables.

### Verse for everything

Ancient India wrote medicine, astronomy and grammar in verse. Aryabhata's astronomy (499 CE) is in verse. The Chinese *Shijing* (Classic of Poetry) collects songs from around 1000 to 600 BCE. **Tamil Sangam** poetry (roughly 300 BCE to 300 CE) sorts poems into **akam** (inner life, love) and **puram** (outer life, war and kings).

### Then prose grows up

Prose appears in law codes, commentaries, letters, histories and stories. Sanskrit prose romances, Greek history (Herodotus), Chinese history (Sima Qian) and Arabic prose all grow once writing is common.

### Today

Prose dominates bookshops, but poetry survives everywhere people want words to be remembered: songs, slogans, prayers, film lyrics, rap and Instagram poems.

**Remember this:** Poetry came first because rhythm was memory; prose came when paper did the remembering.

**Next:** Some of these old poems outlived the kingdoms that made them. Why do some books outlive empires?

## BOK-00.07 Why do some books outlive empires?

**Short answer:** Because they are copied, taught, translated and loved by many communities for different reasons. A "classic" survives when each generation finds something new in it. Luck, religion, schools and translation all help.

**Keywords:** what makes a classic, oldest books still read, why classics survive, Panchatantra translations

### The Panchatantra's journey

The **Panchatantra** (animal fables, compiled perhaps around 300 CE) was translated into Middle Persian in the 6th century, then into Arabic as *Kalila wa Dimna* (Ibn al-Muqaffa, 8th century), then into Hebrew, Latin and most European languages. It is one of the most translated books in history.

### The four reasons books survive

1. **Copying communities**: monasteries, temples, madrasas, families and later schools kept copying.
2. **Rereadability**: the *Mahabharata*, Homer and Shakespeare say different things to different ages.
3. **Translation**: a book in many languages is hard to destroy.
4. **Luck**: fire, war and decay destroyed much. The Library of Alexandria's losses and the burning of Nalanda (around 1193) are remembered as warnings.

### What we lost

Most ancient literature is lost. We know of hundreds of Greek plays by title only. Many Sanskrit, Tamil and Prakrit works survive only as quotations in other books.

**Remember this:** A classic is a book that every generation chooses to copy again.

**Next:** Classics survive in libraries, but what actually happens in your brain when you read one?

## BOK-00.08 What happens in your brain when you read?

**Short answer:** Reading borrows brain circuits built for vision and speech. A small region on the left side, nicknamed the "letterbox area" by the neuroscientist Stanislas Dehaene, learns to recognise written words. Stories also activate areas used for understanding other people's minds.

**Keywords:** reading and the brain, Stanislas Dehaene, Maryanne Wolf, deep reading, how children learn to read

### The brain was not built to read

Writing is too recent for evolution to have made a reading organ. Instead, as Dehaene explains in *Reading in the Brain* (2009), a child's brain **recycles** part of the visual system (the visual word form area) to recognise letters and link them to sounds.

### Deep reading

The scholar **Maryanne Wolf** (*Proust and the Squid*, 2007; *Reader, Come Home*, 2018) describes "deep reading": slow, attentive reading that builds inference, analogy and empathy. She worries that skimming on screens trains a shallower habit, though research on this is still developing.

### Stories and other minds

Psychologists **Keith Oatley** and **Raymond Mar** have found links between reading fiction and the ability to understand others' feelings, though how strong the effect is remains debated (BOK-21).

### Reading in many scripts

Readers of Hindi (an abugida), Chinese (logographic) and English (alphabetic) use slightly different brain strategies. Learning to read in any script changes the brain.

**Remember this:** Reading is a cultural invention that rewires a natural brain, and it trains us to imagine other minds.

**Next:** If reading is so powerful, will e-books, audiobooks and AI replace books?

## BOK-00.09 Will e-books and AI replace books?

**Short answer:** Unlikely. They are adding new ways to read, as printing did. E-books, audiobooks and web novels have grown, but printed books still sell strongly worldwide. AI can write text, but books are also about trust, authorship and shared culture, which raises new questions of credit and copyright.

**Keywords:** future of books, e-books vs print, audiobooks, AI written books, web novels

### Every new medium was meant to kill the book

Radio, cinema, television and the internet were each expected to end reading. Instead, books changed shape.

### The digital shelf

Michael Hart started **Project Gutenberg** in 1971, the first digital library. Amazon's **Kindle** (2007) made e-books mainstream. **Audiobooks** have grown fast. **Web novels** and platforms such as Wattpad, China's web-fiction sites and India's Pratilipi (many Indian languages) have created millions of new readers and writers.

### AI and the book

AI tools can now draft text, translate and summarise. This raises real questions: who is the author, who gets credited, and was the AI trained on writers' work without permission? Courts and publishers are still deciding. Curious Sparoows labels AI-assisted work openly, which is one honest answer.

### What a book still does

A book offers a whole, finished argument or world, by a responsible author, that you can return to. That deep, unbroken attention is hard to replace.

**Remember this:** The book keeps changing its body: tablet, scroll, codex, screen, voice. It has not lost its soul.

## BOK-00.10 Recap: the story of the book in one page

**Short answer:** Humans told stories in memory, wrote them on clay, leaves and scrolls, printed them, turned them into novels and now read them on screens and hear them as audio. The constant is one mind reaching another.

**Keywords:** history of books summary, story of literature, books timeline

1. **Memory** (forever): oral epics, songs and the Vedas.
2. **Writing** (from about 3300 BCE): Sumer, Egypt, China, Mesoamerica.
3. **First authors**: Enheduanna, Gilgamesh, Homer, Sangam poets, Valmiki and Vyasa (by tradition).
4. **Leaves, scrolls and codices**: palm leaf and birch bark in India, papyrus in Egypt, the codex in Rome.
5. **Paper and print**: China (105 CE paper; 868 Diamond Sutra), Korea (1377 Jikji), Gutenberg (about 1455), Goa (1556), Serampore (1800).
6. **The novel**: Genji (around 1010), Don Quixote (1605), Durgeshnandini (1865).
7. **Mass reading**: cheap paperbacks (Penguin, 1935), libraries and literacy campaigns.
8. **Digital**: Project Gutenberg (1971), Kindle (2007), audiobooks, web novels, AI.

**Remember this:** Every book is a conversation across time; you are the latest person invited.
